# Punctuations {#ch44}

[Part A · Grammar]{.chpart}

## 44.1 Why Punctuation Matters

**Punctuation** means the use of **marks** to separate sentences and their parts and to make the meaning clear. In speech we use pauses and intonation; in writing we use punctuation. Wrong punctuation can change the meaning completely:

::: ex
- *Let's eat, Grandma!* (inviting Grandma to eat) vs *Let's eat Grandma!* (a horrible meaning)
- *A woman, without her man, is nothing.* vs *A woman: without her, man is nothing.*
- *The teacher said, "The student is a fool."* vs *"The teacher," said the student, "is a fool."*
:::

"**Punctuate the following passage / sentences**" (with capital letters) is a standard question in SSC, HSC, admission tests and BCS Written. The **SAT** now tests punctuation directly in about a fifth of its Writing questions.

## 44.2 The Main Punctuation Marks

| Mark | Name | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| **.** | Full stop (BrE) / Period (AmE) | ends a statement; abbreviations |
| **,** | Comma | short pause; separates items, clauses, phrases |
| **;** | Semicolon | joins closely related independent clauses; separates complex list items |
| **:** | Colon | introduces a list, explanation, quotation or example |
| **?** | Question mark | ends a direct question |
| **!** | Exclamation mark | ends an exclamation |
| **'** | Apostrophe | possession; contraction |
| **" " / ' '** | Quotation marks (inverted commas) | direct speech; quotations; titles |
| **-** | Hyphen | joins words (compound words) |
| **–  —** | Dash (en dash / em dash) | sudden break; extra information; range |
| **( )** | Parentheses (round brackets) | extra, less important information |
| **[ ]** | Square brackets | editorial additions inside quotations |
| **…** | Ellipsis | omitted words; trailing off |
| **/** | Slash (oblique) | alternatives; fractions; dates |

## 44.3 Capital Letters

Use a **capital letter** for:

1. The **first word of a sentence**: ***T**he sun rises.*
2. The **first word of direct speech**: *He said, "**W**here are you going?"*
3. The pronoun ***I*** and the interjection ***O***: *I know, O Lord.*
4. **Proper nouns** — names of people, places, rivers, mountains, countries, institutions, days, months, festivals, languages, nationalities, religions, holy books: *Rahim, Dhaka, the Padma, Bangladesh, Dhaka University, Friday, March, Eid, Bangla, Bangladeshi, Islam, the Quran*. (Seasons are not capitalised: *summer, winter*.)
5. **Proper adjectives**: *English literature, a Chinese dish*.
6. **Titles** before names: *President Lincoln, Doctor Rahman, Professor Ali, Uncle Karim* (but *my uncle*).
7. **Main words in titles** of books, films, newspapers: *The Old Man and the Sea, The Daily Star*.
8. **God** and pronouns referring to God (traditional): *God is merciful; trust in **H**im.*
9. **Abbreviations** of names and organisations: *UN, BBC, NGO, BCS*.
10. The first word of **each line of traditional poetry** and of a **letter's greeting and closing**: *Dear Sir, Yours faithfully*.

## 44.4 The Full Stop (Period)

1. At the end of an **assertive** or **imperative** sentence: *He is a doctor. Shut the door.*
2. After **indirect questions**: *He asked me where I lived.* (✘ ?)
3. In **abbreviations** (more common in AmE; BrE often omits it when the abbreviation ends with the last letter of the word): *e.g., i.e., etc., a.m., p.m., Prof., Dr / Dr.* , *Mr / Mr.*
4. In **decimals** and **money**: *3.14; Tk 25.50*.
5. **Only one full stop** at the end, even if the sentence ends with an abbreviation: *He came at 9 a.m.* (✘ a.m..)

## 44.5 The Comma

The comma is the most frequently used — and misused — mark. Learn its main uses:

1. **To separate items in a list** (three or more): *I bought rice, fish, vegetables and fruit.* (The **serial / Oxford comma** before *and* is optional in BrE, common in AmE: *rice, fish, vegetables, and fruit*.)
2. **Between coordinate adjectives**: *a long, boring speech* (but not *a big red ball* — Chapter 09.5.4).
3. **Before a coordinating conjunction joining two independent clauses**: *He is poor, but he is honest. Work hard, or you will fail.* (No comma when the clauses are very short: *He came and he went.*)
4. **After an introductory adverb clause, phrase or word**: *When the bell rang, the students left. In the morning, we went out. However, he did not come. Yes, I agree.*
5. **To set off non-defining (non-essential) clauses and phrases**: *My brother, who lives in London, is a doctor. Dhaka, the capital of Bangladesh, is crowded.* (No commas for defining clauses: *The man who came here is my uncle.*)
6. **To set off parenthetical expressions and conjunctive adverbs**: *He is, in fact, very rich. The plan, however, failed.*
7. **Before and after a nominative of address (vocative)**: *Rahim, come here. Listen, my friends, to what I say.*
8. **To introduce or close direct speech**: *He said, "I am busy." / "I am busy," he said.*
9. **In dates, addresses and numbers**: *26 March, 1971* / *March 26, 1971* (AmE); *Mirpur, Dhaka, Bangladesh*; *1,000,000*.
10. **With absolute phrases and participial phrases**: *The sun having set, we went home. Tired of waiting, he left.*
11. **Before a tag question**: *You are coming, aren't you?*
12. **To show omission of a word**: *Rahim likes tea; Karim, coffee.*
13. **After the greeting and before the closing of an informal letter**: *Dear Mother, … Your loving son,*
14. **To avoid confusion**: *Inside, the room was dark.* (without the comma: *Inside the room was dark* — momentarily confusing)

::: rule
**Where NOT to put a comma:**

- ✘ between the **subject and its verb**: *The man who came yesterday**,** is my uncle.*
- ✘ between a **verb and its object**: *He said**,** that he was ill.* (✔ *He said that he was ill.*)
- ✘ before ***that*** introducing a noun clause: *I know**,** that he is honest.*
- ✘ to join two independent clauses **without a conjunction** (comma splice): *It was late**,** we went home.* (✔ *It was late, so we went home* / *It was late; we went home.*)
- ✘ before the first item or after the last item of a list.
- ✘ in a **defining** relative clause.
:::

## 44.6 The Semicolon

1. **To join two closely related independent clauses** without a conjunction: *The sun set; the stars appeared. Some people like tea; others prefer coffee.*
2. **Before conjunctive adverbs** (*however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, otherwise, consequently, besides, thus*) that join two clauses: *He worked hard; **however**, he failed.*
3. **To separate items in a list when the items already contain commas**: *The committee included Dr Rahman, a physician; Mr Karim, a lawyer; and Ms Rina, an engineer.*

::: tip
**Semicolon test:** A semicolon has a **complete sentence on both sides** (except in complex lists). If either side cannot stand alone, use a comma or a colon instead. ✘ *Although he tried; he failed.* ✔ *Although he tried, he failed.*
:::

## 44.7 The Colon

1. **To introduce a list** (after a complete clause): *You need three things: patience, hard work and luck.* (✘ *You need: patience …* — the part before a colon must be a complete sentence.)
2. **To introduce an explanation, example or result**: *He had one ambition: to become a doctor. The reason was clear: he had not studied.*
3. **To introduce a long or formal quotation**: *The Prime Minister said: "…"*
4. **In time, ratios, titles and references**: *10:30 a.m.; 3:1; Grammar: A Guide; Surah 2:255*.
5. **After the salutation in formal letters (AmE)**: *Dear Sir:* (BrE uses a comma).

## 44.8 Question Mark and Exclamation Mark

**Question mark (?):**

- After a **direct question**: *Where do you live?*
- After a **tag question**: *It's hot, isn't it?*
- **Not** after an **indirect question**: *He asked where I lived.* (✘ ?)
- After a statement used as a question in speech: *You're leaving?*

**Exclamation mark (!):**

- After **interjections** and **exclamatory sentences**: *Alas! What a pity! How beautiful the sky is!*
- After **strong commands** and **wishes**: *Get out! May you live long!*
- Do not overuse it, and use only one in formal writing (✘ !!!).

## 44.9 The Apostrophe

1. **Possession** (Chapter 02.6): *Rahim's book; the boys' hostel; the children's park; James's car / James' car; my sister-in-law's house; Rahim and Karim's shop (joint)*.
2. **Contraction** (omitted letters): *don't (do not), can't (cannot), won't (will not), it's (it is / it has), I'm, they're, who's, o'clock (of the clock), '71 (1971)*.
3. **Plurals of small letters and some symbols** for clarity: *Mind your p's and q's. Dot the i's.*

::: rule
**Never use an apostrophe:**

- in **possessive pronouns**: *its, yours, hers, ours, theirs, whose* (✘ it's, your's, her's, their's, who's as possessive);
- to make an **ordinary plural**: ✘ *mango's for sale*, ✘ *the 1990's* (preferred: *the 1990s*), ✘ *two MP's* (preferred: *two MPs*).
:::

| Confused pair | Meaning |
|---|---|
| its / it's | possessive / *it is, it has* |
| your / you're | possessive / *you are* |
| their / they're / there | possessive / *they are* / place |
| whose / who's | possessive / *who is, who has* |

## 44.10 Quotation Marks (Inverted Commas)

1. **Direct speech**: *He said, "I am hungry."*
2. **Quoted words** from a writer: *Nazrul wrote, "I am the rebel …"*
3. **Titles of short works** (poems, articles, chapters, songs, short stories): *"The Rebel", "Amar Sonar Bangla"* — titles of books, newspapers and films are written in *italics* (or underlined in handwriting).
4. **Words used in a special sense or ironically**: *His "help" made things worse.*
5. **Quotation within a quotation**: British style uses single marks outside, double inside (*'He said, "Wait."'*); American style uses double outside, single inside (*"He said, 'Wait.'"*). Be consistent.

### 44.10.1 Punctuating Direct Speech

::: ex
- He said, "I am busy." (comma before the quotation; full stop inside)
- "I am busy," he said. (comma inside before the closing mark)
- "I am busy," he said, "but I will help you." (the sentence continues — small letter for *but*)
- "I am busy," he said. "Come tomorrow." (a new sentence — capital *C*)
- She asked, "Where are you going?" (question mark inside)
- Did he say, "I am busy"? (the whole sentence is a question — mark outside)
- "Hurrah!" shouted the boys. "We have won!"
:::

## 44.11 Hyphen and Dash

**Hyphen (-)** — joins words or parts of words (no spaces):

- **Compound adjectives before a noun**: *a well-known writer, a ten-year-old boy, a five-year plan, up-to-date information* (but *The writer is well known*).
- **Compound nouns**: *mother-in-law, passer-by, editor-in-chief*.
- **Numbers and fractions**: *twenty-one, ninety-nine, two-thirds*.
- **Some prefixes**: *ex-president, self-respect, all-round, anti-American, re-enter, co-operate* (also *cooperate*).
- **Word division** at the end of a line (between syllables).

**Dash (– or —)** — longer than a hyphen:

- **En dash (–)** for **ranges**: *pages 10–20; 1971–1975; the Dhaka–Chattogram highway*.
- **Em dash (—)** for a **sudden break** or **emphasis**, or to set off **extra information** more strongly than commas: *He was — I am sure of it — a liar. Three things matter — honesty, courage and kindness.*

## 44.12 Brackets, Ellipsis and Slash

- **Parentheses ( )** enclose extra information that is less important: *Kazi Nazrul Islam (1899–1976) is our national poet.* A sentence must be grammatically complete without the bracketed words.
- **Square brackets [ ]** show the writer's own additions inside a quotation: *He said, "They [the students] are ready."*
- **Ellipsis (…)** shows omitted words in a quotation or an unfinished thought: *"I have a dream … that one day …"*; *Well, I thought …*
- **Slash (/)** shows alternatives, ratios or dates: *and/or; he/she; 60 km/h; 26/03/1971*.

## 44.13 Punctuating a Passage: Worked Examples

**Example 1 (unpunctuated):**

*one day a fox was very hungry he went out in search of food he saw a crow sitting on a branch of a tree with a piece of meat in its beak the fox said to the crow my dear crow how sweet your voice is will you sing a song for me*

**Punctuated:**

*One day a fox was very hungry. He went out in search of food. He saw a crow sitting on a branch of a tree with a piece of meat in its beak. The fox said to the crow, "My dear crow, how sweet your voice is! Will you sing a song for me?"*

**Example 2 (unpunctuated):**

*the teacher asked the boy what is your name the boy replied my name is rahim sir where do you live asked the teacher i live at mirpur in dhaka said the boy*

**Punctuated:**

*The teacher asked the boy, "What is your name?" The boy replied, "My name is Rahim, sir." "Where do you live?" asked the teacher. "I live at Mirpur in Dhaka," said the boy.*

**Example 3 (unpunctuated):**

*alas said the old man i have lost everything my house my land and my cattle what shall i do now*

**Punctuated:**

*"Alas!" said the old man. "I have lost everything — my house, my land and my cattle. What shall I do now?"*

::: tip
**Steps to punctuate a passage:** (1) Find where each **sentence** ends (subject + finite verb + complete thought) and put a full stop, question mark or exclamation mark. (2) Capitalise the **first word** of each sentence, the pronoun ***I*** and all **proper nouns**. (3) Mark **direct speech** with inverted commas and a comma/colon before it. (4) Add **commas** for lists, vocatives, introductory words and *yes/no*. (5) Add **apostrophes** for possessives and contractions.
:::

## 44.14 Common Punctuation Errors

::: errs
1. ✘ It was raining, we stayed at home. (comma splice) ✔ It was raining, **so** we stayed at home. / It was raining**;** we stayed at home.
2. ✘ He asked me where I lived? ✔ He asked me where I lived**.**
3. ✘ The dog wagged it's tail. ✔ The dog wagged **its** tail.
4. ✘ Mango's for sale. ✔ **Mangoes** for sale.
5. ✘ My brother who lives in Sylhet is a doctor. (if you have only one brother) ✔ My brother**,** who lives in Sylhet**,** is a doctor.
6. ✘ The man, who came here, is my uncle. (defining) ✔ The man who came here is my uncle.
7. ✘ He said that, "I am ill." ✔ He said, "I am ill." / He said that he was ill.
8. ✘ I know, that he is honest. ✔ I know that he is honest.
9. ✘ You need: a pen and a pencil. ✔ You need a pen and a pencil. / You need two things: a pen and a pencil.
10. ✘ Although he tried; he failed. ✔ Although he tried**,** he failed.
11. ✘ He is a well known writer. ✔ He is a **well-known** writer.
12. ✘ "I am tired." He said. ✔ "I am tired**,**" **h**e said.
13. ✘ i went to dhaka on friday. ✔ **I** went to **D**haka on **F**riday.
14. ✘ The childrens' park is closed. ✔ The **children's** park is closed.
15. ✘ He studied hard, however, he failed. ✔ He studied hard**;** however, he failed.
16. ✘ Rahim come here. ✔ Rahim**,** come here.
17. ✘ Its a lovely day. ✔ **It's** a lovely day.
18. ✘ The 1990's were good years. ✔ The **1990s** were good years.
19. ✘ He bought apples oranges and bananas. ✔ He bought apples**,** oranges and bananas.
20. ✘ What a beautiful scene it is. ✔ What a beautiful scene it is**!**
:::

## 44.15 Punctuation in the SAT, GRE and GMAT

::: gre
**The SAT (Standard English Conventions)** tests punctuation heavily:

- **Independent clauses:** join with **comma + FANBOYS**, a **semicolon**, a **colon** (if the second explains the first), or a **period** — never with a comma alone.
- **Non-essential elements** must be set off by a **matching pair** of commas, dashes or parentheses — don't mix a comma with a dash: ✘ *The book — which I bought yesterday, is interesting.*
- **Colons** must follow a **complete independent clause**.
- **No punctuation** between a subject and its verb, a verb and its object, or a preposition and its object.
- **Apostrophes** for singular/plural possessives (*the company's / the companies'*) and *its/it's*.

**GMAT** uses punctuation mainly to signal structure: a comma before *which* signals a non-essential modifier; a semicolon must join two independent clauses. **GRE** does not test punctuation, but reading sentences with dashes and colons correctly helps comprehension.
:::
