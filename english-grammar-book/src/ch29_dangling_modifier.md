## 29.1 What Is a Modifier?

A **modifier** is a word, phrase or clause that **describes** or **gives more information** about another word in the sentence. Adjectives, adverbs, participial phrases, infinitive phrases, prepositional phrases and relative clauses are all modifiers.

::: ex
- The **tired** boy slept. (*tired* modifies *boy*)
- **Running fast**, Rahim caught the bus. (*Running fast* modifies *Rahim*)
- The book **on the table** is mine. (*on the table* modifies *book*)
- The man **who lives next door** is a doctor.
:::

::: rule
**The Modifier Rule:** A modifier must be placed **as close as possible** to the word it modifies, and that word must actually **appear in the sentence**. An introductory modifying phrase (especially one with a participle) automatically refers to the **subject of the main clause** that follows the comma.
:::

When this rule is broken, two kinds of errors occur: **dangling modifiers** and **misplaced modifiers**. Both are among the most frequently tested errors in GMAT and SAT, and they appear in BCS and admission tests as "Correct the sentence" and error detection items.

## 29.2 Dangling Modifiers

A **dangling modifier** is a modifier whose intended **doer is missing** from the sentence. The phrase "dangles" because it has nothing logical to attach to, so it attaches to the wrong noun — usually the subject — often with **absurd** results.

::: ex
- ✘ **Walking along the road**, a snake bit him. (Was the snake walking?)
- ✔ **Walking along the road**, **he** was bitten by a snake.
- ✔ **While he was walking** along the road, a snake bit him.
:::

### 29.2.1 Dangling Participle (the Most Common Type)

::: ex
- ✘ **Having finished the work**, the television was switched on. (Did the television finish the work?)
- ✔ **Having finished the work**, **I** switched on the television.

- ✘ **Reaching the station**, the train had left. (Did the train reach the station?)
- ✔ **Reaching the station**, **we** found that the train had left.
- ✔ **When we reached** the station, the train had left.

- ✘ **Being a rainy day**, we stayed at home. (We were not a rainy day!)
- ✔ **It being a rainy day**, we stayed at home. (absolute construction)
- ✔ **As it was a rainy day**, we stayed at home.

- ✘ **Written in simple English**, students can understand the book easily. (Were the students written?)
- ✔ **Written in simple English**, **the book** is easy for students to understand.

- ✘ **Opening the door**, a strange sight met my eyes.
- ✔ **Opening the door**, **I** saw a strange sight.

- ✘ **Being very poor**, the school fee could not be paid.
- ✔ **Being very poor**, **he** could not pay the school fee.

- ✘ **Hurrying to school**, his books fell from his hand.
- ✔ **Hurrying to school**, **he** dropped his books.
:::

### 29.2.2 Dangling Gerund (Preposition + *-ing*)

::: ex
- ✘ **After finishing the exam**, the hall was left quietly. (Who finished?)
- ✔ **After finishing the exam**, **the students** left the hall quietly.

- ✘ **On entering the room**, the light was switched off.
- ✔ **On entering the room**, **she** switched off the light.

- ✘ **By working hard**, success can be achieved.
- ✔ **By working hard**, **you** can achieve success.

- ✘ **Before going to bed**, the doors should be locked.
- ✔ **Before going to bed**, **you** should lock the doors.
:::

### 29.2.3 Dangling Infinitive

::: ex
- ✘ **To pass the exam**, hard work is necessary. (Who wants to pass?)
- ✔ **To pass the exam**, **you** must work hard.

- ✘ **To get a good job**, a degree is required.
- ✔ **To get a good job**, **one** needs a degree.

- ✘ **To make tea**, the water must be boiled first.
- ✔ **To make tea**, **you** must boil the water first.
:::

### 29.2.4 Dangling Elliptical Clause

An **elliptical clause** is a shortened clause (*while/when/if/though* + participle or adjective) with its subject and verb omitted. The omitted subject must be the same as the subject of the main clause.

::: ex
- ✘ **While cooking dinner**, the phone rang. (Was the phone cooking?)
- ✔ **While I was cooking dinner**, the phone rang.
- ✔ **While cooking dinner**, **I** heard the phone ring.

- ✘ **When only six years old**, my father died.
- ✔ **When I was only six years old**, my father died.

- ✘ **Though very tired**, the work had to be finished.
- ✔ **Though very tired**, **we** had to finish the work.

- ✘ **If found guilty**, the court will punish him heavily.
- ✔ **If found guilty**, **he** will be punished heavily (by the court).
:::

### 29.2.5 Dangling Appositive or Adjective Phrase

::: ex
- ✘ **A brilliant student**, the scholarship was given to Rina. (The scholarship was not a brilliant student.)
- ✔ **A brilliant student**, **Rina** was given the scholarship.

- ✘ **Old and sick**, the doctor examined the patient.
- ✔ The doctor examined the **old and sick** patient.

- ✘ **The capital of Bangladesh**, many people migrate to it every year.
- ✔ **The capital of Bangladesh, Dhaka** attracts many migrants every year.
:::

### 29.2.6 The Possessive Trap

A possessive noun (*Rahim's*) **cannot** be the word that an introductory modifier refers to, because the real subject is the noun after it.

::: ex
- ✘ **Walking down the street**, **Rahim's** wallet was stolen. (The subject is *wallet* — the wallet was not walking.)
- ✔ **Walking down the street**, **Rahim** had his wallet stolen.
- ✔ **While Rahim was walking** down the street, his wallet was stolen.

- ✘ **A great poet**, **Nazrul's** songs are loved by all.
- ✔ **A great poet**, **Nazrul** wrote songs that are loved by all.
:::

## 29.3 How to Correct a Dangling Modifier

There are two methods:

::: rule
**Method 1 — Change the main clause:** Make the **doer** of the modifier the **subject** of the main clause, right after the comma.

✘ *Having eaten lunch, the dishes were washed.* → ✔ *Having eaten lunch, **we** washed the dishes.*

**Method 2 — Change the modifier:** Turn the phrase into a **full clause** with its own subject and verb.

✘ *Having eaten lunch, the dishes were washed.* → ✔ ***After we had eaten lunch**, the dishes were washed.*
:::

## 29.4 Acceptable Constructions That Look Like Dangling Modifiers

### 29.4.1 Absolute Phrases

An **absolute phrase** contains its **own subject**, so it does not dangle (Chapter 14.5.2):

::: ex
- ✔ **The sun having set**, we returned home.
- ✔ **The rain having stopped**, the match resumed.
- ✔ **Weather permitting**, we will go on a picnic.
:::

### 29.4.2 Fixed Participle Expressions

Some participial expressions are accepted idioms and do not need a subject:

*generally speaking, strictly speaking, frankly speaking, considering, judging by/from, taking everything into account, given, provided (that), supposing, assuming, regarding, concerning, including, excluding, according to, owing to*

::: ex
- ✔ **Generally speaking**, Bangladeshi people are hospitable.
- ✔ **Considering** the circumstances, the result was good.
- ✔ **Judging from** his accent, he is from Sylhet.
:::

## 29.5 Misplaced Modifiers

A **misplaced modifier** is present in the sentence with its correct "target", but it is placed **too far** from it or next to the **wrong word**, so the meaning becomes unclear or funny.

### 29.5.1 Misplaced Phrases

::: ex
- ✘ I saw a dead snake **riding my bicycle**. (Was the snake riding?)
- ✔ **Riding my bicycle**, I saw a dead snake.

- ✘ He bought a horse for his son **called Lightning**.
- ✔ He bought a horse **called Lightning** for his son.

- ✘ We saw the Shaheed Minar **driving through the city**. (Was the Minar driving?)
- ✔ **Driving through the city**, we saw the Shaheed Minar.

- ✘ For sale: a piano **by a lady with carved legs**.
- ✔ For sale: a piano **with carved legs**, by a lady.
:::

### 29.5.2 Misplaced Relative Clauses

::: ex
- ✘ I have a cow for my brother **which gives good milk**.
- ✔ For my brother, I have a cow **which gives good milk**.

- ✘ The boy was punished by the teacher **who broke the window**. (Did the teacher break it?)
- ✔ The boy **who broke the window** was punished by the teacher.
:::

### 29.5.3 Misplaced Limiting Adverbs (*only, just, nearly, almost, even, hardly, merely, scarcely*)

These words modify the word **immediately after** them (Chapter 15.5.4).

::: ex
- ✘ He **only** has five taka. ✔ He has **only** five taka.
- ✘ I **nearly** earned 10,000 taka last month. (= I almost earned, but earned nothing?) ✔ I earned **nearly** 10,000 taka last month.
- ✘ He **almost** spent all his salary on books. ✔ He spent **almost** all his salary on books.
- ✘ He **just** finished half of the work. ✔ He finished **just** half of the work.
:::

### 29.5.4 Squinting Modifiers

A **squinting modifier** is placed **between two words** so that it could modify either one:

::: ex
- ✘ Students who study **regularly** succeed. (study regularly or regularly succeed?)
- ✔ Students who **regularly** study succeed. / Students who study succeed **regularly**.

- ✘ He said **on Monday** he would visit us. (said on Monday or visit on Monday?)
- ✔ **On Monday** he said he would visit us. / He said he would visit us **on Monday**.
:::

### 29.5.5 Split Infinitive and Separated Subject–Verb

Long modifiers that split a subject from its verb or a verb from its object make sentences hard to follow:

::: ex
- ✘ The minister, **after hearing the complaints of the villagers about the broken road for more than two hours**, promised to repair it.
- ✔ **After hearing the villagers' complaints about the broken road for more than two hours**, the minister promised to repair it.
:::

## 29.6 *Like / Unlike* and Comparison Modifiers

***Like*** and ***unlike*** phrases at the start of a sentence are modifiers too; they must compare **similar things** and refer to the subject.

::: ex
- ✘ **Like** Dhaka, the traffic in Chattogram is heavy. (Dhaka vs traffic)
- ✔ **Like** Dhaka, **Chattogram** has heavy traffic.
- ✔ **Like that in** Dhaka, **the traffic** in Chattogram is heavy.

- ✘ **Unlike** most poets, Nazrul's poems were written for the common people.
- ✔ **Unlike** most poets, **Nazrul** wrote poems for the common people.
:::

## 29.7 More Examples for Practice in Recognition

::: errs
1. ✘ Being a hot day, I switched on the fan. ✔ **It being** a hot day, I switched on the fan.
2. ✘ Running to catch the bus, my bag fell. ✔ Running to catch the bus, **I dropped my bag**.
3. ✘ Having lost his money, the police were informed. ✔ Having lost his money, **he informed the police**.
4. ✘ On reaching home, the gate was found locked. ✔ On reaching home, **I found the gate locked**.
5. ✘ To win the match, practice is needed. ✔ To win the match, **the team** needs practice.
6. ✘ While sleeping, a mosquito bit me. ✔ While **I was** sleeping, a mosquito bit me.
7. ✘ After reading the novel, the film seemed boring. ✔ After reading the novel, **I found** the film boring.
8. ✘ Born in a poor family, education was difficult for him. ✔ Born in a poor family, **he** found education difficult.
9. ✘ Flying over the city, the buildings looked tiny. ✔ Flying over the city, **we** saw that the buildings looked tiny.
10. ✘ Tired of waiting, the meeting was cancelled. ✔ Tired of waiting, **the members** cancelled the meeting.
11. ✘ Covered with mud, his mother washed the child. ✔ His mother washed the child, **who was covered with mud**.
12. ✘ At the age of five, my grandfather taught me to read. ✔ **When I was five**, my grandfather taught me to read.
13. ✘ Driving carefully, the accident was avoided. ✔ Driving carefully, **she** avoided the accident.
14. ✘ Rushing out of the house, the door was left open. ✔ Rushing out of the house, **he** left the door open.
15. ✘ Hoping to get a good result, the exam was taken seriously. ✔ Hoping to get a good result, **Rina** took the exam seriously.
:::

## 29.8 Modifiers in the SAT, GRE and GMAT

::: gre
**Modifier errors are among the five most tested GMAT Sentence Correction topics.** Apply these checks to every answer choice:

1. **Introductory modifier → subject.** Whatever comes right after the comma must be the doer. ✘ *Developed in the 1990s, many companies now use this software.* ✔ *Developed in the 1990s, **this software** is now used by many companies.*
2. **"Touch rule" for noun modifiers:** a modifier beginning with *which, who, that,* or a noun phrase must **touch** the noun it describes. ✘ *She bought a house from a builder, which has five bedrooms.* (Does the builder have five bedrooms?) ✔ *From a builder, she bought a house **that** has five bedrooms.*
3. ***-ing* modifiers after a comma (GMAT):** a comma + *-ing* phrase modifies the **whole preceding clause** (its subject and action), so it is acceptable when it describes a result: ✔ *The river flooded the village, **destroying** many homes.*
4. ***Which* after a comma** must refer to the **nearest noun**, not the whole clause (Chapter 08.12).
5. **Possessives cannot be antecedents** of introductory modifiers (29.2.6).

In **SAT Writing**, dangling modifiers usually appear as an introductory phrase followed by four versions of the main clause; the correct one begins with the **logical doer**.
:::
