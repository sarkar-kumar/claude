## 23.1 What Is Subject-Verb Agreement?

Every complete sentence has a **subject** (the person or thing we are talking about) and a **verb** (what the subject does or is). *Subject-verb agreement* means that **the verb must match its subject in number and person.**

- **Number** means *singular* (one) or *plural* (more than one).
- **Person** means *first person* (I, we), *second person* (you) or *third person* (he, she, it, they, Rahim, the book, the students).

So, a singular subject needs a singular verb, and a plural subject needs a plural verb. This sounds very easy, and in short sentences it is. The difficulty appears when the subject is hidden, long, compound, collective, or separated from its verb by many words. Almost every competitive exam in Bangladesh — BCS Preliminary, Bank (Officer, Senior Officer, AD of Bangladesh Bank), university admission tests (DU, JU, RU, CU) and international exams like the SAT, GRE and GMAT — tests this topic every year, mainly through *Error Detection*, *Sentence Correction*, *Right Form of Verbs* and *Fill in the Blanks*.

::: ex
- The boy **plays** football. (singular subject → singular verb)
- The boys **play** football. (plural subject → plural verb)
- She **is** a doctor. / They **are** doctors.
- I **am** ready. / We **are** ready.
:::

### 23.1.1 Why the "s" Confuses Learners

In English, an **-s/-es** at the end of a **noun** usually makes it *plural* (book → books). But an **-s/-es** at the end of a **present-tense verb** makes it *singular* (they play → he play**s**). So in a correct present simple sentence, the "s" usually appears **only once** — either on the noun or on the verb, not on both.

::: ex
- The dog bark**s**. (s on the verb → singular)
- The dog**s** bark. (s on the noun → plural)
- ✘ The dogs barks. ✔ The dogs bark.
:::

::: tip
**Exam Tip — The "one s" trick:** In a simple present sentence with a normal noun subject, if both the subject and the verb end in -s, check again — one of them is probably wrong. (This trick does not work for nouns like *news, physics, police, people, children*, so learn those rules below.)
:::

### 23.1.2 Where Agreement Matters

Agreement only shows in a few places in English, so you must know exactly where to look:

1. **Present simple tense** — *he works / they work*.
2. **The verb *be*** — *am / is / are* (present) and *was / were* (past).
3. **The verb *have*** — *has / have*.
4. **The verb *do*** — *does / do*.
5. **Present perfect and present continuous** — *has gone / have gone; is going / are going*.
6. **Past continuous** — *was going / were going*.

Modal auxiliaries (*can, could, may, might, shall, should, will, would, must, ought to*) **never change** with the subject: *He can swim. They can swim.* The **past simple** of ordinary verbs also does not change: *He went. They went.*

| Subject | be (present) | be (past) | have | do | Main verb (present) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| I | am | was | have | do | write |
| We / You / They | are | were | have | do | write |
| He / She / It | is | was | has | does | writes |
| Singular noun (Rahim, the book) | is | was | has | does | writes |
| Plural noun (the boys) | are | were | have | do | write |

::: note
**Remember:** *You* always takes a plural verb even when it refers to one person: *You **are** my friend* (✘ You is). *I* takes *am* in the present but *was* in the past: *I **was** there* (✘ I were there — except in the subjunctive: *If I **were** you*).
:::

## 23.2 Step One: Find the True Subject

Most agreement errors happen because the learner matches the verb with the wrong word. So before applying any rule, **find the real (head) subject**. Ask: *"Who or what is doing the action / being described?"* Then strip away everything that only describes it.

### 23.2.1 Words That Are Never the Subject

The subject of a sentence is **never** inside:

- a **prepositional phrase** (of the students, in the box, with his friends, for the poor);
- an **appositive** set off by commas (*Mr. Karim, our teachers' leader, …*);
- a **relative clause** (*who live next door, which were damaged*);
- a **participial phrase** (*sitting under the trees, written by Tagore*).

Cross these out mentally and look at what is left.

::: ex
- The quality of the mangoes **is** (✘ are) excellent. → *quality* is the subject; *of the mangoes* is a prepositional phrase.
- The box of chocolates **was** (✘ were) stolen.
- The boys in the last bench **are** talking.
- The cost of all these books **has** (✘ have) risen.
- One of my friends **lives** (✘ live) in Sylhet.
- The leader of the workers, together with his followers, **was** (✘ were) arrested.
- A list of the successful candidates **has** (✘ have) been published.
- The use of mobile phones in classrooms **is** (✘ are) prohibited.
- The houses that stand beside the river **are** (✘ is) old.
:::

::: gre
**SAT / GRE / GMAT Corner — The "Long-Distance" Trap:** Standardised tests love to put 10–20 words between the subject and the verb so that a plural noun sits right before a singular verb (or vice versa). Example (GMAT style): *"The rise in the prices of essential commodities such as rice, lentils and edible oils **have** hurt low-income families."* The subject is *rise* (singular), so the correct verb is **has hurt**. Always ask: *What exactly has hurt the families? — The rise.*
:::

### 23.2.2 Subject After the Verb (Inverted Order)

In some sentences the verb comes **before** the subject. You must look **after** the verb to find the subject.

**(a) Sentences beginning with *There* or *Here*.** *There* and *here* are never subjects; the real subject comes after the verb.

::: ex
- There **is** a book on the table.
- There **are** many books on the table.
- There **was** a lot of noise.
- There **were** a lot of people.
- Here **is** your ticket. / Here **are** your tickets.
- ✘ There's three reasons for this. ✔ There **are** three reasons for this.
:::

**(b) Questions.** The subject usually follows the auxiliary.

::: ex
- Where **is** the key? / Where **are** the keys?
- **Does** your brother live here? / **Do** your brothers live here?
- **Has** any of the guests arrived? / **Have** all the guests arrived?
- What **are** the causes of the war? (subject = *the causes*)
:::

**(c) Inversion after adverbs or adverbial phrases of place** (literary style, common in SAT/GRE reading).

::: ex
- On the hill **stands** an old temple. (subject = *an old temple*)
- On the hill **stand** two old temples.
- Among the guests **was** the Prime Minister.
- In the middle of the room **were** a table and two chairs.
- Down **came** the rain. / Away **flew** the birds.
:::

**(d) Inversion after negative adverbs** (*never, seldom, rarely, hardly, scarcely, no sooner, not only, nor, little*). The auxiliary still agrees with the subject that follows it.

::: ex
- Never **has** he told a lie. / Never **have** they told a lie.
- Not only **does** she sing, but she also dances.
- Seldom **do** we see such honesty nowadays.
:::

### 23.2.3 Long Subjects (Phrases and Clauses as Subjects)

When a **gerund phrase**, an **infinitive phrase** or a **noun clause** is the subject, it is treated as **one idea**, so the verb is **singular**.

::: ex
- Swimming in rivers **is** good exercise.
- Reading books **makes** a man perfect.
- Taking long walks in the evening **helps** her relax.
- To err **is** human; to forgive **is** divine.
- To read many books **is** useful.
- What he says **is** true.
- That he is honest **is** known to all.
- Whether they will come **is** uncertain.
- Who stole the money **remains** a mystery.
:::

::: note
**Two gerunds joined by *and*** usually take a plural verb because they are two different activities: *Reading and writing **are** essential skills.* But if they form one combined activity, use singular: *Eating and drinking too much **is** harmful.* (Both are accepted in modern usage; in exams the plural is safer for two clearly separate activities.)
:::

**Special case — *What*-clauses with plural complements.** When a *what*-clause is followed by a plural noun complement, many grammarians accept a plural verb: *What we need **are** more teachers.* The traditional (safe) answer for BCS is singular: *What we need **is** more teachers.* Both are seen in good writing; SAT and GMAT avoid testing this.

## 23.3 Rules for Compound Subjects

A **compound subject** has two or more nouns/pronouns joined by *and, or, nor, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also*, etc.

### Rule 1: Subjects joined by *and* → plural verb

::: rule
**Rule 1.** Two or more subjects joined by **and** generally take a **plural verb**.
:::

::: ex
- Rahim and Karim **are** friends.
- My father and my uncle **were** present.
- He and I **are** classmates.
- Honesty and hard work **are** the keys to success.
- Bangladesh and Nepal **are** neighbouring countries.
- The teacher and the students **have** gone to the museum.
:::

There are several important **exceptions** to Rule 1. These are among the most frequently tested points in BCS and Bank exams.

### Rule 2: Two nouns, one person → singular

::: rule
**Rule 2.** If two nouns joined by *and* refer to the **same person or thing**, the verb is **singular**. The sign: **the article (a/an/the) or possessive is used only once**, before the first noun.
:::

::: ex
- The poet and philosopher **is** dead. (one man who is both a poet and a philosopher)
- The poet and the philosopher **are** dead. (two different men)
- The Headmaster and Secretary **is** present. (one person)
- The Headmaster and the Secretary **are** present. (two persons)
- My friend and benefactor **has** come.
- A black and white cow **is** grazing. (one cow with two colours)
- A black and a white cow **are** grazing. (two cows)
- The writer and director of the film **was** awarded.
:::

### Rule 3: One idea or one dish → singular

::: rule
**Rule 3.** When two nouns joined by *and* express **one idea**, **one unit**, or **one combined thing** (often a dish, a pair or a proverb), the verb is **singular**.
:::

::: ex
- Bread and butter **is** his usual breakfast.
- Rice and curry **is** my favourite food.
- Bread and milk **is** good for children.
- Slow and steady **wins** the race.
- Fish and chips **is** popular in England.
- Law and order **has** deteriorated.
- The horse and carriage **is** at the door. (one vehicle)
- The long and the short of the matter **is** this.
- Gin and tonic **is** a common drink.
- Ups and downs **are** part of life. (*ups and downs* is plural in form and meaning)
:::

::: note
**Traditional proverb exception:** *Time and tide **wait** for none.* Here *time* and *tide* are treated as two separate things, so the verb is plural. Learn it as it is, since it appears often in BCS papers.
:::

**Bread and butter** in its idiomatic sense *(means of livelihood)* is also singular: *Teaching **is** my bread and butter.*

### Rule 4: *Each / Every / No* before *and*-subjects → singular

::: rule
**Rule 4.** If **each, every, many a** or **no** comes before singular subjects joined by *and*, the verb is **singular**.
:::

::: ex
- Every man and woman **is** equal before the law.
- Each boy and each girl **has** a book.
- Every teacher and every student **was** present.
- No man and no woman **was** spared.
- Every house and every shop **was** searched.
- Each day and each night **brings** its own problems.
:::

### Rule 5: *As well as, with, along with* etc. → verb follows the FIRST subject

::: rule
**Rule 5.** When two subjects are joined by **as well as, with, along with, together with, accompanied by, in addition to, besides, including, like, unlike, rather than, more than, no less than, and not, but not, except, but, plus**, the verb agrees with the **first subject**. The second part is only extra information and is usually set off by commas.
:::

::: ex
- The teacher, as well as the students, **was** present. (✘ were)
- The students, as well as the teacher, **were** present.
- The captain, along with his players, **has** arrived.
- The minister, with his secretaries, **is** coming.
- Rina, together with her sisters, **is** going to Cox's Bazar.
- He, and not you, **is** to blame.
- You, and not he, **are** to blame.
- The ship, with its crew, **was** lost.
- Mr. Hasan, accompanied by his wife, **was** at the party.
- The house, including its furniture, **was** sold.
- Karim, like his brothers, **is** a doctor.
- Gold, no less than silver, **has** fallen in price.
- He, rather than his friends, **deserves** punishment.
- The President, in addition to his ministers, **was** present.
- Nobody except the guards **was** allowed inside.
:::

::: gre
**SAT / GMAT Corner — The "False Compound" trap:** Test-makers use *along with, as well as, in addition to, together with* to make a singular subject *look* plural. E.g. *"The CEO, in addition to several board members, **were** accused of fraud."* ✘ → **was** accused. Only **and** can create a true compound (plural) subject. GMAT often uses this with long intervening phrases.
:::

### Rule 6: *Or, nor, either…or, neither…nor* → nearer subject (Proximity Rule)

::: rule
**Rule 6.** When two subjects are joined by **or, nor, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also, not…but**, the verb agrees with the subject **nearer (closer) to it**. This is called the **Law of Proximity**.
:::

::: ex
- Either Rahim or Karim **has** done it. (both singular → singular)
- Either the boys or their father **has** done it. (nearer = father)
- Either the father or his sons **have** done it. (nearer = sons)
- Neither he nor I **am** guilty. (nearer = I)
- Neither you nor he **is** responsible.
- Neither he nor you **are** responsible.
- Not only the students but also the teacher **was** surprised.
- Not only the teacher but also the students **were** surprised.
- Not the principal but the teachers **are** responsible.
- Either you or your brother **has** to go.
- **Has** either Rahim or Karim seen this? (In a question the auxiliary stands nearest to the *first* subject, so if one subject is plural, rewrite: *Have his friends or Rahim seen this?*)
- Ali or his brothers **are** coming.
- My brothers or Ali **is** coming.
:::

::: tip
**Exam Tip — Placing the plural subject last:** When one subject is singular and the other plural, good style puts the **plural subject nearer the verb** and uses a **plural verb**: *Neither the captain nor the players **were** happy.* This sounds more natural than *Neither the players nor the captain was happy*, though both are grammatically correct.
:::

**Person problem.** When the subjects joined by *or/nor* are of different persons (*you* and *I*, *he* and *I*), the verb still agrees with the nearer subject. But because such sentences sound awkward, it is better to repeat the verb:

::: ex
- Either you or I **am** wrong. → Better: Either you **are** wrong, or I **am**.
- Neither he nor you **are** to blame. → Better: He **is** not to blame, nor **are** you.
:::

**Order of persons with *and*.** When pronouns of different persons are joined by *and*, the polite order in good English is **231** for ordinary/positive sentences: second person first, then third, then first (*You, he and I*). When admitting a fault or in negative sense, the order is **123** (*I, you and he are to blame*). The verb is always plural.

::: ex
- You, he and I **are** good friends.
- I, you and he **are** guilty.
:::

### Rule 7: *Both…and* → plural

*Both…and* always joins two subjects firmly, so the verb is always **plural**.

::: ex
- Both Rahim and Karim **are** absent.
- Both the cat and the dog **were** hungry.
:::

## 23.4 Indefinite Pronouns and Distributive Words

**Indefinite pronouns** refer to people or things in a general way. Some are always singular, some always plural, and some can be either.

### Rule 8: Always singular indefinite pronouns

::: rule
**Rule 8.** The following words are **always singular** and take a **singular verb**: **each, every, either, neither, one, everyone, everybody, everything, someone, somebody, something, anyone, anybody, anything, no one, nobody, nothing, another, much, many a, more than one, little (amount).**
:::

::: ex
- Everybody **knows** the answer.
- Everyone **was** happy.
- Somebody **has** taken my pen.
- Nobody **likes** to be insulted.
- Nothing **is** impossible.
- Anyone **is** allowed to apply.
- Something **is** wrong with the engine.
- Much **has** been said about the matter.
- Each of the boys **was** given a prize.
- Every one of the students **has** passed.
- Either of the two roads **leads** to the station.
- Neither of the answers **is** correct.
- One of the girls **is** absent.
- One of the most important problems **is** unemployment.
:::

::: tip
**Exam Tip — "One of the + plural noun + singular verb":** *One of the boys **is*** (✘ one of the boy is / ✘ one of the boys are). Note two things: (i) the noun after *one of the* must be **plural**; (ii) the verb must be **singular** because the subject is *one*. Both errors are tested regularly.
:::

**Either / Neither** are used for **two** persons or things only. For more than two, use *any one / none*.

::: ex
- Either of the two brothers **is** suitable.
- Any one of the five candidates **is** suitable. (✘ Either of the five)
- Neither of the two sisters **sings** well.
- None of the ten students **has/have** passed. (✘ Neither of the ten)
:::

::: note
In informal English, *neither of* + plural noun with a plural verb is common (*Neither of them **are** coming*). In BCS, Bank and admission exams, always use the **singular** verb: *Neither of them **is** coming.*
:::

### Rule 9: *Each* after a plural subject

When *each* comes **after** a plural subject (as an appositive), the verb is **plural**, because the subject is still plural. When *each* comes **before** (as the subject), the verb is singular.

::: ex
- They each **have** a car. (subject = *they*)
- Each of them **has** a car. (subject = *each*)
- The students each **have** their own computers.
- We each **have** a different opinion.
- Each student **has** a different opinion.
:::

### Rule 10: Always plural indefinite pronouns

::: rule
**Rule 10.** **Both, few, a few, many, several, others** are always **plural**.
:::

::: ex
- Both **are** guilty.
- Few **were** present at the meeting.
- Many **have** come, but a few **have** gone back.
- Several of the books **are** missing.
- Others **think** differently.
:::

### Rule 11: SANAM pronouns — singular or plural

::: rule
**Rule 11 (The SANAM Rule).** **S**ome, **A**ny, **N**one, **A**ll, **M**ost (and also *half, part, rest, enough, more, lot, plenty, majority, percent, fractions*) can be singular or plural. Look at the noun **after *of*** (or the noun they refer to): if it is **uncountable or singular → singular verb**; if it is **countable plural → plural verb**.
:::

::: ex
- Some of the milk **is** sour. / Some of the mangoes **are** sour.
- All of the money **was** stolen. / All of the books **were** stolen.
- Most of the work **is** done. / Most of the workers **are** absent.
- Any of the food **is** fine. / Are any of the boys here?
- Half of the cake **has** been eaten. / Half of the cakes **have** been eaten.
- Part of the building **was** damaged. / Part of the walls **were** damaged.
- The rest of the story **is** boring. / The rest of the students **are** in the hall.
- A lot of water **is** wasted. / A lot of people **are** waiting.
- Plenty of time **is** left. / Plenty of seats **are** available.
- All that glitters **is** not gold. (*all* = everything → singular)
- All **are** not thieves that dogs bark at. (*all* = all persons → plural)
- All is well. / All are well.
:::

### Rule 12: *None*

*None* means *not one* or *not any*. Traditional grammar treated it as singular, but modern usage (and most Bangladeshi exam keys) follows the SANAM rule:

::: ex
- None of the information **was** useful. (uncountable → singular)
- None of the money **is** mine.
- None of the students **were/was** present. (countable plural → both accepted; plural is more common, singular is more formal)
- None **were** present. (= no persons)
- None but the brave **deserves** the fair. (famous line of Dryden — learn as it is)
- None of these two answers... ✘ → Neither of these two answers **is** correct.
:::

::: tip
**Exam Tip on *none*:** If an option says *"None of the boys were present"* or *"None of the boys was present"*, both can be correct. Look for another clear error in the sentence. If you must choose, the singular is the "classic" formal answer, while the plural matches modern usage (SAT/GRE accept both). With an uncountable noun, only the singular is correct.
:::

### Rule 13: *Many a* → singular verb and singular noun

::: rule
**Rule 13.** **Many a / many an** is followed by a **singular noun** and a **singular verb** (and a singular pronoun), though its meaning is "many".
:::

::: ex
- Many a man **has** lost his life in this war.
- Many a student **was** absent yesterday.
- Many a flower **is** born to blush unseen. (Thomas Gray)
- Many an opportunity **has** been lost.
- ✘ Many a boys were present. ✔ Many a boy **was** present.
:::

Compare: **A great many / a good many** take a **plural noun** and **plural verb**: *A great many students **were** present.* And **many** alone is plural: *Many students **were** present.*

### Rule 14: *More than one* → singular; *More + plural noun + than one* → plural

::: ex
- More than one boy **was** absent. (✘ More than one boys were)
- More than one student **has** failed.
- More boys than one **were** absent. (the subject is *boys*)
- More than two boys **were** absent. (*two* is plural)
:::

### Rule 15: *A number of* vs *The number of*

::: rule
**Rule 15.** **A number of** (= many) + plural noun → **plural verb**. **The number of** (= the total figure) + plural noun → **singular verb**.
:::

::: ex
- A number of students **are** absent today.
- The number of students **is** increasing day by day.
- A number of accidents **have** occurred on this road.
- The number of road accidents **has** risen sharply.
- A large number of people **were** killed.
- The number of unemployed youths **is** alarming.
:::

The same logic applies to similar expressions:

::: ex
- A variety of fruits **are** sold here. / The variety of fruits **is** amazing.
- A total of fifty people **were** injured. / The total **is** fifty.
- A majority of voters **support** him. / The majority **is** in favour.
- A couple of days **have** passed.
- A pair of shoes **is** under the bed. (a pair = one unit; see Rule 22)
:::

## 23.5 Relative Pronouns (who, which, that)

A relative pronoun has no number of its own. The verb in a relative clause agrees with the **antecedent** (the noun or pronoun the relative pronoun refers to).

### Rule 16: Verb agrees with the antecedent of *who/which/that*

::: rule
**Rule 16.** In a relative clause, the verb agrees in number **and person** with the antecedent of the relative pronoun.
:::

::: ex
- I, who **am** your friend, will help you. (✘ who is)
- You, who **are** my friend, should help me.
- He, who **is** my friend, will help me.
- It is I who **am** to blame. (✘ It is I who is to blame)
- It is you who **are** to blame.
- It is he who **is** responsible.
- It is they who **have** done this.
- The girl who **sings** well is my sister.
- The girls who **sing** well are my sisters.
- This is the book that **was** missing.
- These are the books that **were** missing.
:::

### Rule 17: *One of those who* → plural; *The only one of those who* → singular

This is a favourite trap of BCS, Bank and GMAT.

::: rule
**Rule 17.** In "**one of the + plural noun + who/that/which**", the antecedent of *who* is the **plural noun**, so the verb in the relative clause is **plural**. But in "**the only one of the + plural noun + who**", the antecedent is **one**, so the verb is **singular**.
:::

::: ex
- He is one of the best players who **have** ever played for Bangladesh. (many players have played; he is one of them)
- She is one of those women who **work** hard.
- This is one of the books that **are** worth reading.
- He is one of the boys who **are** always late.
- He is the only one of the boys who **is** always late. (only he is late)
- She is the only one of the applicants who **has** a driving licence.
- Rahim is the only one of my friends who **lives** in Dhaka.
:::

::: tip
**Quick test:** Turn the sentence around: *"Of the boys who **are** always late, he is one."* — the plural is clearly correct. But for *the only one*: *"Of the boys, he is the only one who **is** always late."* — clearly singular.
:::

## 23.6 Collective Nouns

A **collective noun** names a group of persons or things: *family, team, class, committee, jury, crowd, audience, army, crew, staff, government, council, public, board, company, flock, herd, fleet, mob, parliament, party, majority, enemy, union, orchestra, band, gang, association.*

### Rule 18: Collective noun as a unit → singular; as individuals → plural

::: rule
**Rule 18.** If the group acts **as one unit**, use a **singular verb** (and singular pronoun *it/its*). If the members act **separately or disagree**, use a **plural verb** (and plural pronoun *they/their*).
:::

::: ex
- The jury **has** given its verdict. (one decision)
- The jury **were** divided in their opinions. (members disagree)
- The committee **has** submitted its report.
- The committee **are** arguing among themselves.
- The team **is** playing well this season.
- The team **are** taking their seats on the bus.
- My family **is** a large one.
- My family **are** all early risers.
- The class **was** dismissed at noon.
- The class **were** busy with their own work.
- The army **is** marching forward.
- The audience **was** enormous. / The audience **were** clapping their hands.
- The crowd **has** dispersed. / The crowd **were** shouting slogans.
- The staff **is** small. / The staff **are** on leave.
- The public **is** requested not to litter. (unit) / The public **are** divided on this issue.
:::

::: note
**Consistency matters:** Never mix singular and plural for the same collective noun in one sentence. ✘ *The committee **has** made **their** decision.* ✔ *The committee **has** made **its** decision.* This "verb–pronoun consistency" is heavily tested on the SAT and GMAT.
:::

::: gre
**British vs American English:** In American English (the standard of the SAT, GRE and GMAT), collective nouns are **almost always singular**: *The government **is** …, The team **is** …* In British English, plural is common when we think of members: *The government **are** …, The team **are** …* Bangladeshi exams mostly follow the traditional British "unit vs individuals" rule. In international tests, choose the **singular** unless the sentence clearly shows separate action (like *disagree among themselves*).
:::

### Rule 19: Nouns that look singular but are always plural

::: rule
**Rule 19.** Some collective nouns are **always plural**, even though they have no -s: **people (= persons), police, cattle, poultry, gentry, clergy, peasantry, vermin, swine, folk, militia, livestock (often)**. They never take *a/an* and never add -s in this sense.
:::

::: ex
- The people **are** happy. (✘ The people is happy)
- Many people **were** killed.
- The police **have** arrested the thief. (✘ The police has)
- The police **are** investigating the case.
- The cattle **are** grazing in the field. (✘ cattles)
- Poultry **are** kept on this farm.
- The gentry **were** invited.
- The clergy **are** against the move.
- Vermin **are** destroying the crops.
:::

::: note
***People*** meaning "a nation / an ethnic group" is countable and has a plural ***peoples***: *The Bengalis **are** a brave people. The peoples of Asia **are** diverse.* ***A policeman / a police officer*** is the singular of *police*: ✘ *A police came.* ✔ *A policeman came.*
:::

## 23.7 Nouns with Tricky Forms

### Rule 20: Plural in form, singular in meaning

::: rule
**Rule 20.** Some nouns end in **-s** but are **singular** in meaning and take a **singular verb**.
:::

**(a) Names of subjects / sciences:** *mathematics, physics, economics, politics, ethics, statistics, phonetics, linguistics, civics, electronics, genetics, athletics, gymnastics.*

**(b) Names of diseases:** *measles, mumps, rickets, diabetes, shingles, rabies, herpes.*

**(c) Names of games:** *billiards, darts, draughts, dominoes, cards (as a game).*

**(d) Other words:** *news, innings, gallows, summons, means (method), wages (in some idioms), amends.*

::: ex
- Mathematics **is** my favourite subject.
- Economics **is** a social science.
- Physics **deals** with matter and energy.
- Measles **is** an infectious disease.
- Diabetes **is** common in Bangladesh.
- Billiards **is** an indoor game.
- The news **is** true. (✘ The news are true) — *a piece of news*, not *a news*.
- No news **is** good news.
- Bangladesh's first innings **was** good. / An innings **lasts** for hours.
- The gallows **was** set up.
- A summons **has** been issued. (plural: *summonses*)
:::

::: note
**Subject words used in other senses take a plural verb:** *His politics **are** confusing.* (his political views) *The statistics **show** a rise in crime.* (a set of figures) *The economics of the project **are** not clear.* (financial aspects) *His mathematics **are** weak.* (his calculations — some books accept). But as subjects of study: *Politics **is** a dirty game. Statistics **is** an important subject.*
:::

### Rule 21: Plural-only nouns (summation plurals)

::: rule
**Rule 21.** Some nouns are used **only in the plural** and always take a **plural verb**: **scissors, trousers, pants, pyjamas, jeans, shorts, spectacles, glasses (eyeglasses), binoculars, tongs, pliers, bellows, shears, thanks, riches, alms, proceeds, premises, surroundings, outskirts, environs, arrears, savings, belongings, clothes, goods, contents, assets, annals, archives, credentials, earnings, remains, dregs, eaves, lodgings, odds, tidings, valuables, congratulations.**
:::

::: ex
- My trousers **are** new. (✘ my trouser is)
- Your spectacles **are** on the table.
- The scissors **are** sharp.
- Riches **do** not bring happiness.
- Alms **are** given to the poor.
- The proceeds of the show **were** donated.
- The premises **are** guarded.
- The surroundings of the lake **are** beautiful.
- His clothes **are** dirty.
- The goods **have** been dispatched.
- Thanks **are** due to the organisers.
- The contents of the letter **were** secret.
:::

### Rule 22: *A pair of* + plural noun → singular

::: ex
- A pair of scissors **is** on the table.
- This pair of trousers **is** expensive.
- Two pairs of shoes **are** needed.
- My pair of spectacles **was** broken.
:::

### Rule 23: Same form in singular and plural

Some nouns have **the same form** in both numbers: *sheep, deer, swine, fish (usually), aircraft, spacecraft, series, species, means, headquarters, crossroads, works (factory), offspring, salmon, trout, cod, corps.* The determiner or context tells us the number.

::: ex
- A sheep **is** grazing. / Ten sheep **are** grazing. (✘ sheeps)
- A deer **was** killed. / Many deer **were** killed.
- A series of lectures **was** arranged. / Two series of stamps **have** been issued.
- This species of bird **is** rare. / These species **are** endangered.
- Every means **has** been tried. / All means **have** been tried.
- The headquarters of the company **is/are** in Dhaka. (both accepted)
- An aircraft **was** missing. / Three aircraft **were** missing.
:::

### Rule 24: Uncountable (mass) nouns → always singular

::: rule
**Rule 24.** **Uncountable nouns** take a **singular verb** and never take *a/an* or a plural ending: **information, advice, furniture, luggage, baggage, equipment, machinery, scenery, poetry, jewellery, knowledge, homework, work (= labour), stationery, traffic, weather, bread, money, evidence, mischief, damage (harm), news, hair (on the head), dust, rubbish, grass, offspring (sometimes), crockery, cutlery, business (affairs), progress, research, behaviour, wealth, health, music, fun, patience, permission.**
:::

::: ex
- The information **is** correct. (✘ The informations are)
- His advice **was** helpful. (✘ advices)
- The furniture **is** new. (✘ furnitures) → *a piece of furniture / pieces of furniture*
- My luggage **has** been lost.
- The scenery of Rangamati **is** charming. (✘ sceneries)
- The machinery **was** imported from Japan.
- Her hair **is** long and black. (✘ hairs are) — but *There are two grey hairs on his head* (individual strands)
- Poetry **is** the music of the soul.
- The evidence **is** strong.
- Much progress **has** been made.
- The traffic **is** heavy today.
:::

The British-sense **plural of measurement** follows the countable unit: *Two pieces of advice **were** given. Three items of furniture **were** sold.*

### Rule 25: Foreign (Latin / Greek) plurals → plural verb

::: rule
**Rule 25.** Many nouns borrowed from Latin and Greek keep their foreign plurals, and those plurals take a **plural verb** in formal English.
:::

| Singular | Plural | Singular | Plural |
|---|---|---|---|
| datum | data | syllabus | syllabi / syllabuses |
| criterion | criteria | crisis | crises |
| phenomenon | phenomena | analysis | analyses |
| medium | media | thesis | theses |
| curriculum | curricula | hypothesis | hypotheses |
| memorandum | memoranda | basis | bases |
| bacterium | bacteria | oasis | oases |
| stratum | strata | axis | axes |
| alumnus | alumni | formula | formulae / formulas |
| stimulus | stimuli | index | indices / indexes |
| radius | radii | appendix | appendices |
| nucleus | nuclei | bureau | bureaux / bureaus |
| fungus | fungi |  |  |

::: ex
- These data **are** reliable. (formal) / This data **is** reliable. (accepted in modern and computing English)
- The criteria for selection **are** strict. (✘ The criteria is)
- This criterion **is** important.
- These phenomena **are** rare. / This phenomenon **is** rare.
- The bacteria **multiply** quickly.
- The media **have** a great influence. (formal) / The media **has** … (modern, accepted)
- The crises **were** handled well.
- The alumni of DU **are** meeting today.
:::

::: gre
**GRE / GMAT note:** *Criteria* and *phenomena* are **always plural** — a common trap. *Data* and *media* are accepted with both, but the GMAT tends to treat *data* as plural in formal contexts. If an answer choice says *"the criteria is"*, it is wrong.
:::

## 23.8 Quantities, Amounts and Numbers

### Rule 26: Amount of time, money, distance, weight as one sum → singular

::: rule
**Rule 26.** When a plural noun of **time, money, distance, weight, volume, or measurement** is thought of as **one total amount**, the verb is **singular**.
:::

::: ex
- Ten miles **is** a long distance.
- Five hundred taka **is** too much for this shirt.
- Two thousand dollars **is** a big sum.
- Five years **is** a long time.
- Fifty kilograms **is** too heavy for a child to carry.
- Two hours **is** enough to finish the exam.
- Twenty litres of water **is** required every day.
- Three-fourths of the distance **has** been covered.
:::

But when the units are thought of **separately (one by one)**, the verb is **plural**:

::: ex
- Five years **have** passed since his death. (years passing one after another)
- Ten taka coins **were** scattered on the floor.
- Many miles **were** walked by the pilgrims.
- Two hours **have** passed since he left.
:::

### Rule 27: Arithmetic

In addition and multiplication, both singular and plural verbs are used; in subtraction and division, the singular is used.

::: ex
- Two and two **make/makes** four. (both accepted; *make* is more common in BCS keys)
- Five times six **is/are** thirty.
- Ten minus four **is** six.
- Twenty divided by five **is** four.
- Six multiplied by two **is** twelve.
:::

### Rule 28: Fractions and percentages → follow the noun after *of*

::: rule
**Rule 28.** With **fractions, percentages, proportions** (two-thirds of, half of, 50 percent of, a quarter of, a proportion of, a part of), the verb agrees with the **noun after *of***.
:::

::: ex
- Two-thirds of the land **is** under water.
- Two-thirds of the people **are** illiterate.
- Fifty percent of the work **has** been completed.
- Fifty percent of the workers **have** joined the strike.
- A quarter of the cake **was** eaten.
- Three-quarters of the students **were** present.
- One-third of the mangoes **are** rotten.
- Forty percent of the population **lives/live** in villages. (*population* is collective; singular is preferred, plural acceptable)
- What percentage of students **were** successful?
:::

### Rule 29: *One and a half* and decimal numbers

Expressions like *one and a half* refer to more than one but traditional grammar treats the head (*one*) as decisive: **one and a half + plural noun + singular verb** when seen as an amount. Many modern grammars accept a plural verb. The safest exam answer:

::: ex
- One and a half hours **is** enough. (amount → singular)
- One and a half years **has** passed since then. (traditional) / **have** passed (modern)
- 1.5 kilograms **is** the weight of the parcel.
- 0.5 percent **is** a small amount.
:::

### Rule 30: Numbers as names, titles, words, letters

When a number, word, letter or phrase is mentioned **as a word**, it is singular.

::: ex
- "Seven" **is** a lucky number for some people.
- "Ps and Qs" **is** an idiom meaning manners.
- Two **is** an even number.
- "Mice" **is** the plural of "mouse".
:::

## 23.9 Titles, Names and Special Subjects

### Rule 31: Titles of books, films, newspapers, songs → singular

::: rule
**Rule 31.** The **title** of a book, film, play, newspaper, magazine, song or painting is **singular** even if it is plural in form — because it is the name of **one** work.
:::

::: ex
- *The Arabian Nights* **is** an interesting book. (✘ are)
- *Gulliver's Travels* **was** written by Jonathan Swift.
- *Great Expectations* **is** a novel by Charles Dickens.
- *The Canterbury Tales* **was** written by Chaucer.
- *Sons and Lovers* **is** a novel by D. H. Lawrence.
- *The Daily Star* **is** a popular English daily.
- *Romeo and Juliet* **is** a tragedy by Shakespeare.
- *The Three Musketeers* **is** an adventure novel.
- *The Times* **has** published the report.
:::

### Rule 32: Names of countries, organisations, firms

Names of countries, states, organisations and companies that look plural are **singular** because each is one unit.

::: ex
- The United States **is** a powerful country.
- The Netherlands **is** famous for tulips.
- The Philippines **is** an island nation.
- The United Nations **was** founded in 1945.
- Ahmed and Sons **is** a well-known firm.
- General Motors **has** opened a new plant.
- The Maldives **is** a beautiful tourist destination.
:::

Note: When we mean the islands themselves, plural is possible: *The Maldives **are** a chain of coral islands.*

### Rule 33: *The + adjective* → plural verb (when it means a class of people)

::: rule
**Rule 33.** When an adjective is used with *the* to mean a **group/class of people**, it is **plural**: **the rich, the poor, the old, the young, the sick, the blind, the deaf, the dead, the wounded, the unemployed, the educated, the honest, the brave, the wise, the injured.**
:::

::: ex
- The rich **are** not always happy.
- The poor **are** getting poorer.
- The wounded **were** taken to hospital.
- The young **have** a duty to the old.
- The honest **are** respected everywhere.
- The brave **deserve** the fair.
:::

But when *the + adjective* refers to an **abstract quality** or a **single person**, use a **singular** verb:

::: ex
- The beautiful **is** not always good. (abstract quality, *beauty*)
- The unknown **is** always frightening.
- The impossible **has** happened.
- The accused **was** acquitted. (one person; *the accused were* if more than one)
- The deceased **was** a teacher.
:::

**Nationalities** ending in *-sh, -ch, -ese, -ss* (*the English, the French, the Dutch, the Chinese, the Japanese, the Swiss*) are plural: *The English **are** a practical people. The Japanese **are** hard-working.*

## 23.10 Subject and Complement

### Rule 34: The verb agrees with the subject, not the complement

::: rule
**Rule 34.** A **linking verb** (*be, become, seem, remain*) agrees with its **subject** (the word before it in a normal statement), **not** with the complement (the word after it).
:::

::: ex
- Our only hope **is** your sons. (subject = *hope*)
- Your sons **are** our only hope. (subject = *sons*)
- The main problem **was** the floods.
- The floods **were** the main problem.
- His greatest strength **is** his teammates.
- Bad roads **are** the main cause of accidents.
- The main cause of accidents **is** bad roads.
- Books **are** my best friend.
- My best friend **is** books.
:::

### Rule 35: "It" as a dummy (anticipatory) subject → always singular

::: ex
- It **is** the students who **are** protesting. (*It is* singular; *who are* agrees with *students*)
- It **was** the floods that **destroyed** the crops.
- It **is** they who **are** responsible.
:::

## 23.11 Distributive, Partitive and Comparative Structures

### Rule 36: *Kind of / sort of / type of*

::: rule
**Rule 36.** **This/That + kind (sort, type) of + singular noun + singular verb**; **These/Those + kinds (sorts, types) of + plural noun + plural verb**.
:::

::: ex
- This kind of book **is** useful. (✘ These kind of books are)
- These kinds of books **are** useful.
- That type of man **is** dangerous.
- Those types of men **are** dangerous.
- What sort of people **are** they?
:::

In informal speech, "*these kind of things*" is common, but it is **wrong** in exams.

### Rule 37: Comparisons do not change the subject

Words introduced by *than* or *as…as* do not affect the verb.

::: ex
- My brother, more than my sisters, **likes** music.
- She is taller than her sisters **are**. (verb agrees with *sisters*)
- Rahim works harder than his friends **do**.
- He sings better than I **do**. (✘ better than me do)
:::

### Rule 38: *A great deal of / A large amount of / Much / Little / Less* → singular

::: ex
- A great deal of money **has** been spent.
- A large amount of rice **is** wasted every year.
- Much water **has** flowed under the bridge.
- Little knowledge **is** a dangerous thing.
- Less work **is** required now.
:::

Compare: *A large number of trees **were** cut down.* (countable → plural)

### Rule 39: *Enough / Plenty / Lots / Heaps* → follow the noun

::: ex
- Lots of food **is** available. / Lots of people **are** coming.
- Plenty of money **is** needed. / Plenty of jobs **are** available.
- Enough rice **is** stored. / Enough chairs **are** available.
:::

## 23.12 Special Verb Forms Affecting Agreement

### Rule 40: The subjunctive breaks the normal agreement

The **subjunctive mood** uses the **base form** of the verb (no -s) and **were** for all persons in unreal conditions. It is tested in GMAT, GRE and BCS.

**(a) Unreal / hypothetical *were*:**

::: ex
- If I **were** a bird, I would fly. (✘ If I was a bird — informal)
- If he **were** rich, he would help the poor.
- I wish I **were** a king.
- He talks as if he **were** the owner.
- Suppose she **were** here, what would you say?
:::

**(b) Mandative (formal) subjunctive** after verbs and expressions of demand, suggestion, order, necessity — *suggest, recommend, insist, demand, require, propose, request, order, urge, it is essential/necessary/important/vital/imperative that*:

::: ex
- The teacher suggested that he **attend** the class regularly. (✘ attends)
- The doctor recommended that she **take** rest.
- It is essential that every student **submit** the form today.
- The court ordered that the prisoner **be** released.
- They demanded that the manager **resign**.
- I insist that he **leave** at once.
- It is important that she **be** present.
:::

::: gre
**GMAT Corner:** After *require, demand, insist, recommend, suggest, request*, the GMAT expects the **bare subjunctive**: *"The law requires that every driver **carry** a licence"* — **not** *carries*, **not** *should carry* (often marked wordy) and **not** *to carry* after *that*. Also remember: *"require X to do"* (infinitive) is fine without *that*.
:::

**(c) Formulaic subjunctive** in fixed wishes and prayers:

::: ex
- God **bless** you. (✘ blesses)
- Long **live** Bangladesh! (✘ lives)
- God **save** the King.
- Heaven **help** us.
- Be that as it **may**…
:::

### Rule 41: Verb after *Let's / Let him* etc.

After *let*, the verb is in the bare form: *Let him **go**. Let them **come**. Let us **work** hard.* (✘ Let him goes.)

### Rule 42: Verb after "*Who* / *What* / *Which*" as subjects in questions

*Who* and *what* used as subjects generally take a **singular** verb unless the answer is clearly expected to be plural.

::: ex
- Who **is** there? (✘ Who are there — unless you know there are several persons)
- Who **are** those people?
- What **is** the matter?
- What **are** your plans? (subject = *your plans*)
- Which of these books **is** yours? (one book expected)
- Which of these books **are** yours? (more than one expected)
:::

## 23.13 Pronoun–Antecedent Agreement

Agreement is not only between subject and verb. **A pronoun must also agree with the noun it refers to (its antecedent)** in number, person and gender. This topic is closely related and is heavily tested on the SAT and GMAT, and in BCS "error detection" items.

### Rule 43: Singular antecedent → singular pronoun

::: ex
- Every student must bring **his or her** own pen. (formal)
- Each of the boys has done **his** duty. (✘ their duty in formal exams)
- Everyone should love **his** country. (traditional) / **their** country (modern, gender-neutral; accepted by SAT style rules today)
- Neither of the girls has finished **her** homework.
- Many a man has lost **his** life.
- One should do **one's** duty. (✘ one should do his duty in strict British usage; American English accepts *his*)
:::

::: note
**Singular "they":** Modern English (and the College Board/SAT) accepts *they/their* for an indefinite person: *Someone left **their** umbrella.* But a **collective noun used as a unit** or a **named thing** must not take *they*: ✘ *The company announced **their** profits.* ✔ *The company announced **its** profits.* In BCS and Bank exams, prefer the traditional *his / his or her* with *each, every, everyone, everybody, anybody, nobody*.
:::

### Rule 44: Compound antecedents

- Joined by *and* → plural pronoun: *Rahim and Karim did **their** best.*
- Joined by *or/nor* → nearest antecedent: *Neither the teacher nor the students raised **their** hands. Neither the students nor the teacher raised **his** hand.*
- *As well as / with* → first antecedent: *The captain, as well as the players, did **his** best.*

### Rule 45: Collective antecedents

The pronoun must follow the same logic as the verb: *The jury **has** given **its** verdict. / The jury **are** divided in **their** opinions.* (Never: *The jury has given their verdict.*)

## 23.14 Agreement in Sentences with *There* and Compound Subjects

### Rule 46: *There is / There are* with compound subjects

When a compound subject follows *there*, formal grammar uses a **plural** verb. However, many grammars and native speakers use a **singular** verb when the **first noun is singular** (proximity). For exam purposes:

::: ex
- There **are** a pen and two pencils in the box. (formal / safest with *and*)
- There **is** a pen and two pencils in the box. (accepted informally — proximity)
- There **are** two pencils and a pen in the box. (first noun plural → plural, no debate)
- There **is** a lot of work to do.
- There **are** a lot of mistakes.
- There **is** no one in the room.
- There **is** no water in the bottle. / There **are** no books on the shelf.
- There **is** more than one reason. (*more than one* → singular)
:::

### Rule 47: Agreement in correlative sentences with *as well as* at the start

When *as well as* begins the sentence, the subject after it is still the main one:

::: ex
- As well as his brother, Rahim **is** coming.
- Along with his friends, the boy **was** arrested.
- In addition to the manager, the clerks **were** absent.
:::

## 23.15 Agreement of Tense and Number in Complex Sentences

### Rule 48: Each clause has its own subject

In complex and compound sentences, **each verb agrees with its own subject**, not with the main subject of the sentence.

::: ex
- The boy who **plays** the guitar **lives** next door.
- The man whose sons **are** doctors **is** my uncle.
- I know that the students **are** ready and that the teacher **is** coming.
- The book I gave to the girls **was** expensive.
- The girls I gave the book to **were** happy.
- Everyone thinks that the questions **were** difficult.
:::

### Rule 49: Parallel verbs share the same subject

When one subject has two or more verbs, **all** must agree.

::: ex
- She **reads**, **writes** and **speaks** English fluently. (✘ She reads, write and speaks)
- The students **have** completed the work and **are** waiting for the results.
- He neither **drinks** nor **smokes**.
:::

## 23.16 Miscellaneous Rules Frequently Tested in Bangladesh

### Rule 50: *Each other / one another* do not affect agreement

*They love each other. We help one another.* The verb agrees with *they/we*.

### Rule 51: *Nobody but / No one except / Nothing but* → singular

::: ex
- Nobody but the teachers **was** present. (✘ were)
- No one except his parents **knows** the truth.
- Nothing but leaves **was** found on the tree.
- Nothing but trees **is** seen here.
:::

### Rule 52: *Not one of / Not a single* → singular

::: ex
- Not one of the students **has** done the homework.
- Not a single tree **was** left standing.
:::

### Rule 53: Infinitive or bare verb after certain verbs

Verbs after *to* never change: *He wants **to go**.* (✘ to goes) After auxiliaries *do/does/did*, use the base form: *He **does not know**.* (✘ does not knows) *Did she **come**?* (✘ did she came) These are "agreement" errors frequently placed in Right-Form-of-Verb items.

### Rule 54: *The* + *superlative* + plural noun + relative clause

*He is the best of all the players who **have** played here.* The relative clause refers to *players* (plural).

### Rule 55: *Either / Neither* as determiners

*Either* and *neither* before a noun take a **singular noun** and a **singular verb**: *Neither answer **is** correct. Either side **is** acceptable.* (✘ Neither answers are correct.)

### Rule 56: "*A* + adjective + *number / few / many*"

*A good many / a great many / a few / a large number* → plural. *A great deal / a large amount / a little / a good deal* → singular.

### Rule 57: Distributive *every* with numbers

*Every* can be followed by a number and a plural noun to show regular intervals: *every two days, every three hours, every five years.* Here the time expression is usually an adverbial, not the subject, so the verb agrees with the real subject: *Every three hours, a bus **leaves** the terminal. The census **is** held every ten years.*

### Rule 58: Double subjects with *and* in negative form

*Not only… but also* follows proximity (Rule 6). But "*X, and not Y*" follows **X** (Rule 5): *The students, and not the teacher, **are** responsible.*

### Rule 59: Hyphenated compound adjectives do not take plural

A number used as part of a compound adjective before a noun stays singular: *a five-year plan* (✘ five-years plan), *a ten-taka note* (✘ ten-takas note), *a two-mile walk*, *a three-day tour*. This is closely related to agreement and is a very common BCS error item.

::: ex
- He made a ten-minute speech. (✘ ten-minutes speech)
- She has a five-year-old son. (✘ five-years-old son)
- Bangladesh launched its first five-year plan in 1973.
:::

### Rule 60: Nouns of number after a numeral: *dozen, score, hundred, thousand, million, pair, stone, head (of cattle), gross*

When these words follow a definite number, they take **no -s**: *two dozen eggs, three hundred people, five thousand taka, four score years, ten head of cattle.* (✘ two dozens eggs). With *of* and no number, -s is used: *dozens of eggs, hundreds of people, thousands of taka.*

::: ex
- Two dozen pens **were** bought.
- Three hundred students **are** taking the test.
- Dozens of people **were** injured.
- Millions of people **live** in Dhaka.
:::

## 23.17 Common Error Patterns: Incorrect vs Correct

The following sentences show the errors that appear most often in Bangladeshi competitive exams and in the error-identification sections of the SAT and GMAT. Study the reason for each correction.

::: errs
1. ✘ The news are very sad. ✔ The news **is** very sad. — *news* is singular.
2. ✘ Each of the students have a book. ✔ Each of the students **has** a book. — *each* is singular.
3. ✘ Neither of the two boys were present. ✔ Neither of the two boys **was** present.
4. ✘ One of my friend is a doctor. ✔ One of my **friends** is a doctor.
5. ✘ The number of students are increasing. ✔ The number of students **is** increasing.
6. ✘ A number of boys was absent. ✔ A number of boys **were** absent.
7. ✘ Many a boys were present. ✔ Many a **boy was** present.
8. ✘ The police has arrested the thief. ✔ The police **have** arrested the thief.
9. ✘ The cattle is grazing. ✔ The cattle **are** grazing.
10. ✘ Mathematics are a difficult subject. ✔ Mathematics **is** a difficult subject.
11. ✘ The teacher as well as his students were present. ✔ … **was** present.

## 23.18 A Step-by-Step Method for Any Agreement Question

When you face any agreement item in an exam, follow these steps in order:

1. **Find the verb(s)** in the sentence. Underline every finite verb (*is, are, was, were, has, have, does, do* and present-tense main verbs).
2. **Find the true subject** of each verb. Cross out prepositional phrases (*of…, in…, with…*), appositives and relative clauses. Look *after* the verb in sentences beginning with *There/Here*, in questions and in inverted sentences.
3. **Check the joining words.** *And* → usually plural (but check Rules 2, 3, 4). *Or/nor/either…or/neither…nor/not only…but also* → nearer subject. *As well as/with/along with/together with/besides* → first subject.
4. **Check special words.** Is the subject an indefinite pronoun (each, every, either, neither, everyone, none, all, some, most)? A collective noun? A noun of the *news/mathematics/measles* type? An uncountable noun? A *people/police/cattle* type noun? A Latin plural?
5. **Check amounts and titles.** Money, time, distance and weight as one sum → singular. Titles of books and names of countries → singular. Fractions → follow the noun after *of*.
6. **Check the relative clause.** *Who/which/that* → agrees with its antecedent (and in person: *I who am, you who are*). *One of the … who* → plural; *the only one of the … who* → singular.
7. **Check the mood.** *If I were / I wish I were*; mandative *that he go / that she be*; *Long live*, *God bless*.
8. **Check the pronouns.** A pronoun referring back to the subject must match it (*the committee … its; each … his*).

::: tip
**Final Advice:** Read the sentence **aloud in your mind without the extra words**. *"The box ~~of old and rusty iron nails~~ is heavy."* Once the "noise" is removed, the correct agreement becomes obvious. This single habit will solve more than half of all agreement questions in BCS, Bank, admission, SAT, GRE and GMAT papers.
:::
