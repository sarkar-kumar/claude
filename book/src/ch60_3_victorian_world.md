## 60.17 World Writers of the Victorian Era

Competitive exams in Bangladesh regularly ask about major European and American writers who were contemporaries of the Victorians. They are grouped here.

### 60.17.1 Leo Tolstoy (1828–1910)

::: writer
**Nationality:** Russian · **Full name:** Count Lev Nikolayevich Tolstoy · **Estate:** **Yasnaya Polyana** · **Titles:** one of the greatest novelists of all time; later a moral and religious teacher of **non-violence** (influenced **Mahatma Gandhi**) · **Major works:** ***War and Peace*** (1865–69), ***Anna Karenina*** (1875–77), *Childhood, Boyhood, Youth* (autobiographical trilogy), *Sevastopol Sketches*, *The Cossacks*, ***The Death of Ivan Ilyich*** (1886), *The Kreutzer Sonata* (1889), ***Resurrection*** (1899), *Hadji Murad*, *A Confession* (1882), *What Is Art?* (1897), *The Kingdom of God Is Within You* (1894), short stories *How Much Land Does a Man Need?*, *Where Love Is, God Is*, *Master and Man*
:::

::: work
**War and Peace (1865–69).** An immense epic novel of Russian society during the **Napoleonic Wars** (1805–1812), including Napoleon's invasion of Russia, the **Battle of Borodino** and the burning of Moscow. It follows several aristocratic families — the **Rostovs**, **Bolkonskys** and **Bezukhovs** — and above all three characters: the awkward, searching **Pierre Bezukhov**, who inherits a vast fortune and seeks the meaning of life; the proud, brave **Prince Andrei Bolkonsky**, who is mortally wounded at Borodino; and the lively, warm-hearted **Natasha Rostova**, who loves Andrei and finally marries Pierre. Real historical figures include **Napoleon** and the Russian commander **Kutuzov**. Tolstoy includes long essays on his **philosophy of history**: great men do not control events; history is shaped by countless ordinary actions. Other characters: Nikolai Rostov, Princess Marya, Hélène Kuragina, Anatole Kuragin, Platon Karataev, Sonya, Dolokhov.

**Anna Karenina (1875–77).** **Anna Karenina**, the beautiful wife of the cold senior official **Alexei Karenin** in St Petersburg, falls passionately in love with the dashing cavalry officer **Count Alexei Vronsky**. She leaves her husband and her beloved son **Seryozha** to live with Vronsky and has his child, but society rejects her, Karenin refuses a divorce, and her jealousy and isolation grow until she throws herself **under a train**. In a parallel plot, the sincere landowner **Konstantin Levin** (partly Tolstoy himself) marries **Kitty Shcherbatskaya** after a first refusal and finds meaning in family, farm work and faith. Also: Stepan Oblonsky (Stiva), Dolly.
:::

::: quote
"All happy families are alike; each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way." — *Anna Karenina* (opening)
:::

### 60.17.2 Karl Marx (1818–1883)

::: writer
**Nationality:** German (born in Trier); lived in exile in **London** from 1849 until his death; buried in Highgate Cemetery · **Field:** philosophy, economics, political theory — founder of **Marxism** / scientific socialism · **Collaborator:** **Friedrich Engels** · **Major works:** ***The Communist Manifesto*** (**1848**, with Engels), ***Das Kapital*** (*Capital*, Vol. 1, **1867**; vols. 2–3 edited by Engels, 1885, 1894), *The German Ideology*, *The Eighteenth Brumaire of Louis Napoleon*, *A Contribution to the Critique of Political Economy* (1859), *Economic and Philosophic Manuscripts of 1844*
:::

Marx argued that history is driven by **class struggle** and by changes in the material conditions of production (**historical materialism**). Under capitalism, the **bourgeoisie** (owners of capital) exploit the **proletariat** (wage workers), appropriating **surplus value**; capitalism would be overthrown by a proletarian revolution, leading to a classless **communist** society. His ideas inspired the Russian Revolution (1917) and shaped twentieth-century politics, sociology and literary criticism (**Marxist criticism**).

::: quote
"The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles." — *The Communist Manifesto*

"Workers of the world, unite! You have nothing to lose but your chains." — *The Communist Manifesto* (popular form of the closing words)

"Religion … is the opium of the people." — *A Contribution to the Critique of Hegel's Philosophy of Right* (1844)

"The philosophers have only interpreted the world, in various ways; the point is to change it." — *Theses on Feuerbach*
:::

### 60.17.3 Edgar Allan Poe (1809–1849)

::: writer
**Nationality:** American (born Boston; raised in Richmond, Virginia, by John Allan) · **Titles:** **Father of the Detective Story**; master of the **Gothic horror** short story; theorist of the short story ("single effect") · **Wife:** his young cousin Virginia Clemm (died 1847) · **Died:** mysteriously in Baltimore, 1849 · **Poems:** ***The Raven*** (1845), *Annabel Lee*, *To Helen*, *The Bells*, *Ulalume*, *Lenore*, *A Dream Within a Dream* · **Stories:** ***The Murders in the Rue Morgue*** (1841 — first modern detective story, detective **C. Auguste Dupin**), *The Mystery of Marie Rogêt*, *The Purloined Letter*, ***The Fall of the House of Usher*** (1839), ***The Tell-Tale Heart*** (1843), *The Black Cat*, *The Pit and the Pendulum*, *The Masque of the Red Death*, *The Cask of Amontillado*, *Ligeia*, *The Gold-Bug* · **Novel:** *The Narrative of Arthur Gordon Pym* · **Criticism:** *The Philosophy of Composition* (1846), *The Poetic Principle*
:::

::: work
**The Raven (1845).** On a "midnight dreary" in December, a grieving scholar, mourning his lost love **Lenore**, hears a tapping; a **raven** enters and perches on a bust of **Pallas** (Athena) above his door. To every question — whether he will find comfort, whether he will meet Lenore in heaven — the bird answers "**Nevermore**", driving him into despair. In *The Philosophy of Composition*, Poe explained how he constructed the poem logically for the effect of melancholy beauty.

**The Fall of the House of Usher (1839).** The narrator visits his boyhood friend **Roderick Usher**, who lives in a decaying mansion with his twin sister **Madeline**. Madeline seemingly dies and is entombed in a vault; days later, during a storm, she appears, bloody, having escaped her premature burial, and falls dead upon Roderick, who dies of terror; the house splits and sinks into the tarn.

**The Tell-Tale Heart (1843).** A madman, insisting on his sanity, describes how he murdered an old man because of his "vulture eye" and hid the body under the floorboards; when the police come, he imagines he hears the dead man's heart beating louder and louder, and confesses.
:::

::: quote
"Quoth the Raven, 'Nevermore.'" — *The Raven*

"All that we see or seem / Is but a dream within a dream." — *A Dream Within a Dream*

"To the glory that was Greece, / And the grandeur that was Rome." — *To Helen*
:::

### 60.17.4 Alexandre Dumas (père) (1802–1870)

::: writer
**Nationality:** French · **Titles:** master of the **historical adventure novel**; one of the most widely read French writers · **Grandfather:** a French nobleman; **grandmother:** an enslaved Afro-Caribbean woman of Saint-Domingue (Haiti); his father was a general in Napoleon's army · **Son:** Alexandre Dumas *fils* — author of *La Dame aux Camélias* (*The Lady of the Camellias*, 1848, basis of Verdi's opera *La Traviata*) · **Major works:** ***The Three Musketeers*** (1844), ***The Count of Monte Cristo*** (1844–46), *Twenty Years After* (1845), *The Vicomte of Bragelonne* (including *The Man in the Iron Mask*), *Queen Margot*, *The Black Tulip* (1850)
:::

::: work
**The Three Musketeers (1844).** In seventeenth-century France, the young Gascon **d'Artagnan** comes to Paris to join the King's Musketeers and befriends three of them — **Athos**, **Porthos** and **Aramis**. They serve **King Louis XIII** and **Queen Anne of Austria** against the scheming **Cardinal Richelieu** and his beautiful, deadly agent **Milady de Winter**; in the famous episode of the queen's **diamond studs**, they recover jewels the queen had given to the Duke of Buckingham. Their motto: "**All for one, one for all**" (*Tous pour un, un pour tous*).

**The Count of Monte Cristo (1844–46).** The young sailor **Edmond Dantès**, about to marry **Mercédès**, is falsely denounced as a Bonapartist agent by his jealous rivals **Danglars** and **Fernand Mondego**, and imprisoned without trial by the ambitious prosecutor **Villefort** in the island fortress **Château d'If**. After fourteen years, his fellow prisoner **Abbé Faria** educates him and tells him of a hidden treasure on the island of **Monte Cristo**. Dantès escapes by taking Faria's place in a burial sack, finds the treasure, and returns to Paris as the mysterious, fabulously rich **Count of Monte Cristo** to reward his friends and take elaborate revenge on his enemies. He ends with the words "**Wait and hope**" (*Attendre et espérer*).
:::

### 60.17.5 Victor Hugo (1802–1885)

::: writer
**Nationality:** French · **Titles:** leader of **French Romanticism**; France's greatest poet and novelist of the nineteenth century · **Exile:** opposed Napoleon III's coup (1851) and lived in exile in the Channel Islands (**Jersey and Guernsey**) for almost 19 years · **Buried:** the Panthéon, Paris (state funeral attended by two million people) · **Major works:** novels ***Notre-Dame de Paris*** (*The Hunchback of Notre-Dame*, 1831), ***Les Misérables*** (1862), *Toilers of the Sea* (1866), *The Man Who Laughs*, *Ninety-Three*; plays *Cromwell* (with its Preface — the manifesto of French Romanticism, 1827), ***Hernani*** (1830), *Ruy Blas*; poetry *Les Contemplations*, *La Légende des siècles*, *Les Châtiments*
:::

::: work
**Les Misérables (1862).** The former convict **Jean Valjean**, imprisoned for **19 years** for stealing a loaf of bread to feed his sister's children, is transformed when the kind **Bishop Myriel** forgives him for stealing silver and gives him the candlesticks too. Under a new name (**Monsieur Madeleine**) he becomes a mayor and factory owner. He promises the dying **Fantine**, a poor woman driven to prostitution, to care for her daughter **Cosette**, whom he rescues from the cruel innkeepers, the **Thénardiers**. He is relentlessly pursued by the police inspector **Javert**, who believes that the law must never forgive. During the **June Rebellion of 1832** in Paris, Valjean saves the life of **Marius Pontmercy**, who loves Cosette, carrying him through the sewers; Javert, unable to reconcile Valjean's mercy with his rigid sense of duty, drowns himself in the Seine. Marius marries Cosette; Valjean dies peacefully. Other characters: **Éponine**, **Gavroche**, **Enjolras**.

**The Hunchback of Notre-Dame (1831).** In fifteenth-century Paris, the deformed, deaf bell-ringer of Notre-Dame, **Quasimodo**, loves the beautiful gypsy dancer **Esmeralda**, who is also desired by his guardian, the archdeacon **Claude Frollo**. Frollo stabs Esmeralda's lover, the captain **Phoebus**, and lets her be accused; Quasimodo saves her by claiming sanctuary in the cathedral, but she is finally hanged. Quasimodo throws Frollo from the cathedral tower and dies embracing Esmeralda's body in a tomb.
:::

::: quote
"To love or to have loved, that is enough. Ask nothing further." — *Les Misérables*

"Nothing is more powerful than an idea whose time has come." — attributed to Hugo (adapted from *Histoire d'un crime*)
:::

### 60.17.6 Walt Whitman (1819–1892)

::: writer
**Nationality:** American (Long Island, New York; Brooklyn) · **Titles:** the "**Father of Free Verse**"; "the Good Gray Poet"; the poet of American **democracy** · **Career:** printer, teacher, journalist; volunteer nurse in army hospitals during the **American Civil War** · **Masterpiece:** ***Leaves of Grass*** (first edition **1855**, 12 poems; revised and enlarged through nine editions until the "Deathbed edition" of 1891–92) · **Key poems:** ***Song of Myself***, *I Hear America Singing*, *Crossing Brooklyn Ferry*, *Out of the Cradle Endlessly Rocking*, *I Sing the Body Electric*, *Passage to India*, ***O Captain! My Captain!*** and ***When Lilacs Last in the Dooryard Bloom'd*** (elegies on **Abraham Lincoln**, 1865), *Drum-Taps*
:::

Whitman broke with traditional metre and rhyme, writing long, rolling lines of **free verse** with catalogues, repetition and parallelism (influenced by the Bible). He celebrated the self, the body, the common people, democracy, comradeship and the American nation. **Emerson** greeted the first edition: "I greet you at the beginning of a great career."

::: quote
"I celebrate myself, and sing myself, / And what I assume you shall assume." — *Song of Myself*

"Do I contradict myself? / Very well then I contradict myself, / (I am large, I contain multitudes.)" — *Song of Myself*

"O Captain! my Captain! our fearful trip is done, / The ship has weather'd every rack, the prize we sought is won." — *O Captain! My Captain!*

"I hear America singing, the varied carols I hear." — *I Hear America Singing*
:::

### 60.17.7 Nathaniel Hawthorne (1804–1864)

::: writer
**Nationality:** American (born **Salem, Massachusetts**; an ancestor was a judge in the Salem witch trials) · **Movement:** American Romanticism / Dark Romanticism · **Friend of:** Herman Melville (who dedicated *Moby-Dick* to him) · **Major works:** ***The Scarlet Letter*** (1850), ***The House of the Seven Gables*** (1851), *The Blithedale Romance* (1852), *The Marble Faun* (1860); story collections *Twice-Told Tales* (1837), *Mosses from an Old Manse* — stories *Young Goodman Brown*, *The Minister's Black Veil*, *Rappaccini's Daughter*, *The Birthmark*; children's *A Wonder-Book for Girls and Boys*, *Tanglewood Tales*
:::

::: work
**The Scarlet Letter (1850).** In **seventeenth-century Puritan Boston**, **Hester Prynne** is punished for **adultery** — she has borne a daughter, **Pearl**, while her elderly husband was missing — by being made to stand on the scaffold and wear a scarlet letter "**A**" on her breast for life. She refuses to name her lover, the young, respected minister **Arthur Dimmesdale**, who suffers secretly from guilt. Hester's husband returns under the name of **Roger Chillingworth**, a physician, and devotes himself to tormenting Dimmesdale. Hester proposes that they flee to Europe, but Dimmesdale, after preaching a brilliant election-day sermon, publicly confesses on the scaffold, revealing a mark on his chest, and dies in Hester's arms. Chillingworth dies within a year, leaving his fortune to Pearl. Hester lives on in Boston, doing charitable work, until the "A" comes to mean "Able". Themes: sin, guilt, hypocrisy, Puritan rigidity, the individual against society.
:::

### 60.17.8 Maxim Gorky (1868–1936)

::: writer
**Real name:** Alexei Maximovich Peshkov · **Pen-name:** **Gorky** — "bitter" in Russian · **Nationality:** Russian · **Titles:** founder of **Socialist Realism**; the "stormy petrel" of the Russian Revolution · **Life:** orphaned young, worked as a dishwasher, baker and tramp across Russia; supported the revolution; lived abroad (Capri) and returned to the USSR; the city of Nizhny Novgorod was named Gorky (1932–1990) · **Major works:** novel ***Mother*** (1906/07), play ***The Lower Depths*** (1902), autobiographical trilogy ***My Childhood*** (1913–14), ***In the World*** (*My Apprenticeship*, 1916), ***My Universities*** (1923); *Foma Gordeyev* (1899), *The Life of Klim Samgin*; stories *Chelkash*, *Twenty-Six Men and a Girl*, *Makar Chudra*; *The Song of the Stormy Petrel* (poem)
:::

::: work
**Mother (1906).** **Pelageya Nilovna Vlasova**, a simple, timid, illiterate factory worker's widow who has suffered from her drunken, brutal husband, is gradually awakened to political consciousness through her son **Pavel Vlasov**, a revolutionary worker. She begins to help his comrades by smuggling leaflets into the factory; after Pavel's arrest and defiant speech at his trial, she continues his work and is finally seized by the police while distributing his speech, crying out for truth. It is regarded as the first novel of **Socialist Realism**.

**The Lower Depths (1902).** A play set in a cellar-like doss-house (night shelter) where outcasts — a thief (Pepel), an actor, a baron, a prostitute, a locksmith — live in misery. The wandering pilgrim **Luka** comforts them with consoling lies, while **Satin** argues that "**Man — that has a proud sound!**" and that truth is the god of a free man.
:::

**SAT / GRE Corner — Victorian and Nineteenth-Century Echoes:** Many familiar words and phrases come from these writers: **Dickensian** (squalid, or full of colourful characters), **Scrooge** (a miser), **Pecksniffian** (hypocritical), **Micawber-like** (always hopeful), **Gradgrind** (obsessed with facts), **Pickwickian**, **Dorian Gray** (eternal youth), **Jekyll and Hyde** (split personality), **Sherlock** (a detective), **Byronic**, **quixotic**, **Panglossian**, **Malapropism**, **Victorian** (prudish, morally strict).
