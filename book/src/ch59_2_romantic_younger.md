## 59.6 Lord Byron (George Gordon Byron, 6th Baron Byron, 1788–1824)

::: writer
**Born:** London; lame from birth (club foot) · **Education:** Harrow; Trinity College, Cambridge · **Title:** inherited his barony at 10 · **Fame:** "I awoke one morning and found myself famous" (after *Childe Harold* I–II, 1812) · **Exile:** left England for ever in 1816 after scandal and separation from his wife Annabella Milbanke (their daughter **Ada Lovelace** became a pioneer of computing) · **Death:** of fever at **Missolonghi**, Greece, in **1824**, while supporting the **Greek War of Independence** against the Turks — a national hero in Greece · **Major works:** *Hours of Idleness* (1807), *English Bards and Scotch Reviewers* (1809, satire), ***Childe Harold's Pilgrimage*** (1812–18), the "Turkish tales" (*The Giaour*, *The Bride of Abydos*, *The Corsair*, *Lara*), *The Prisoner of Chillon* (1816), ***Manfred*** (1817, verse drama), *Beppo* (1818), *Mazeppa*, ***Don Juan*** (1819–24, unfinished, 16 cantos), ***The Vision of Judgment*** (1822, satire on Southey), *Cain* (1821)
:::

**Byron** was the most famous poet in Europe in his lifetime, and his personality — proud, passionate, rebellious, melancholy, contemptuous of convention — created the **Byronic hero**: a moody, isolated outcast with a mysterious guilt, defiant of society and fate (Childe Harold, Manfred, the Corsair). Unlike the other Romantics, he admired **Pope** and the eighteenth-century satirists, and his greatest achievement is **satire** in the comic epic *Don Juan*, written in **ottava rima** (*abababcc*).

::: work
**Childe Harold's Pilgrimage (1812–1818).** A long poem in **Spenserian stanzas**, in four cantos. A world-weary young nobleman ("Childe" = a youth of noble birth), sated with pleasure, travels through Portugal, Spain, Greece (Cantos I–II), Belgium, the Rhine, Switzerland (Canto III — with the famous stanzas on **Waterloo**) and Italy (Canto IV — Venice, Rome, the ocean). The poem mixes travel description, historical reflection and the poet's own melancholy, and gradually the narrator replaces Harold.

**Don Juan (1819–24).** A comic epic satire in **ottava rima**, in 16 cantos (unfinished). Byron reverses the legend of the great seducer: his **Don Juan** is a handsome, innocent young Spaniard who is pursued by women. Sent abroad from Seville after an affair with **Donna Julia**, he is shipwrecked; loved by **Haidée**, a Greek pirate's daughter, on an island idyll; sold as a slave in Constantinople, where he is disguised as a woman in the Sultan's harem (Sultana **Gulbeyaz**); fights at the siege of **Ismail** in the Russian army; becomes a favourite of **Catherine the Great**; and is sent to England, where he moves in high society (Lady **Adeline Amundeville**, Aurora Raby, the Duchess of Fitz-Fulke). The digressive narrator mocks hypocrisy, cant, war, politics and Romantic poets (especially the "Lakers").

**Manfred (1817).** A Faustian verse drama: **Manfred**, a guilt-ridden noble in the Alps, haunted by a crime connected with his beloved **Astarte**, summons spirits but seeks only forgetfulness; he refuses to submit to either Church or demons and dies defiant.

**The Prisoner of Chillon (1816).** **François Bonivard**, a Swiss patriot imprisoned in the castle of Chillon on Lake Geneva, watches his two brothers die in the dungeon; when at last freed, he regrets leaving his prison.
:::

## 59.7 Percy Bysshe Shelley (1792–1822)

::: writer
**Born:** Field Place, Sussex · **Education:** Eton; University College, Oxford — **expelled (1811)** for the pamphlet *The Necessity of Atheism* · **Titles:** the **revolutionary idealist**; "**a beautiful and ineffectual angel, beating in the void his luminous wings in vain**" (Matthew Arnold); "the poet of poets"; "Mad Shelley" (at Eton) · **Wives:** Harriet Westbrook; then **Mary Wollstonecraft Godwin** (**Mary Shelley**, author of *Frankenstein*) · **Death:** drowned when his boat, the *Don Juan*, sank in a storm in the **Gulf of Spezia, Italy**, July **1822**, aged 29; his body was cremated on the beach at Viareggio; his ashes are buried in the Protestant Cemetery in **Rome**, near Keats ("Cor Cordium" — heart of hearts) · **Major works:** *Queen Mab* (1813), *Alastor* (1816), *The Revolt of Islam* (1818), ***Prometheus Unbound*** (1820, lyrical drama), ***The Cenci*** (1819, tragedy), ***Adonais*** (1821, elegy on Keats), *Epipsychidion* (1821), *Hellas*, *The Mask of Anarchy* (1819), ***Ode to the West Wind*** (1819), ***To a Skylark*** (1820), ***Ozymandias*** (1818), *The Cloud*, *Mont Blanc*, *Hymn to Intellectual Beauty*, *The Triumph of Life* (unfinished) · **Prose:** ***A Defence of Poetry*** (1821)
:::

**Shelley** was a passionate idealist who believed in the perfectibility of man through **love, liberty and imagination**, and who attacked tyranny, organised religion, conventional marriage and social injustice (influenced by his father-in-law **William Godwin**'s *Political Justice*). His poetry is **lyrical, musical, ethereal and visionary**, full of images of wind, cloud, light and fire. In *A Defence of Poetry* he declared that "**poets are the unacknowledged legislators of the world**".

::: work
**Ode to the West Wind (1819).** Written in a wood near Florence on a stormy autumn day, the ode is composed of five sections, each made of four **terza rima** tercets and a couplet. The poet addresses the **West Wind** as "**Destroyer and Preserver**", which drives the dead leaves over the earth (I), the clouds through the sky (II) and the waves of the sea (III). He prays to be lifted "as a wave, a leaf, a cloud" (IV), and asks the wind to make him its **lyre** and to scatter his words, like ashes and sparks, among mankind, to awaken the world: "*If Winter comes, can Spring be far behind?*" (V).

**Prometheus Unbound (1820).** A lyrical drama in four acts, reversing Aeschylus. The Titan **Prometheus**, chained to a rock by **Jupiter** for helping mankind, revokes the curse he once uttered against his tyrant, forgiving instead of hating. **Demogorgon** overthrows Jupiter; Prometheus is freed by **Hercules** and reunited with **Asia** (love); mankind is regenerated in a world of universal love and freedom.

**Adonais (1821).** A pastoral elegy on the death of **John Keats** (whom Shelley believed killed by hostile reviews), in 55 Spenserian stanzas. It moves from mourning to the consolation that Keats has become one with the eternal beauty of nature: "*He is made one with Nature*" and "*He has outsoared the shadow of our night*".

**Ozymandias (1818).** A sonnet: a traveller describes a ruined statue in the desert — only "two vast and trunkless legs of stone" and a shattered face with a "sneer of cold command"; the pedestal proclaims, "*My name is Ozymandias, King of Kings: / Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!*" — yet "Nothing beside remains", only the "lone and level sands" — a lesson in the vanity of power.
:::

::: quote
"O wild West Wind, thou breath of Autumn's being." — *Ode to the West Wind*

"I fall upon the thorns of life! I bleed!" — *Ode to the West Wind*

"If Winter comes, can Spring be far behind?" — *Ode to the West Wind* (last line)

"Hail to thee, blithe Spirit! / Bird thou never wert." — *To a Skylark*

"We look before and after, / And pine for what is not… / Our sweetest songs are those that tell of saddest thought." — *To a Skylark*

"Look on my Works, ye Mighty, and despair!" — *Ozymandias*

"Poets are the unacknowledged legislators of the world." — *A Defence of Poetry*

"Poetry is the record of the best and happiest moments of the happiest and best minds." — *A Defence of Poetry*

"Life, like a dome of many-coloured glass, / Stains the white radiance of Eternity." — *Adonais*
:::

## 59.8 John Keats (1795–1821)

::: writer
**Born:** London (Moorfields), son of a livery-stable keeper · **Training:** apprenticed to a surgeon-apothecary; studied at Guy's Hospital; qualified as an apothecary (1816) but gave it up for poetry · **Love:** **Fanny Brawne** · **Illness:** tuberculosis (consumption), which also killed his mother and brother Tom · **Death:** in **Rome**, 23 February **1821**, aged **25**; buried in the Protestant Cemetery · **Epitaph (his own choice):** "**Here lies One Whose Name was writ in Water**" · **Titles:** "the poet of beauty"; "the most Shakespearean of the Romantics" · **Publications:** *Poems* (1817), ***Endymion*** (1818, savagely attacked by *Blackwood's Magazine* and the *Quarterly Review*), ***Lamia, Isabella, The Eve of St. Agnes, and Other Poems*** (1820) — containing the great odes · **Letters:** his letters contain his ideas of "**Negative Capability**" and "the vale of Soul-making"
:::

**Keats** wrote almost all his great poetry in a single remarkable year, **1819** (the "*annus mirabilis*" or "living year"), while ill and in love. His poetry is rich in **sensuous imagery** (sight, sound, taste, touch, smell), worships **beauty**, and is haunted by the conflict between the ideal world of art and imagination and the real world of pain, change and death. Influenced by **Spenser, Shakespeare and Milton**, he loved Greek mythology (**Hellenism**) and the Middle Ages (*The Eve of St. Agnes*, *La Belle Dame sans Merci*).

In a letter (1817) he described **Negative Capability** — the quality of a great poet like Shakespeare — "**when man is capable of being in uncertainties, Mysteries, doubts, without any irritable reaching after fact and reason**".

**Major poems:**

- *On First Looking into Chapman's Homer* (1816, sonnet) — his joy at discovering Homer in Chapman's translation, like "stout Cortez" first seeing the Pacific (historically it was Balboa).
- ***Endymion*** (1818) — long poem on the love of the shepherd Endymion for the moon goddess (Cynthia).
- ***Hyperion*** and *The Fall of Hyperion* — unfinished Miltonic epics on the overthrow of the Titans by the Olympian gods.
- ***The Eve of St. Agnes*** (1819) — a medieval romance in Spenserian stanzas: on St Agnes' Eve, **Porphyro** steals into the castle of his enemies to woo **Madeline**, who believes she will see her future husband in a dream; they elope into the storm.
- ***La Belle Dame sans Merci*** (1819) — a ballad: a pale knight-at-arms, enchanted by a beautiful fairy lady "without pity", wakes alone "on the cold hill's side".
- ***Lamia*** (1819) — a serpent-woman who loves Lycius is destroyed by the philosopher Apollonius's "cold philosophy".
- *Isabella, or the Pot of Basil* — from Boccaccio.
- **The Great Odes (1819):** ***Ode to a Nightingale***, ***Ode on a Grecian Urn***, ***To Autumn***, ***Ode on Melancholy***, ***Ode to Psyche***, *Ode on Indolence*.
- Sonnets: *When I have fears that I may cease to be*, *Bright star, would I were steadfast as thou art*, *On the Grasshopper and Cricket*.

::: work
**Ode to a Nightingale (1819).** Written in a Hampstead garden where a nightingale had built its nest. The poet, heart-aching and drowsy, longs to escape with the immortal bird from a world "where youth grows pale, and spectre-thin, and dies" (recalling his brother Tom's death) — first through wine, then "on the viewless wings of Poesy". In the darkness he imagines the flowers he cannot see, and feels half in love with "easeful Death". The bird's song is "immortal", heard by emperor and clown, by Ruth "amid the alien corn". But the word "forlorn" tolls him back to himself; the song fades — "Was it a vision, or a waking dream? / Fled is that music: — Do I wake or sleep?"

**Ode on a Grecian Urn (1819).** Contemplating an ancient Greek urn decorated with scenes — a lover chasing a maiden, a piper, trees, a sacrifice procession — the poet reflects that art freezes moments into eternity: the lover can never kiss but she will never fade; the melodies are unheard but "sweeter". Art is a "Cold Pastoral" which teases us "out of thought", and the urn's message is: "*Beauty is truth, truth beauty, — that is all / Ye know on earth, and all ye need to know.*"

**To Autumn (1819).** Keats's most serene and perfect ode: autumn as a "season of mists and mellow fruitfulness", personified as a harvester in the granary, asleep in the furrow, and a cider-presser; the poem accepts the passing of the year with the music of gnats, crickets, robins and swallows.
:::

::: quote
"A thing of beauty is a joy for ever: / Its loveliness increases; it will never / Pass into nothingness." — *Endymion* (opening)

"Much have I travell'd in the realms of gold." — *On First Looking into Chapman's Homer*

"My heart aches, and a drowsy numbness pains / My sense." — *Ode to a Nightingale*

"Thou wast not born for death, immortal Bird!" — *Ode to a Nightingale*

"Heard melodies are sweet, but those unheard / Are sweeter." — *Ode on a Grecian Urn*

"Beauty is truth, truth beauty, — that is all / Ye know on earth, and all ye need to know." — *Ode on a Grecian Urn*

## 59.9 Charles Lamb (1775–1834)

::: writer
**Born:** London · **Education:** Christ's Hospital (school friend of Coleridge) · **Career:** clerk at the **East India House** for 33 years · **Pen-name:** "**Elia**" · **Titles:** "**the Prince of English Essayists**"; the most lovable of English essayists · **Major works:** ***Essays of Elia*** (1823) and ***Last Essays of Elia*** (1833); ***Tales from Shakespeare*** (1807, with his sister **Mary Lamb**); *Specimens of English Dramatic Poets* (1808); *The Adventures of Ulysses*; poems *The Old Familiar Faces*
:::

Lamb's life was marked by tragedy: in 1796 his sister **Mary**, in a fit of madness, killed their mother with a knife. Charles devoted his life to caring for her, giving up thoughts of marriage. Despite this, his essays are full of gentle humour, whimsy, pathos and love of London, old books and old customs. His style is personal, intimate, quaint and full of seventeenth-century words.

**Famous *Elia* essays:** *Dream Children: A Reverie* (he tells imaginary children about their great-grandmother and "Alice W——", then they fade away), *A Dissertation upon Roast Pig* (the comic legend of how the Chinese discovered roast pork when Bo-bo burned down his father's house), *Old China*, *The Superannuated Man*, *The South-Sea House*, *Christ's Hospital Five and Thirty Years Ago*, *Mrs. Battle's Opinions on Whist*, *The Praise of Chimney-Sweepers*, *Poor Relations*, *Imperfect Sympathies*, *Witches and Other Night-Fears*, *Bachelor's Complaint of the Behaviour of Married People*.

***Tales from Shakespeare*** (Charles retold the tragedies, Mary the comedies) introduced generations of children to Shakespeare's plots.

::: quote
"I have had playmates, I have had companions, / In my days of childhood, in my joyful school-days; / All, all are gone, the old familiar faces." — *The Old Familiar Faces*

"We are not of Alice, nor of thee, nor are we children at all… We are nothing; less than nothing, and dreams." — *Dream Children*
:::
