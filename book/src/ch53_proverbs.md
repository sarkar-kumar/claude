## 53.1 What Is a Proverb?

A **proverb** is a short, well-known saying that expresses a **general truth or piece of practical wisdom** in a vivid way: *A stitch in time saves nine.* Proverbs are the distilled experience of generations, and every language has them. Many English proverbs have close **Bangla equivalents**, and BCS, Bank and admission exams regularly ask: *"Translate into Bangla / English"*, *"What does the proverb … mean?"*, or *"Complete the proverb"*.

::: tip
**How to use this list:** Learn the exact English wording (proverbs are fixed — *Rome was not built in a day*, not *in one day*), the Bangla equivalent, and the meaning. Many proverbs come in **opposite pairs** (*Too many cooks spoil the broth* / *Many hands make light work*); noticing these helps memory.
:::

**Hunger is the best sauce.** [ক্ষুধার রাজ্যে পৃথিবী গদ্যময়; ক্ষুধা পেলে সবই মিঠা।]{.bn}

**If wishes were horses, beggars would ride.** [ইচ্ছা পূরণ হলে ভিখারিও রাজা হতো।]{.bn} — Wishing alone achieves nothing.

**Ill got, ill spent.** [পাপের ধন প্রায়শ্চিত্তে যায়।]{.bn}

**Self-help is the best help.** [নিজের কাজ নিজে করাই শ্রেয়।]{.bn}

**Slow and steady wins the race.** [ধীরে চলো, টিকে থাকো / সবুরে মেওয়া ফলে।]{.bn}

**Something is better than nothing.** [নেই মামার চেয়ে কানা মামা ভালো।]{.bn}

**Spare the rod and spoil the child.** [শাসন না করলে ছেলে নষ্ট হয়।]{.bn}

## 53.4 Proverbs in Opposite Pairs

| Proverb | Its "opposite" |
|---|---|
| Too many cooks spoil the broth. | Many hands make light work. |
| Look before you leap. | He who hesitates is lost. |
| Absence makes the heart grow fonder. | Out of sight, out of mind. |
| The pen is mightier than the sword. | Actions speak louder than words. |
| Birds of a feather flock together. | Opposites attract. |
| Slow and steady wins the race. | Strike while the iron is hot. |
| Clothes make the man. | Don't judge a book by its cover. |
| Great minds think alike. | Fools seldom differ. |

::: gre
**SAT / GRE / GMAT Corner:** Proverbs themselves rarely appear on international tests, but GRE and SAT reading passages often **allude** to them ("a classic case of the cure being worse than the disease", "the proof is in the pudding") and expect you to understand the implied point. In GMAT Critical Reasoning, many arguments rest on proverb-like assumptions (*"prevention is better than cure"*); identifying such hidden assumptions is the key to "assumption" and "weaken" questions.
:::
