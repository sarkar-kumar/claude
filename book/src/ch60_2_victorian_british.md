## 60.6 William Makepeace Thackeray (1811–1863)

::: writer
**Born:** **Calcutta (Kolkata), India**, where his father worked for the East India Company · **Education:** Charterhouse; Trinity College, Cambridge (left without degree) · **Career:** lost his fortune; journalist and illustrator for *Punch*; first editor of *The Cornhill Magazine* (1860) · **Titles:** the great **satirist** of Victorian society; Dickens's chief rival · **Major works:** ***Vanity Fair*** (1847–48), *The Book of Snobs* (1848), ***The History of Pendennis*** (1848–50), ***The History of Henry Esmond*** (1852, historical novel), *The Newcomes* (1853–55), *The Virginians*, *The Luck of Barry Lyndon* (1844)
:::

Thackeray's novels satirise **snobbery, hypocrisy, greed and social climbing** among the upper and middle classes, with a worldly, ironic, often intrusive narrator who comments on the characters like a showman with his puppets.

::: work
**Vanity Fair: A Novel without a Hero (1847–48).** The title comes from **Bunyan's *The Pilgrim's Progress***. Set during the Napoleonic Wars, the novel follows two contrasting young women who leave Miss Pinkerton's academy together. **Becky Sharp**, the poor, clever, unscrupulous daughter of an artist and a French opera girl, uses charm and cunning to climb society; she secretly marries **Rawdon Crawley**, the younger son of the coarse baronet **Sir Pitt Crawley**, and later uses the wealthy, wicked **Lord Steyne** for money and position, until her husband discovers them and leaves her. **Amelia Sedley**, gentle and good but foolish, marries the selfish, vain **George Osborne**, who is killed at **Waterloo**; she worships his memory for years, ignoring the faithful **Captain William Dobbin**, who has always loved her. Finally Becky, now a disreputable adventuress, shows Amelia George's letter proposing to elope with her, freeing Amelia to marry Dobbin. Becky ends living on an annuity, posing as a respectable charitable lady. Other characters: **Jos Sedley** (Amelia's fat brother, a collector in Bengal), **Miss Crawley**, **Mr. Osborne**, **Lady Jane**.
:::

::: quote
"Ah! Vanitas Vanitatum! which of us is happy in this world? Which of us has his desire? or, having it, is satisfied?" — *Vanity Fair* (closing)

"I think I could be a good woman if I had five thousand a year." — Becky Sharp, *Vanity Fair*
:::

## 60.7 Charlotte Brontë (1816–1855)

::: writer
**Born:** Thornton, Yorkshire; grew up at **Haworth Parsonage** on the Yorkshire moors, daughter of the Irish clergyman Patrick Brontë · **Sisters:** Emily and Anne (also novelists), brother Branwell · **Pen-name:** "**Currer Bell**" (sisters: Ellis and Acton Bell) · **Married:** Arthur Bell Nicholls (1854); died in pregnancy (1855) · **Biography:** by **Elizabeth Gaskell** (*The Life of Charlotte Brontë*, 1857) · **Novels:** ***Jane Eyre*** (1847), ***Shirley*** (1849), ***Villette*** (1853), *The Professor* (posthumous, 1857)
:::

The Brontë sisters published a joint volume of *Poems* (1846) under their male pen-names, then each published a novel in 1847: Charlotte's *Jane Eyre*, Emily's *Wuthering Heights* and Anne's *Agnes Grey*. Charlotte's fiction gives a passionate, first-person voice to a woman's inner life — her desire for love, independence and dignity.

::: work
**Jane Eyre (1847).** Subtitled *An Autobiography*. **Jane Eyre**, an orphan, is ill-treated by her aunt **Mrs. Reed** and her cousins at **Gateshead**, and sent to the harsh charity school **Lowood**, run by the hypocritical **Mr. Brocklehurst**, where her friend **Helen Burns** dies of consumption. After eight years, Jane becomes a governess at **Thornfield Hall**, teaching **Adèle**, the ward of the brooding, sardonic master **Edward Rochester**. They fall in love, but on their wedding day it is revealed that Rochester is already married to **Bertha Mason**, a violently mad Creole woman, whom he keeps locked in the attic (the source of strange laughter and a fire). Refusing to become his mistress, Jane flees, nearly dies of hunger and is taken in by the clergyman **St. John Rivers** and his sisters, who turn out to be her cousins; she inherits a fortune from an uncle. St. John asks her to marry him and go to **India** as a missionary's wife, but Jane, hearing in a mysterious moment Rochester's voice calling her, returns to Thornfield. She finds it burned down: Bertha set fire to it and died, and Rochester, trying to save her, lost a hand and was blinded. Jane marries him; he partly recovers his sight. Other characters: **Grace Poole**, **Mrs. Fairfax**, **Blanche Ingram**, **Miss Temple**, **Richard Mason**, **Bessie**.

**Villette (1853).** **Lucy Snowe**, a reserved English woman, teaches in a girls' school in the Belgian town of **Villette** (Brussels) and falls in love with the fiery little professor **Paul Emanuel**.
:::

::: quote
"Reader, I married him." — *Jane Eyre*, Chapter 38

"I am no bird; and no net ensnares me: I am a free human being with an independent will." — *Jane Eyre*

"Do you think, because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little, I am soulless and heartless? You think wrong!" — *Jane Eyre*
:::

## 60.8 Emily Brontë (1818–1848)

::: writer
**Pen-name:** "**Ellis Bell**" · **Life:** spent almost all her life at Haworth, loving the wild moors; died of tuberculosis at 30 · **Only novel:** ***Wuthering Heights*** (1847) · **Poems:** *No coward soul is mine*, *Remembrance*, *The Old Stoic*; the imaginary kingdom of **Gondal** (with Anne)
:::

::: work
**Wuthering Heights (1847).** A novel of **elemental passion**, set on the Yorkshire moors, with a complex structure of narrators: **Mr. Lockwood**, a tenant, and the housekeeper **Nelly (Ellen) Dean**. **Mr. Earnshaw** of the farmhouse **Wuthering Heights** brings home a dark, wild foundling from Liverpool, **Heathcliff**, who grows up with his children **Catherine** and **Hindley**. Catherine and Heathcliff form a fierce, almost spiritual bond, but after Mr. Earnshaw's death, Hindley degrades Heathcliff to a servant. Catherine marries the refined, gentle **Edgar Linton** of **Thrushcross Grange** for social position; overhearing her say that it would degrade her to marry him, Heathcliff disappears. He returns three years later, rich and mysterious, and takes **revenge**: he gains control of Wuthering Heights from the drunken Hindley, marries Edgar's sister **Isabella** out of spite and ill-treats her. Catherine dies after giving birth to a daughter, **Cathy**. In the second generation, Heathcliff tyrannises over Hindley's son **Hareton**, his own sickly son **Linton**, and young Cathy, whom he forces to marry Linton to gain the Grange. At last, haunted by Catherine's ghost and longing to join her, Heathcliff loses interest in revenge and dies. Cathy and Hareton fall in love and are to marry. Other characters: **Joseph** (the old religious servant), **Frances** (Hindley's wife).
:::

::: quote
"Whatever our souls are made of, his and mine are the same." — Catherine, *Wuthering Heights*

"I am Heathcliff! He's always, always in my mind." — Catherine, *Wuthering Heights*

"No coward soul is mine, / No trembler in the world's storm-troubled sphere." — *No Coward Soul Is Mine*
:::

::: note
**Anne Brontë (1820–1849)**, pen-name "**Acton Bell**", wrote ***Agnes Grey*** (1847), about a governess, and ***The Tenant of Wildfell Hall*** (1848), an early feminist novel about a woman (Helen Graham) who leaves her alcoholic, abusive husband.
:::

## 60.9 George Eliot (Mary Ann Evans, 1819–1880)

::: writer
**Real name:** **Mary Ann (Marian) Evans** · **Pen-name:** **George Eliot** (she used a male name to be taken seriously and to protect her private life) · **Born:** Arbury, Warwickshire · **Life:** a deeply learned intellectual; translated Strauss's *Life of Jesus* and Feuerbach; assistant editor of the *Westminster Review*; lived openly with the married critic **George Henry Lewes** (1854–1878); married John Cross in 1880 · **Titles:** the greatest **intellectual novelist** of the Victorian age; pioneer of the **psychological novel** · **Novels:** *Scenes of Clerical Life* (1858), ***Adam Bede*** (1859), ***The Mill on the Floss*** (1860), ***Silas Marner*** (1861), ***Romola*** (1862–63, set in Renaissance Florence), *Felix Holt, the Radical* (1866), ***Middlemarch*** (1871–72), ***Daniel Deronda*** (1876)
:::

George Eliot's novels, mostly set in the rural Midlands of her youth, combine **realism** with deep **moral and psychological analysis**. She traces how small choices shape character, how individuals are bound to their community, and the importance of **sympathy, duty and self-sacrifice**. Virginia Woolf called *Middlemarch* "one of the few English novels written for grown-up people".

::: work
**Adam Bede (1859).** In the village of Hayslope, the upright carpenter **Adam Bede** loves the pretty, vain dairymaid **Hetty Sorrel**, who is seduced by the young squire **Captain Arthur Donnithorne**. Pregnant and abandoned, Hetty runs away and abandons her newborn baby, who dies; she is convicted of child murder but, through the Methodist preacher **Dinah Morris**'s spiritual help, confesses; her sentence is changed to transportation at the last moment by Arthur's intervention. Adam later marries Dinah. (Not to be confused with the Venerable Bede — Chapter 55.)

**The Mill on the Floss (1860).** The intelligent, passionate **Maggie Tulliver** grows up at Dorlcote Mill on the river Floss, adoring her rigid brother **Tom**. Her father loses the mill to the lawyer **Wakem**; Maggie's secret friendship with Wakem's deformed son **Philip** angers Tom. Later she is drawn to **Stephen Guest**, her cousin Lucy's fiancé, and drifts away with him in a boat; though she refuses to marry him, she is disgraced. In a great flood, Maggie rescues Tom; brother and sister are reconciled just as the boat overturns, and they drown "in an embrace never to be parted". Epitaph: "*In their death they were not divided*."

**Silas Marner (1861).** **Silas Marner**, a linen weaver, falsely accused of theft by his friend **William Dane** and rejected by his chapel community at Lantern Yard, settles in the village of **Raveloe**, where he lives alone and hoards gold. His gold is stolen by **Dunstan Cass**, the wicked son of the Squire. One winter night, a little golden-haired girl toddles into his cottage — the child of **Godfrey Cass** (Dunstan's brother) by a secret, opium-addicted wife who has died in the snow. Silas adopts the child, **Eppie**, and through love for her he is restored to humanity and community. Years later, Dunstan's skeleton and the gold are found; Godfrey confesses and asks to take Eppie back, but she chooses to stay with Silas and marries **Aaron Winthrop**. Other characters: **Dolly Winthrop**, **Nancy Lammeter**.

**Middlemarch: A Study of Provincial Life (1871–72).** A panoramic novel of a Midland town around 1830, with several interwoven stories. The idealistic **Dorothea Brooke** marries the dry, elderly scholar **Mr. Casaubon**, hoping to help in his great work (*The Key to All Mythologies*), but finds him cold and his work futile; after his death she marries his young cousin **Will Ladislaw**, giving up her inheritance. The ambitious young doctor **Tertius Lydgate** marries the beautiful, selfish **Rosamond Vincy** and is ruined by debt and scandal involving the hypocritical banker **Bulstrode**. **Fred Vincy** and **Mary Garth** find happiness through honest work.
:::

::: quote
"It is never too late to be what you might have been." — attributed to George Eliot

"The growing good of the world is partly dependent on unhistoric acts." — *Middlemarch* (Finale)

"In their death they were not divided." — *The Mill on the Floss* (epitaph)
:::

## 60.10 Elizabeth Barrett Browning (1806–1861)

::: writer
**Born:** Durham; grew up at Hope End, Herefordshire · **Life:** an invalid for years, kept at home by her tyrannical father at **50 Wimpole Street**, London; secretly married **Robert Browning** (1846) and eloped to **Italy** (Pisa, then Florence, Casa Guidi), where she died in 1861 · **Reputation:** in her lifetime more famous than Robert; considered for the laureateship in 1850 · **Major works:** ***Sonnets from the Portuguese*** (1850), ***Aurora Leigh*** (1856, verse novel), *The Cry of the Children* (1843, against child labour), *Casa Guidi Windows* (1851), *Poems before Congress*, *The Seraphim*
:::

***Sonnets from the Portuguese*** — 44 **Petrarchan sonnets** recording her love for Robert Browning during their courtship. The title pretended they were translations (Robert called her "my little Portuguese", after her dark complexion). ***Aurora Leigh*** is a nine-book blank-verse "novel in verse" about the growth of a woman poet and the role of women in society. *The Cry of the Children* protested against child labour in mines and factories.

::: quote
"How do I love thee? Let me count the ways. / I love thee to the depth and breadth and height / My soul can reach." — *Sonnets from the Portuguese*, 43

"Do ye hear the children weeping, O my brothers?" — *The Cry of the Children*
:::

## 60.11 Thomas Hardy (1840–1928)

::: writer
**Born:** Higher Bockhampton, near Dorchester, **Dorset** · **Profession:** trained as an **architect** · **Fictional region:** "**Wessex**" — the south-western counties of England (Dorchester = "Casterbridge") · **Titles:** novelist of fate and tragic pessimism; a bridge between the Victorian and Modern ages; poet of the twentieth century · **Gave up novels** after the hostile reception of *Jude the Obscure* (1895) and wrote only poetry thereafter · **Buried:** his ashes in Westminster Abbey, his heart at Stinsford, Dorset · **Novels:** *Desperate Remedies* (1871), *Under the Greenwood Tree* (1872), *A Pair of Blue Eyes* (1873), ***Far from the Madding Crowd*** (1874), ***The Return of the Native*** (1878), *The Trumpet-Major* (1880), ***The Mayor of Casterbridge*** (1886), ***The Woodlanders*** (1887), ***Tess of the d'Urbervilles*** (1891), ***Jude the Obscure*** (1895), *The Well-Beloved* · **Short stories:** *Wessex Tales*, *Life's Little Ironies* · **Poetry:** *Wessex Poems* (1898), *Poems of the Past and the Present*, *Satires of Circumstance*, *Moments of Vision*, ***The Dynasts*** (1904–08, epic drama of the Napoleonic Wars); poems *The Darkling Thrush*, *The Convergence of the Twain* (on the *Titanic*), *Channel Firing*, *In Time of "The Breaking of Nations"*, *Neutral Tones*, *The Man He Killed*
:::

Hardy's novels portray rural Wessex life, its customs and landscapes, in a time of change. His characters struggle against an indifferent or hostile **Fate** (which he called the "**Immanent Will**" or "**President of the Immortals**"), **chance and coincidence**, social convention and their own passions; his vision is **tragic and pessimistic**, though he called himself a "meliorist".

::: work
**Tess of the d'Urbervilles: A Pure Woman (1891).** **Tess Durbeyfield**, a beautiful country girl, is sent by her poor, shiftless parents — who have learned that they descend from the ancient Norman family of **d'Urberville** — to claim kinship with a rich family of that name. Its son, the dissolute **Alec d'Urberville** (whose family merely bought the name), seduces (or rapes) her in **the Chase**; her baby, "Sorrow", dies. At **Talbothays** dairy farm she and the idealistic clergyman's son **Angel Clare** fall in love; on their wedding night, after Angel confesses a past affair, Tess confesses hers — but Angel cannot forgive her and leaves for **Brazil**. Tess falls into poverty and harsh labour at **Flintcomb-Ash**, and finally, to support her family, becomes Alec's mistress. When the repentant Angel returns, Tess, in despair, **murders Alec**. She and Angel have a few days of happiness hiding in the countryside, and she is arrested at **Stonehenge** and hanged at "Wintoncester". Last line: "**'Justice' was done, and the President of the Immortals … had ended his sport with Tess.**" Angel is left with her sister **'Liza-Lu**.

**Far from the Madding Crowd (1874).** (Title from Gray's *Elegy*.) The independent, spirited **Bathsheba Everdene**, who inherits a farm, is loved by three men: the loyal shepherd **Gabriel Oak**, the proud, repressed farmer **William Boldwood**, and the dashing soldier **Sergeant Francis Troy**. She marries Troy, who still loves **Fanny Robin**, whom he abandoned and who dies in the workhouse with their child. Troy disappears, returns, and is shot by Boldwood, who is sent to prison as insane. Bathsheba finally marries Gabriel Oak.

**The Mayor of Casterbridge (1886).** Full title: *The Life and Death of a Man of Character*. In a drunken rage at a fair, the young hay-trusser **Michael Henchard** **sells his wife Susan and baby daughter Elizabeth-Jane to a sailor (Newson) for five guineas**. Repentant, he swears not to drink for 21 years and rises to become the prosperous **Mayor of Casterbridge**. When Susan and Elizabeth-Jane return, his past begins to haunt him. His rivalry with the clever young Scotsman **Donald Farfrae**, whom he first employs, his impulsive temper, the revelation of his past, and a series of misfortunes destroy him: he loses his business, his position and the love of Elizabeth-Jane (who turns out to be Newson's daughter, the real Elizabeth-Jane having died). Henchard dies alone on Egdon Heath, leaving a bitter will ("that no man remember me"). Another key character: **Lucetta Templeman**. "**Character is Fate**" (Novalis) is quoted in the novel.

**The Return of the Native (1878).** On the gloomy **Egdon Heath**, the restless, passionate **Eustacia Vye** marries **Clym Yeobright**, returned from Paris (the "native"), hoping he will take her away; he chooses to stay and becomes a furze-cutter after his eyesight fails. Misunderstandings cause the death of Clym's mother; Eustacia drowns in Shadwater Weir with her former lover **Damon Wildeve**. The reddleman **Diggory Venn** marries Thomasin.

**Jude the Obscure (1895).** **Jude Fawley**, a poor stonemason who dreams of studying at the university of **Christminster** (Oxford), is shut out by class barriers; he is trapped in an unhappy marriage to the coarse **Arabella Donn**, and loves his free-spirited cousin **Sue Bridehead**. They live together unmarried and are persecuted by society; in a terrible scene, Jude's son "**Little Father Time**" hangs Sue's children and himself, leaving a note: "*Done because we are too menny*." Sue returns in penitence to her husband Phillotson; Jude dies in misery. The novel's attack on marriage, religion and education caused an outcry (it was nicknamed "Jude the Obscene").
:::

::: quote
"An aged thrush, frail, gaunt, and small, / In blast-beruffled plume, / Had chosen thus to fling his soul / Upon the growing gloom." — *The Darkling Thrush* (written at the end of 1900)

"Happiness was but the occasional episode in a general drama of pain." — *The Mayor of Casterbridge* (last words)

"Character is Fate, said Novalis." — *The Mayor of Casterbridge*

"Done because we are too menny." — *Jude the Obscure*
:::

## 60.12 John Stuart Mill (1806–1873)

::: writer
**Field:** philosophy, political economy — the leading **liberal** thinker and **utilitarian** of the age · **Education:** famously rigorous home education by his father **James Mill** (Greek at three) · **Career:** East India Company official (1823–1858); MP for Westminster (1865–1868) — introduced a bill for **women's suffrage** · **Wife:** Harriet Taylor (collaborator) · **Major works:** *A System of Logic* (1843), *Principles of Political Economy* (1848), ***On Liberty*** (1859), ***Utilitarianism*** (1861/1863), *Considerations on Representative Government* (1861), ***The Subjection of Women*** (1869), ***Autobiography*** (1873)
:::

In ***On Liberty***, Mill defended the freedom of thought, speech and individual action, stating the "**harm principle**": society may interfere with an individual's liberty only to prevent harm to others. He warned against the "**tyranny of the majority**". In ***Utilitarianism*** he refined Jeremy Bentham's principle of "**the greatest happiness of the greatest number**", distinguishing **higher and lower pleasures**. ***The Subjection of Women*** argued for the full legal and social equality of women. His *Autobiography* describes a mental crisis at age twenty, from which he recovered through reading **Wordsworth**.

::: quote
"It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." — *Utilitarianism*

"The only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others." — *On Liberty*
:::

## 60.13 Samuel Butler (1835–1902)

::: writer
**Life:** grandson of a bishop; rebelled against his clergyman father; sheep farmer in **New Zealand** (1860–64); painter, musician and controversialist (argued that the *Odyssey* was written by a woman) · **Major works:** ***Erewhon*** (1872), *Erewhon Revisited* (1901), ***The Way of All Flesh*** (written 1873–84, published posthumously **1903**), *Life and Habit*, *Notebooks* · **Don't confuse with:** Samuel Butler (1613–1680), author of *Hudibras* (Chapter 58)
:::

***Erewhon*** ("**nowhere**" spelled roughly backwards) is a **satirical utopia** set in an imaginary country (inspired by New Zealand) where values are reversed: illness is punished as a crime, while crime is treated with sympathy as an illness; machines have been destroyed for fear that they will evolve consciousness and enslave man ("**The Book of the Machines**"); and the "**Musical Banks**" (the Church) issue a currency nobody really uses. ***The Way of All Flesh*** is a semi-autobiographical novel attacking **Victorian family life, religion and hypocrisy**: **Ernest Pontifex** is crushed by his tyrannical, pious father **Theobald** and mother **Christina**, becomes a clergyman, goes to prison, marries unhappily, and finally achieves independence with the help of his godfather's legacy (narrated by **Edward Overton**). It influenced Shaw and the anti-Victorian writers of the twentieth century.

## 60.14 Edward FitzGerald (1809–1883)

::: writer
**Fame:** ***The Rubáiyát of Omar Khayyám*** (1859) — a free English verse translation/adaptation of the quatrains (**rubáiyát**) of the Persian poet, mathematician and astronomer **Omar Khayyam** (1048–1131) · **Form:** the "**Rubaiyat stanza**" — iambic pentameter quatrains rhyming *aaba* · **Friend of:** Tennyson, Thackeray, Carlyle
:::

FitzGerald, a shy, wealthy scholar who lived quietly in Suffolk, published the *Rubáiyát* anonymously in 1859; it sold almost nothing until the Pre-Raphaelites (Rossetti, Swinburne) discovered it, and it became one of the most popular poems in English. Its **hedonistic, sceptical carpe diem** philosophy — enjoy the present moment, for life is short and its mysteries unknowable — contrasted with Victorian earnestness. FitzGerald revised it through five editions.

::: quote
"A Book of Verses underneath the Bough, / A Jug of Wine, a Loaf of Bread — and Thou / Beside me singing in the Wilderness — / Oh, Wilderness were Paradise enow!" — *Rubáiyát*

"The Moving Finger writes; and, having writ, / Moves on: nor all your Piety nor Wit / Shall lure it back to cancel half a Line, / Nor all your Tears wash out a Word of it." — *Rubáiyát*
:::

## 60.15 Charles Robert Darwin (1809–1882)

::: writer
**Field:** naturalist, biologist — founder of the theory of **evolution by natural selection** · **Voyage:** naturalist on **HMS *Beagle*** (1831–1836), visiting South America and the **Galápagos Islands** · **Major works:** *The Voyage of the Beagle* (1839), ***On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection*** (**1859**), ***The Descent of Man*** (1871), *The Expression of the Emotions in Man and Animals* (1872) · **Co-discoverer:** Alfred Russel Wallace · **Buried:** Westminster Abbey
:::

***On the Origin of Species*** argued that species are not fixed but have **evolved** over vast periods from common ancestors through **natural selection**: individuals with variations better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more ("**the survival of the fittest**" — a phrase actually coined by **Herbert Spencer** and adopted by Darwin in later editions). The theory challenged the literal biblical account of creation and deepened the **Victorian crisis of faith**, reflected in Tennyson's *In Memoriam*, Arnold's *Dover Beach* and Hardy's novels. ***The Descent of Man*** applied evolution to human beings. **Thomas Henry Huxley** ("Darwin's bulldog") defended the theory against Bishop Samuel Wilberforce at Oxford (1860).

## 60.16 Oscar Wilde (1854–1900)

::: writer
**Full name:** Oscar Fingal O'Flahertie Wills Wilde · **Born:** Dublin · **Education:** Trinity College Dublin; Magdalen College, Oxford (Newdigate Prize for *Ravenna*) · **Movement:** leader of **Aestheticism** ("**art for art's sake**"), influenced by Walter Pater · **Downfall:** convicted of "gross indecency" (1895) after a libel case against the Marquess of Queensberry, father of Lord Alfred Douglas; two years' hard labour in **Reading Gaol** · **Died:** in poverty in **Paris** (1900) · **Major works:** novel ***The Picture of Dorian Gray*** (1890/91); comedies *Lady Windermere's Fan* (1892), *A Woman of No Importance* (1893), ***An Ideal Husband*** (1895), ***The Importance of Being Earnest*** (1895); tragedy *Salomé* (written in French, 1891); fairy tales *The Happy Prince and Other Tales* (1888 — *The Happy Prince*, *The Nightingale and the Rose*, *The Selfish Giant*), *A House of Pomegranates*; ***The Ballad of Reading Gaol*** (1898); ***De Profundis*** (prison letter to Douglas, published 1905); essays *The Decay of Lying*, *The Critic as Artist*, *The Soul of Man under Socialism*
:::

Wilde was famous for his wit, **epigrams and paradoxes**, flamboyant dress and conversation. His comedies satirise the hypocrisy and triviality of Victorian high society with brilliant dialogue.

::: work
**The Picture of Dorian Gray (1890/91).** The beautiful young **Dorian Gray** has his portrait painted by the artist **Basil Hallward**. Influenced by the cynical, witty **Lord Henry Wotton**, who preaches a philosophy of pleasure and youth, Dorian wishes that the portrait might grow old instead of him. The wish comes true: he stays young and handsome while the hidden portrait records every sin and cruelty — first his heartless rejection of the actress **Sibyl Vane**, who kills herself. Over eighteen years of secret vice, Dorian murders Basil and drives others to ruin. At last, trying to destroy the hideous portrait with a knife, he kills himself; the servants find the portrait restored to its original beauty and a withered, loathsome old man dead on the floor. Other characters: **James Vane**, **Alan Campbell**.

**The Importance of Being Earnest: A Trivial Comedy for Serious People (1895).** Two young gentlemen lead double lives: **John (Jack) Worthing**, a respectable guardian in the country, calls himself "**Ernest**" in town, pretending to visit a wicked younger brother of that name; **Algernon Moncrieff** invents an invalid friend, "**Bunbury**", in the country, to escape social duties ("Bunburying"). Jack loves Algernon's cousin **Gwendolen Fairfax**, who wants to marry only a man named Ernest; Algernon, pretending to be "Ernest", woos Jack's young ward **Cecily Cardew**. Gwendolen's formidable mother **Lady Bracknell** objects because Jack was found as a baby **in a handbag** at **Victoria Station**. The governess **Miss Prism** turns out to have left him there by mistake in place of her novel manuscript; Jack is really Algernon's elder brother, and his name really is **Ernest**. Three couples are united (including Miss Prism and Canon **Chasuble**).
:::
