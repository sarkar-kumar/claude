## 56.1 Background (1066–1500)

The **Norman Conquest of 1066**, when William, Duke of Normandy, defeated King Harold at the **Battle of Hastings**, changed English language and literature profoundly. For about three centuries **Norman French** was the language of the court, law and aristocracy, and **Latin** the language of the Church and learning; English survived as the speech of ordinary people. It lost most of its inflections and absorbed thousands of French words (*government, justice, beef, mutton, court, crown, prayer, beauty*). By the fourteenth century — helped by the Hundred Years' War with France and the rise of a middle class — **English re-emerged** as the national literary language.

The period is usually divided as follows:

| Sub-period | Dates | Features |
|---|---|---|
| **Anglo-Norman Period** | 1066–1340 | French romances, Latin chronicles (Geoffrey of Monmouth's *History of the Kings of Britain*, c. 1136, source of Arthurian legend), early English works (*The Owl and the Nightingale*, Layamon's *Brut*, *Ancrene Wisse*) |
| **Age of Chaucer** | 1340–1400 | the great revival: Chaucer, Langland, Gower, the *Pearl/Gawain*-poet, Wycliffe |
| **Barren / Dark Age (Fifteenth Century)** | 1400–1500 | imitators of Chaucer (Lydgate, Hoccleve), the **Scottish Chaucerians** (Henryson, Dunbar, Gavin Douglas), popular ballads, miracle and morality plays, Caxton's press (1476), Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur* (1485) |

### 56.1.1 Literary Forms of the Period

- **Romance** — tales of knights, chivalry, courtly love and adventure (Arthurian cycle; *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*).
- **Allegory and dream-vision** — *Piers Plowman*, *The Romaunt of the Rose*, *Pearl*.
- **Fabliaux** — short comic, often bawdy tales (some of Chaucer's tales).
- **Ballads** — anonymous narrative songs (*Robin Hood* ballads, *Sir Patrick Spens*, *Chevy Chase*).
- **Drama** — **Mystery plays** (Bible stories) and **Miracle plays** (lives of saints) performed by town guilds in cycles (York, Chester, Wakefield, N-Town); **Morality plays** with allegorical characters (*Everyman*, c. 1495; *The Castle of Perseverance*); **Interludes**.

## 56.2 Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1343–1400)

::: writer
**Born:** c. 1343, London · **Died:** 25 October 1400 · **Buried:** Westminster Abbey — the first poet buried in what became **Poets' Corner** · **Titles:** **Father of English Poetry**; Father of English Literature; "the first finder of our fair language" (Hoccleve); "a well of English undefiled" (Spenser); "the morning star of song" (Tennyson) · **Masterpiece:** *The Canterbury Tales*
:::

Chaucer was the son of a London wine merchant. He served as a page, soldier (captured in France and ransomed, 1360), diplomat (missions to Italy, where he discovered Dante, Petrarch and Boccaccio), Controller of Customs for the port of London, and Clerk of the King's Works. His career gave him a wide knowledge of every class of society, which shines through his poetry.

He chose to write in the **East Midland dialect of English** (the London speech), at a time when French and Latin were more prestigious, and so helped make it the basis of standard English. He introduced the **rhyme royal** stanza (seven lines, *ababbcc*) and the **heroic couplet** (rhyming iambic pentameter couplets) into English.

### 56.2.1 Three Periods of Chaucer's Work

| Period | Influence | Works |
|---|---|---|
| **French period** (to c. 1372) | French allegory | *The Romaunt of the Rose* (translation), *The Book of the Duchess* (1369, elegy on Blanche, wife of John of Gaunt) |
| **Italian period** (c. 1372–1385) | Dante, Petrarch, Boccaccio | *The House of Fame*, *The Parliament of Fowls* (Valentine's Day poem, birds choosing mates), ***Troilus and Criseyde*** (his greatest complete poem, from Boccaccio's *Il Filostrato*), *The Legend of Good Women* |
| **English period** (c. 1386–1400) | mature, original | ***The Canterbury Tales*** |

Prose: *A Treatise on the Astrolabe* (written for his son "little Lewis"), translation of Boethius (*Boece*).

### 56.2.2 The Canterbury Tales (c. 1387–1400)

::: work
**Frame story.** In April, the narrator (Chaucer himself) stays at the **Tabard Inn in Southwark**, London, where he joins a company of **twenty-nine pilgrims** travelling to the shrine of **St Thomas Becket at Canterbury**. The innkeeper, **Harry Bailly (the Host)**, proposes a storytelling contest: each pilgrim will tell **two tales on the way and two on the return**; the best story-teller will get a free supper at the Tabard on their return. The plan (about 120 tales) was never completed: Chaucer wrote only **24 tales** (two unfinished, plus some fragments), and the pilgrims never arrive at Canterbury in the text.

**The General Prologue** (858 lines) is a gallery of vivid portraits of medieval society — the **Knight** ("a verray, parfit gentil knyght"), his son the **Squire**, the **Yeoman**, the **Prioress** (Madame Eglantine, with her French "after the scole of Stratford atte Bowe"), the **Monk**, the **Friar**, the **Merchant**, the **Clerk** of Oxford, the **Sergeant of Law**, the **Franklin**, the **Guildsmen**, the **Cook**, the **Shipman**, the **Doctor of Physic**, the **Wife of Bath** (married five times), the **Parson** (the ideal priest), the **Plowman**, the **Miller**, the **Manciple**, the **Reeve**, the **Summoner**, the **Pardoner** (selling false relics), and the **Host**.

**Some important tales:**

- **The Knight's Tale** — two Theban cousins, **Palamon and Arcite**, fall in love with **Emily**; a chivalric romance from Boccaccio.
- **The Miller's Tale** — a bawdy fabliau about the carpenter John, his young wife Alisoun, and the clerk Nicholas.
- **The Wife of Bath's Tale** — a knight must discover "what women most desire" (answer: **sovereignty** over their husbands); its long Prologue is a famous comic defence of women.
- **The Pardoner's Tale** — three young rioters set out to kill **Death**; an old man directs them to a tree where they find gold, and they kill one another out of greed. Moral: *Radix malorum est cupiditas* ("**Greed is the root of all evil**").
- **The Nun's Priest's Tale** — a beast fable of the cock **Chauntecleer**, his wife **Pertelote** and the fox **Daun Russell**; a mock-heroic about flattery and dreams.
- **The Clerk's Tale** — the patient **Griselda**, tested cruelly by her husband Walter.
- **The Franklin's Tale** — Dorigen, Arveragus and Aurelius; a tale of "trouthe" (keeping promises) and generosity.
- **The Prioress's Tale**, **The Monk's Tale** (tragedies of fallen great men), **The Parson's Tale** (a prose sermon on penitence), **The Tale of Sir Thopas** (told by Chaucer the pilgrim and interrupted by the Host for being boring).
:::

::: quote
"Whan that Aprille with his shoures soote / The droghte of March hath perced to the roote…" — *The Canterbury Tales*, General Prologue (opening lines)

"He was a verray, parfit gentil knyght." — General Prologue (on the Knight)

"And gladly wolde he lerne and gladly teche." — General Prologue (on the Clerk)

"Radix malorum est cupiditas." — The Pardoner's Tale

"Love is noght oold as whan that it is newe." — The Clerk's Tale
:::

::: note
**Famous critical remarks:** **Dryden** called Chaucer "the father of English poetry" and said of the Tales, "**Here is God's plenty.**" **Matthew Arnold** said Chaucer lacked "high seriousness". **Spenser** called him "the well of English undefiled".
:::

## 56.3 William Langland (c. 1332–c. 1386)

::: writer
**Work:** ***Piers Plowman*** (*The Vision of William concerning Piers the Plowman*) · **Form:** alliterative dream-vision allegory · **Versions:** A, B and C texts (c. 1370–1390)
:::

Little is known of Langland; he was probably from the West Midlands (the poem opens in the **Malvern Hills**) and lived in London as a poor cleric. ***Piers Plowman*** is written in the old **alliterative verse** without rhyme — part of the **Alliterative Revival** — unlike Chaucer's rhymed, French-influenced verse.

::: work
**Summary.** The dreamer, **Will**, falls asleep on a May morning on the Malvern Hills and sees a **"fair field full of folk"** — all classes of society — between the **Tower of Truth** (heaven) and the **Dungeon** (hell). **Holy Church** explains the vision. Will sees the corrupting **Lady Meed** (reward/bribery) and the confession of the **Seven Deadly Sins**. **Piers the Plowman**, an honest labourer, appears as the guide to Truth and later becomes a Christ-like figure. In later visions the dreamer seeks **Do-well, Do-better and Do-best**. The poem attacks the corruption of the Church and society and calls for honest work, charity and spiritual renewal.

**Characters:** Will (the dreamer), Piers Plowman, Holy Church, Lady Meed, Conscience, Reason, the Seven Deadly Sins, Do-well, Do-better, Do-best.
:::

::: quote
"In a somer seson, whan softe was the sonne…" / "A fair feeld ful of folk fond I ther bitwene." — *Piers Plowman* (Prologue)
:::

## 56.4 John Wycliffe (c. 1328–1384)

::: writer
**Titles:** **Morning Star of the Reformation**; Father of English Prose (Middle English) · **Achievement:** inspired the **first complete translation of the Bible into English** (c. 1382–1395) · **Followers:** the **Lollards**
:::

**John Wycliffe** was an Oxford theologian and reformer who criticised the wealth and corruption of the Church, papal authority, the sale of indulgences and the doctrine of transubstantiation. He believed every Christian should be able to read the Bible in his own language, and he inspired the **Wycliffite Bible**, translated from the Latin Vulgate by Wycliffe and his followers (notably **Nicholas of Hereford** and **John Purvey**). His followers, called **Lollards**, were persecuted. Long after his death, the Council of Constance (1415) declared him a heretic, and his remains were dug up and burned (1428). His ideas influenced **Jan Hus** and, later, the Protestant Reformation — hence the title "Morning Star of the Reformation".

## 56.5 Sir Thomas Malory (c. 1415–1471)

::: writer
**Work:** ***Le Morte d'Arthur*** ("The Death of Arthur") · **Completed:** 1469–70, in prison · **Printed:** by **William Caxton, 1485** · **Language:** English prose (despite the French title)
:::

**Sir Thomas Malory**, a knight of Warwickshire, spent many years in prison on various charges, where he compiled ***Le Morte d'Arthur***, the most famous English version of the **Arthurian legends**, drawn mainly from French romances. Caxton printed it in 1485 and divided it into **21 books**; a manuscript found at Winchester in 1934 shows Malory's original arrangement into eight tales.

::: work
**Summary.** The book tells the whole story of **King Arthur**: his birth to **Uther Pendragon** and **Igraine** with the help of the magician **Merlin**; his proof of kingship by drawing the **sword from the stone**; his receiving **Excalibur** from the **Lady of the Lake**; his marriage to **Guinevere** and the founding of the fellowship of the **Round Table** at **Camelot**. It narrates the adventures of the knights — **Lancelot**, **Tristram** and **Isolde**, **Gareth**, **Gawain**, **Percival** — and the **Quest for the Holy Grail**, achieved by the pure knight **Sir Galahad** (son of Lancelot). The fellowship breaks apart when the love of **Lancelot and Guinevere** is exposed by **Agravain** and **Mordred**; civil war follows, and at the **Battle of Camlann** Arthur kills his treacherous son/nephew **Mordred** but is mortally wounded. **Sir Bedivere** throws Excalibur back into the lake, and Arthur is carried by queens in a barge to **Avalon**. Guinevere becomes a nun and Lancelot a hermit.

**Characters:** Arthur, Guinevere, Lancelot, Merlin, Morgan le Fay, Mordred, Gawain, Galahad, Percival, Tristram, Isolde (La Beale Isoud), Bedivere, Kay, the Lady of the Lake, Uther Pendragon.
:::

::: quote
"Hic iacet Arthurus, rex quondam rexque futurus." — "Here lies Arthur, the once and future king." (*Le Morte d'Arthur*)
:::

## 56.6 Dante Alighieri (1265–1321)

::: writer
**Nationality:** Italian (Florence) · **Titles:** Father of the Italian language; "the supreme poet" (*il Sommo Poeta*) · **Masterpiece:** ***La Divina Commedia*** (*The Divine Comedy*, c. 1308–1321) · **Other works:** *La Vita Nuova* (*The New Life*), *De vulgari eloquentia*, *Convivio*, *De Monarchia*
:::

Dante is not an English writer, but he is included in the English syllabus because of his immense influence on Chaucer, Milton and modern poets such as T. S. Eliot. Exiled from Florence in 1302 for political reasons, he wrote the *Divine Comedy* in **Italian** (the Tuscan vernacular) rather than Latin, helping to establish Italian as a literary language. He died in exile at **Ravenna**.

::: work
**The Divine Comedy — Summary.** The poem, written in **terza rima** (*aba bcb cdc…*) and divided into **three parts (canticles)** — **Inferno** (Hell), **Purgatorio** (Purgatory) and **Paradiso** (Paradise) — with **100 cantos** in all, describes Dante's imaginary journey through the afterlife during Easter week of 1300.

Lost "in a dark wood" at the midpoint of his life, Dante is rescued by the Roman poet **Virgil**, who guides him through the **nine circles of Hell**, where sinners are punished according to their sins (with Satan frozen at the centre), and up the **mountain of Purgatory**, where souls are purified. At the summit, **Beatrice** — the idealised woman Dante loved in his youth (Beatrice Portinari) — replaces Virgil and leads him through the **nine heavenly spheres of Paradise**, until he finally beholds the vision of God, "the love that moves the sun and the other stars".

**Characters:** Dante (pilgrim), Virgil (guide), Beatrice, St Bernard (final guide), Paolo and Francesca, Ulysses, Count Ugolino, Lucifer/Satan, and many historical figures.
:::

::: quote
"Nel mezzo del cammin di nostra vita / mi ritrovai per una selva oscura." — "Midway upon the journey of our life I found myself within a dark wood." (*Inferno*, Canto I)

"Lasciate ogne speranza, voi ch'intrate." — "Abandon all hope, ye who enter here." (Inscription over the gate of Hell, *Inferno*, Canto III)

"L'amor che move il sole e l'altre stelle." — "The love that moves the sun and the other stars." (*Paradiso*, last line)
:::

## 56.7 Other Writers and Works of the Middle English Period

| Writer / Work | Notes |
|---|---|
| **John Gower** (c. 1330–1408) | Chaucer's friend; called "**moral Gower**" by Chaucer; wrote in three languages: *Speculum Meditantis* (French), *Vox Clamantis* (Latin), ***Confessio Amantis*** (English) |
| **The *Pearl*-poet / *Gawain*-poet** (anonymous, late 14th c.) | ***Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*** — Arthurian romance: the Green Knight's beheading challenge at Camelot, Gawain's journey, the temptations by Lady Bertilak, the green girdle; also ***Pearl*** (elegiac dream-vision of a father for his dead daughter), *Patience*, *Cleanness* |
| **Layamon** (c. 1200) | *Brut* — first English account of King Arthur |
| ***The Owl and the Nightingale*** (c. 1200) | debate poem |
| **Sir John Mandeville** | *The Travels of Sir John Mandeville* (largely fictional travel book) |
| **John Barbour** (Scottish) | *The Bruce* (1375), epic on Robert the Bruce |
| **John Lydgate**, **Thomas Hoccleve** | English Chaucerians (15th c.) |
| **Robert Henryson**, **William Dunbar**, **Gavin Douglas** | **Scottish Chaucerians**; Henryson's *Testament of Cresseid*; Dunbar's *Lament for the Makaris*; Douglas's translation of Virgil's *Aeneid* (first complete British translation) |
| ***Everyman*** (c. 1495) | the best-known **morality play**: Death summons Everyman; only Good Deeds goes with him to the grave |
| **Giovanni Boccaccio** (Italian, 1313–1375) | ***The Decameron*** (100 tales told by 10 young people fleeing the plague) — model for Chaucer's frame story; *Il Filostrato* (source of *Troilus*) |
| **Francesco Petrarca (Petrarch)** (Italian, 1304–1374) | Father of Humanism; love sonnets to **Laura** (*Canzoniere*) — the **Petrarchan sonnet** |
| **William Caxton** (c. 1422–1491) | first English printer (Westminster, 1476); printed *The Canterbury Tales* and *Le Morte d'Arthur* |

::: gre
**SAT / GRE Corner:** Many GRE words come from the medieval world: *chivalrous, courtly, allegory, pilgrimage, pardon, mystery, morality, romance, quest, grail* (anything eagerly sought — "the holy grail of physics"), *Byzantine*, *Dantesque* (grim, infernal), *Canterbury* ("tales"), *Quixotic* (from the later Spanish romance-parody). Understanding allegory (Langland) and dream-vision (Dante) also helps in reading literary passages.
