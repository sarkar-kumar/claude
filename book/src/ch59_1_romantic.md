## 59.1 Background (1798–1832)

The **Romantic Period** is usually dated from the publication of ***Lyrical Ballads*** by **Wordsworth and Coleridge (1798)** to the **Reform Act** and the death of **Sir Walter Scott (1832)**. It was an age of revolution: the **American Revolution (1776)**, the **French Revolution (1789)** with its ideals of liberty, equality and fraternity, the Napoleonic Wars (ending at **Waterloo, 1815**), and the **Industrial Revolution**, which drew people from the countryside into crowded factory towns.

### 59.1.1 Characteristics of Romanticism

Romanticism was a **reaction against Neo-Classicism** — against its reason, rules, artificial diction and town life. Its main features:

1. **Imagination over reason** — imagination is the highest creative and visionary faculty (Coleridge's "primary" and "secondary" imagination; Blake; Keats's "truth of imagination").
2. **Emotion and spontaneity** — "Poetry is the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings" (Wordsworth).
3. **Love of nature** — nature as a source of beauty, healing, moral teaching and spiritual presence (*pantheism*).
4. **Interest in the common man and rustic life**, and in childhood and innocence.
5. **Individualism and subjectivity** — the poet's own feelings and experiences (the "egotistical sublime").
6. **Revolt and liberty** — political and personal freedom (Byron, Shelley).
7. **Supernaturalism and the exotic** (Coleridge's *Ancient Mariner*, *Kubla Khan*), **medievalism** (Keats, Scott) and **Hellenism** (Keats, Shelley).
8. **Melancholy, longing and escape** (Keats's odes).
9. **Simple, natural language** — "the real language of men" — and a revival of the **ballad**, **sonnet**, **ode** and **lyric**; rejection of the heroic couplet.

### 59.1.2 The Two Generations

| Generation | Poets | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **First generation (older)** | **Blake**, **Wordsworth**, **Coleridge**, **Southey** | Wordsworth, Coleridge and Southey are called the **Lake Poets** (they lived in the Lake District of Cumberland/Westmorland) |
| **Second generation (younger)** | **Byron**, **Shelley**, **Keats** | all died young and abroad — Keats (25) in Rome, Shelley (29) drowned off Italy, Byron (36) in Greece |

## 59.2 Pioneers (Precursors) of Romanticism

The **Pre-Romantic** or **Transitional poets** of the eighteenth century anticipated the Romantic spirit:

| Pioneer | Romantic features |
|---|---|
| **James Thomson** (*The Seasons*, 1726–30) | detailed nature description in blank verse |
| **Thomas Gray** (*Elegy*, 1751; *The Bard*) | melancholy, sympathy for the humble poor, interest in Celtic and Norse past |
| **William Collins** (*Ode to Evening*) | imagination, personification, evening mood |
| **Edward Young** and the **Graveyard poets** (*Night Thoughts*; Robert Blair's *The Grave*) | melancholy, death, night |
| **James Macpherson** (*Ossian*, 1760–65) | wild Celtic past, primitive grandeur |
| **Thomas Percy** (*Reliques of Ancient English Poetry*, 1765) | revival of the **ballad**, medieval interest |
| **Thomas Chatterton** (the "Rowley" poems) | medievalism; the doomed young poet |
| **William Cowper** (*The Task*, 1785) | love of countryside, simple life, humanitarianism |
| **George Crabbe** (*The Village*, 1783) | realistic picture of rural poverty |
| **Robert Burns** (1786) | spontaneity, dialect, love, nature, democracy ("A man's a man for a' that") |
| **William Blake** (*Songs*, 1789–94) | imagination, vision, revolt, childhood |
| **Horace Walpole** and **Ann Radcliffe** | the Gothic — mystery, terror, the supernatural |
| **Rousseau** (French) | nature, feeling, the individual, liberty |

## 59.3 William Wordsworth (1770–1850)

::: writer
**Born:** Cockermouth, Cumberland (Lake District) · **Education:** Hawkshead Grammar School; St John's College, Cambridge · **Titles:** **Leader of the Romantic Movement**; **Poet of Nature**; "Worshipper of Nature"; a **Lake Poet**; **Poet Laureate (1843–1850)**, succeeding Southey · **Sister:** **Dorothy Wordsworth** (diarist, his companion) · **Wife:** Mary Hutchinson · **Homes:** Dove Cottage and Rydal Mount, Grasmere · **Major works:** ***Lyrical Ballads*** (1798, with Coleridge; Preface 1800/1802), ***The Prelude*** (completed 1805; published posthumously 1850), *Poems in Two Volumes* (1807), ***The Excursion*** (1814)
:::

Wordsworth lost both parents early. He visited **France** during the Revolution (1790–92), was inspired by its ideals and had a daughter, Caroline, with **Annette Vallon**. Disillusioned by the Terror and the war, he suffered a moral crisis, from which he recovered through **nature** and the support of his sister Dorothy and Coleridge. With Coleridge he published ***Lyrical Ballads*** (1798), which began the Romantic movement.

**The Preface to *Lyrical Ballads* (1800; enlarged 1802)** is his poetic manifesto. Its main points:

- Poetry should deal with "**incidents and situations from common life**", especially **humble and rustic life**, in "**a selection of language really used by men**".
- "**All good poetry is the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings**: it takes its origin from **emotion recollected in tranquillity**."
- The poet is "**a man speaking to men**", with more sensibility and imagination than others.
- Rejection of the artificial "poetic diction" of the eighteenth century.

**Major poems:**

- ***Lines Composed a Few Miles above Tintern Abbey*** (1798) — on revisiting the Wye valley after five years: the stages of his love of nature and its moral power.
- ***Ode: Intimations of Immortality from Recollections of Early Childhood*** (1807) — the child's heavenly vision fades as he grows; the doctrine of pre-existence; consolation in "the philosophic mind".
- ***The Prelude, or Growth of a Poet's Mind*** — a long autobiographical poem in blank verse (14 books, 1850 version), addressed to Coleridge.
- ***The Solitary Reaper***, ***I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud*** (*Daffodils*), ***The Daffodils***, ***Lucy poems*** (*She dwelt among the untrodden ways*, *Strange fits of passion*, *A slumber did my spirit seal*, *Three years she grew*), *Michael*, *Resolution and Independence*, *Ode to Duty*, *Elegiac Stanzas* (Peele Castle), *My Heart Leaps Up*, *We Are Seven*, *Expostulation and Reply*, *The Tables Turned*, *Lines Written in Early Spring*, *Simon Lee*, *The Idiot Boy*, *To the Cuckoo*, *The World Is Too Much with Us*, *Composed upon Westminster Bridge*, *London, 1802* (sonnet to Milton), *It is a beauteous evening*, *Nuns fret not*, *The Excursion*.

::: work
**I Wandered Lonely as a Cloud (Daffodils).** Inspired by a walk with Dorothy near **Ullswater** (April 1802). The poet, wandering alone, suddenly sees "a host of golden daffodils" dancing beside the lake. At the time he hardly realises their value, but later, when he lies "in vacant or in pensive mood", the memory flashes upon "that inward eye / Which is the bliss of solitude", and his heart dances with the daffodils — illustrating "emotion recollected in tranquillity".

**The Solitary Reaper.** The poet sees a Highland girl reaping and singing alone in a field. He cannot understand her Gaelic song, but its melancholy beauty — more welcome than the nightingale's or the cuckoo's — stays in his heart "long after it was heard no more".

**Tintern Abbey.** The poet describes three stages of his relationship with nature: the "glad animal movements" of boyhood, the passionate, sensuous love of youth, and the mature awareness of "the still, sad music of humanity" and of "a presence" that "rolls through all things". Nature is "the anchor of my purest thoughts". He ends by blessing his sister Dorothy.
:::

::: quote
"My heart leaps up when I behold / A rainbow in the sky… / The Child is father of the Man." — *My Heart Leaps Up*

## 59.4 Samuel Taylor Coleridge (1772–1834)

::: writer
**Born:** Ottery St Mary, Devon · **Education:** Christ's Hospital (with Charles Lamb); Jesus College, Cambridge · **Titles:** poet of the **supernatural**; "the **Sage of Highgate**"; a **Lake Poet**; one of the greatest English critics · **Addiction:** opium (laudanum) · **Major poems:** ***The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*** (1798), ***Kubla Khan*** (written 1797, published 1816), ***Christabel*** (unfinished, published 1816), *Frost at Midnight*, *Dejection: An Ode*, *This Lime-Tree Bower My Prison*, *The Eolian Harp* · **Prose:** ***Biographia Literaria*** (1817), *Lectures on Shakespeare*, *Table Talk*, *Aids to Reflection*
:::

Coleridge, a brilliant talker and thinker, planned with **Robert Southey** a utopian community ("**Pantisocracy**") on the banks of the Susquehanna in America (never realised). His friendship with Wordsworth (1797–98) produced *Lyrical Ballads*; according to the plan, Coleridge was to write about **supernatural** subjects made believable, while Wordsworth would give the charm of novelty to everyday things. In ***Biographia Literaria*** he distinguished **Imagination** (the creative, unifying power — "**esemplastic**") from **Fancy** (a mechanical power of association), and defined the reader's "**willing suspension of disbelief**" for the moment, "which constitutes poetic faith".

::: work
**The Rime of the Ancient Mariner (1798).** An old sailor stops one of three guests on his way to a wedding feast and, holding him with his "glittering eye", tells his story. His ship was driven by storms to the icy South Pole; an **albatross** appeared and was welcomed as a good omen, guiding them out of the ice. Without reason, the Mariner **shot the albatross with his crossbow**. The ship was becalmed under a burning sun ("*Water, water, every where, / Nor any drop to drink*"); the sailors hung the dead albatross round his neck. A ghost-ship appeared with **Death** and **Life-in-Death** playing dice; Life-in-Death won the Mariner, and his two hundred shipmates dropped dead. Alone, he suffered for seven days, until, seeing the beauty of the **water-snakes** in the moonlight, he "**blessed them unaware**"; the albatross fell from his neck into the sea. Angelic spirits drove the ship home, where it sank, and a holy **Hermit** rescued him. As penance, he must wander from land to land and tell his tale. The Wedding-Guest goes away "a sadder and a wiser man". Moral: love all God's creatures.

**Kubla Khan, or A Vision in a Dream (1797/1816).** Coleridge said he composed 200–300 lines in an **opium dream** after reading about the palace of the Mongol emperor **Kublai Khan** in *Purchas his Pilgrimage*; on waking he began to write but was interrupted by "**a person on business from Porlock**", and the rest of the vision vanished — leaving a famous fragment of 54 lines about the "stately pleasure-dome" in **Xanadu**, the sacred river **Alph**, caverns "measureless to man", and the inspired poet with "flashing eyes" and "floating hair".

**Christabel.** An unfinished Gothic poem: the innocent **Christabel** finds the mysterious lady **Geraldine** in the forest and brings her to her father **Sir Leoline**'s castle; Geraldine, a serpent-like enchantress, casts an evil spell on her.
:::

::: quote
"Water, water, every where, / Nor any drop to drink." — *The Rime of the Ancient Mariner*

"He prayeth best, who loveth best / All things both great and small." — *The Ancient Mariner*

"A sadder and a wiser man, / He rose the morrow morn." — *The Ancient Mariner*

"In Xanadu did Kubla Khan / A stately pleasure-dome decree: / Where Alph, the sacred river, ran / Through caverns measureless to man / Down to a sunless sea." — *Kubla Khan*

"That willing suspension of disbelief for the moment, which constitutes poetic faith." — *Biographia Literaria*, Ch. XIV

"Prose = words in their best order; poetry = the best words in the best order." — *Table Talk*
:::

## 59.5 Jane Austen (1775–1817)

::: writer
**Born:** Steventon, Hampshire (daughter of a clergyman, the Rev. George Austen) · **Lived:** Steventon, Bath, Southampton, **Chawton** · **Died:** Winchester, 1817; buried in Winchester Cathedral · **Genre:** **novel of manners** — domestic comedy of middle-class and gentry life · **Published anonymously** ("By a Lady") · **Novels:** ***Sense and Sensibility*** (1811), ***Pride and Prejudice*** (1813), ***Mansfield Park*** (1814), ***Emma*** (1815), ***Northanger Abbey*** and ***Persuasion*** (both 1817/1818, posthumous); unfinished *Sanditon*, *The Watsons*; *Lady Susan*
:::

Though she lived during the Romantic Age, Austen is a **realist and ironist** with a classical sense of proportion. She described her art as working on "**a little bit (two inches wide) of ivory**", and her subject as "**three or four families in a country village**". Her novels deal with **love, courtship, marriage, money and social class** among the English gentry, and with the moral education of heroines who learn to judge themselves and others truly. She is admired for her **irony**, perfect construction, sharp dialogue and use of **free indirect discourse**. Sir Walter Scott praised her "exquisite touch".

::: work
**Pride and Prejudice (1813).** Originally titled *First Impressions*. **Mr. and Mrs. Bennet** of **Longbourn** have **five daughters** and no son, so their estate is entailed on a cousin, the pompous clergyman **Mr. Collins**. Mrs. Bennet's only aim is to marry her daughters well. The rich, amiable **Mr. Bingley** rents nearby **Netherfield** and falls in love with the eldest, gentle **Jane**. His proud friend **Fitzwilliam Darcy** offends the witty second daughter, **Elizabeth**, by refusing to dance with her ("*She is tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me*"). Elizabeth's **prejudice** against Darcy grows when the charming officer **George Wickham** falsely tells her Darcy cheated him. She refuses Mr. Collins, who then marries her friend **Charlotte Lucas**. Darcy, against his pride, proposes to Elizabeth; she angrily refuses him, accusing him of separating Jane and Bingley and wronging Wickham. Darcy's **letter** explains the truth: Wickham is a scoundrel who tried to elope with Darcy's young sister **Georgiana**. Visiting Darcy's estate **Pemberley**, Elizabeth sees his true goodness. When her youngest sister **Lydia** elopes with Wickham, Darcy secretly arranges and pays for their marriage, saving the family's reputation. Bingley returns to Jane. Darcy's aunt, the arrogant **Lady Catherine de Bourgh**, tries to stop his marriage to Elizabeth, but only hastens it. Darcy and Elizabeth, having overcome his **pride** and her **prejudice**, marry.

**Characters:** Elizabeth Bennet, Fitzwilliam Darcy, Jane Bennet, Charles Bingley, Mr. and Mrs. Bennet, Mary, Kitty and Lydia Bennet, George Wickham, Mr. Collins, Charlotte Lucas, Lady Catherine de Bourgh, Georgiana Darcy, Caroline Bingley, Mr. and Mrs. Gardiner.
:::

::: work
**Sense and Sensibility (1811).** Two sisters represent **sense** (prudence, self-control) — **Elinor Dashwood** — and **sensibility** (emotion, romance) — **Marianne Dashwood**. Marianne loves the dashing **John Willoughby**, who deserts her for a rich heiress; she nearly dies of grief and finally marries the steady **Colonel Brandon**. Elinor quietly loves **Edward Ferrars**, who is secretly engaged to the scheming **Lucy Steele**; when Lucy marries Edward's brother Robert, Edward marries Elinor.

**Emma (1815).** **Emma Woodhouse**, "handsome, clever, and rich", lives with her valetudinarian father at **Hartfield** in the village of **Highbury**. She amuses herself by matchmaking, misleading her humble friend **Harriet Smith** about the vicar **Mr. Elton** and others. After many misunderstandings — involving **Frank Churchill** and **Jane Fairfax** — she realises her own errors and her love for the wise, older **Mr. Knightley**, whom she marries.

**Mansfield Park (1814).** The poor, modest **Fanny Price** is brought up by her rich relatives, the Bertrams of Mansfield Park; she secretly loves her cousin **Edmund Bertram**, resists the charming **Henry Crawford**, and finally marries Edmund after the worldly Crawfords are exposed.

**Northanger Abbey (1817).** A parody of **Gothic novels**: **Catherine Morland**, who has read too many Gothic romances (like *The Mysteries of Udolpho*), visits **Northanger Abbey** and imagines that **General Tilney** murdered his wife; she learns the difference between fiction and life and marries **Henry Tilney**.

**Persuasion (1817).** Eight years before the novel opens, **Anne Elliot** was persuaded by her friend **Lady Russell** to break her engagement to the poor naval officer **Frederick Wentworth**. Now a rich captain, Wentworth returns; after misunderstandings (and Louisa Musgrove's fall at Lyme), his famous letter renews their love.
:::

::: quote
"It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune, must be in want of a wife." — *Pride and Prejudice* (opening)

"I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine." — Elizabeth, *Pride and Prejudice*

"Emma Woodhouse, handsome, clever, and rich, with a comfortable home and happy disposition, seemed to unite some of the best blessings of existence." — *Emma* (opening)

"You pierce my soul. I am half agony, half hope." — Wentworth's letter, *Persuasion*
:::
