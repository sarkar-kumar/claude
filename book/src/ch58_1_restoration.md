## 58.1 Background and Characteristics (1660–1798)

The **Neo-Classical Period** begins with the **Restoration of the monarchy** in 1660, when Charles II returned from exile, and ends with the publication of ***Lyrical Ballads*** (1798), which opened the Romantic Age. Writers of this period looked back to the **classical writers of Greece and Rome** (Homer, Virgil, Horace, Juvenal, Cicero) as models, and they valued:

- **Reason, order and good sense** over imagination and passion;
- **Decorum, correctness and clarity** of style;
- **Wit and satire** to correct the follies and vices of society;
- **Urban, social life** (the coffee-house, the court, the city) rather than solitary nature;
- **Fixed forms**: the **heroic couplet** (rhymed iambic pentameter couplets) in poetry, the periodical essay, the comedy of manners, the novel.

It is divided into three sub-periods:

| Sub-period | Dates | Also called | Key writers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. The Restoration Period** | 1660–1700 | Age of Dryden | Milton (*Paradise Lost*), Butler, Dryden, Bunyan, Congreve, Wycherley, Etherege, Locke, Aphra Behn, Pepys |
| **B. The Augustan Period** | 1700–1745 | Age of Pope; Classical Age | Pope, Swift, Defoe, Addison, Steele, Gay, Richardson (early) |
| **C. The Age of Sensibility** | 1745–1798 | Age of Johnson; Age of Transition | Johnson, Fielding, Richardson, Sterne, Smollett, Goldsmith, Gray, Collins, Cowper, Burns, Burke, Blake (early) |

## 58.2 A. The Restoration Period (1660–1700)

### 58.2.1 Background

The return of **Charles II** in 1660 ended Puritan rule. The court, influenced by France where the King had spent his exile, was witty, worldly and pleasure-loving. **Theatres reopened** (with **actresses** on the English stage for the first time), producing the bawdy, witty **Restoration comedy of manners** and **heroic tragedy**. The **Royal Society** (1660) promoted science and a plain, clear prose style. Other events: the **Great Plague (1665)**, the **Great Fire of London (1666)**, and the **Glorious Revolution (1688)**, which brought William III and Mary II to the throne. Samuel **Pepys** recorded the age in his famous ***Diary*** (1660–1669).

### 58.2.2 John Milton (1608–1674)

::: writer
**Titles:** the greatest English epic poet; "**the organ voice of England**" (Tennyson); "**God-gifted organ-voice**"; a "mighty-mouthed inventor of harmonies" (Tennyson) · **Education:** Christ's College, Cambridge ("the Lady of Christ's") · **Career:** Puritan pamphleteer; **Latin Secretary** (Secretary for Foreign Tongues) to Cromwell's Council of State (1649) · **Blindness:** completely blind by **1652** · **Masterpiece:** ***Paradise Lost*** (1667; 10 books; second edition 1674 in **12 books**) · **Other epics:** ***Paradise Regained*** (1671); tragedy ***Samson Agonistes*** (1671)
:::

Milton's life spans the Caroline, Commonwealth and Restoration periods; his great epics belong to the Restoration.

**Early poems (Caroline period):** *On the Morning of Christ's Nativity* (1629), the companion poems ***L'Allegro*** (the cheerful man) and ***Il Penseroso*** (the thoughtful man), the masque ***Comus*** (1634), and the pastoral elegy ***Lycidas*** (1637) on the death of his Cambridge friend **Edward King**, drowned in the Irish Sea.

**Prose (Commonwealth period):** Milton put aside poetry for twenty years to defend liberty. ***Areopagitica*** (1644) is the classic defence of the **freedom of the press** against censorship; *The Doctrine and Discipline of Divorce*; *Of Education*; ***The Tenure of Kings and Magistrates*** (1649, defending the right to depose a tyrant); ***Eikonoklastes*** (answering the royalist *Eikon Basilike*); *Defensio pro Populo Anglicano*.

**Sonnets:** *On His Blindness* ("When I consider how my light is spent"), *On the Late Massacre in Piedmont*, *On His Deceased Wife*, sonnets to Cromwell and Fairfax.

After the Restoration, Milton was briefly arrested, then lived in retirement, dictating his epics to his daughters and secretaries. He was paid only £10 (in two instalments of £5) for *Paradise Lost*.

::: work
**Paradise Lost (1667) — Summary.** Written in **blank verse**, in **12 books** (final edition), the epic's stated purpose is to "**justify the ways of God to men**". It begins *in medias res*.

**Books I–II.** **Satan** and his rebel angels, defeated in a war in Heaven, lie in the burning lake of **Hell**. Satan rouses them; they build the palace **Pandemonium**. In a great council, the fallen angels **Moloch** (open war), **Belial** (ease), **Mammon** (build a kingdom in hell) and **Beelzebub** (Satan's plan) debate; they decide to take revenge on God by corrupting His new creation, **Man**. Satan volunteers, passes the gates guarded by **Sin** and **Death**, and crosses **Chaos**.

**Book III.** In Heaven, God foresees the Fall; the **Son** offers to die to redeem mankind. Satan, disguised, deceives the angel **Uriel** and reaches Earth.

**Books IV–VIII.** In the **Garden of Eden**, Satan sees **Adam and Eve** in their innocence; he is caught whispering to Eve in the form of a toad and expelled by the angel **Gabriel**. God sends the archangel **Raphael** to warn Adam; Raphael narrates the war in Heaven, the defeat of Satan by the Son, and the Creation.

**Book IX.** Satan enters a **serpent** and persuades Eve, working alone, to eat the fruit of the **Tree of Knowledge**; Adam eats it too, out of love for her. They feel lust, shame and quarrel.

**Books X–XII.** The Son judges them; Sin and Death build a bridge to Earth; Satan, returning to Hell in triumph, is turned into a serpent with all his followers. Adam and Eve repent. The archangel **Michael** shows Adam the future history of mankind up to the redemption by Christ. Adam and Eve leave Paradise, hand in hand, to begin their life in the world.

**Characters:** God the Father, the Son (Messiah), Satan, Beelzebub, Moloch, Belial, Mammon, Mulciber, Sin, Death, Adam, Eve, Raphael, Michael, Gabriel, Uriel, Abdiel (the faithful angel).
:::

::: quote
"Of Man's first disobedience, and the fruit / Of that forbidden tree…" — *Paradise Lost*, I.1 (opening)

"…And justify the ways of God to men." — *Paradise Lost*, I.26

"The mind is its own place, and in itself / Can make a Heav'n of Hell, a Hell of Heav'n." — Satan, I.254

"Better to reign in Hell, than serve in Heav'n." — Satan, I.263

"The world was all before them, where to choose / Their place of rest, and Providence their guide." — XII (closing lines)

"They also serve who only stand and wait." — *On His Blindness*

"Fame is the spur that the clear spirit doth raise… / To scorn delights, and live laborious days." — *Lycidas*

"Tomorrow to fresh woods, and pastures new." — *Lycidas* (last line)

"Who kills a man kills a reasonable creature, God's image; but he who destroys a good book, kills reason itself." — *Areopagitica*
:::

::: work
**Paradise Regained (1671).** In four books, the Son (Christ) resists Satan's temptations in the wilderness (from Luke), regaining for man the paradise lost by Adam's disobedience.

**Samson Agonistes (1671).** A closet tragedy on the Greek model: the blind **Samson**, a prisoner of the **Philistines** at Gaza ("**Eyeless in Gaza, at the mill with slaves**"), is visited by his father **Manoa**, his treacherous wife **Dalila**, and the giant **Harapha**; summoned to entertain the Philistines at the feast of **Dagon**, he pulls down the temple, killing them and himself. It reflects Milton's own blindness and political defeat.
:::

::: note
**Romantic view of Satan:** **Blake** wrote that Milton "was a true Poet and of the Devil's party without knowing it"; **Shelley** also admired Satan's heroic rebellion. **Dryden** adapted *Paradise Lost* as an opera, *The State of Innocence*. **Dr. Johnson** said of the epic: "None ever wished it longer than it is."
:::

### 58.2.3 Samuel Butler (1613–1680)

::: writer
**Masterpiece:** ***Hudibras*** (three parts: 1663, 1664, 1678) — a **mock-heroic satire** on the Puritans · **Verse form:** **octosyllabic couplets**, comic rhymes ("**Hudibrastic verse**") · **Don't confuse with:** Samuel Butler (1835–1902), the Victorian author of *Erewhon* and *The Way of All Flesh* (Chapter 60)
:::

***Hudibras***, modelled on Cervantes' *Don Quixote*, ridicules the hypocrisy and fanaticism of the Puritans. The Presbyterian knight **Sir Hudibras** rides out with his squire **Ralpho**, an Independent, to reform the world; they quarrel endlessly over religion and are involved in absurd adventures (attacking a bear-baiting, being put in the stocks, wooing a rich widow). The poem was a favourite of Charles II, though Butler died poor.

::: quote
"For every why he had a wherefore." — *Hudibras*

"Love is a boy by poets styled; / Then spare the rod and spoil the child." — *Hudibras*
:::

### 58.2.4 John Dryden (1631–1700)

::: writer
**Titles:** **first official Poet Laureate** (1668); **Father of English Criticism** (Dr. Johnson); "**Father of English prose**" (modern prose — some books); the dominant figure of the age (**Age of Dryden**) · **Lost laureateship:** in 1688, as a Catholic who refused to swear allegiance to William III · **Buried:** Westminster Abbey, next to Chaucer
:::

Dryden excelled in **poetry, drama, criticism and translation**. He perfected the **heroic couplet**, which Pope later polished.

**Poems:** *Heroic Stanzas* on Cromwell's death (1659); *Astraea Redux* (1660, welcoming Charles II); ***Annus Mirabilis*** (1667, on the year 1666 — the Dutch war and the Great Fire); ***Absalom and Achitophel*** (1681, political satire); ***Mac Flecknoe*** (1682, mock-heroic satire on the poet **Thomas Shadwell**); *The Medal*; ***Religio Laici*** (1682, defending the Church of England); ***The Hind and the Panther*** (1687, defending Catholicism after his conversion; the Hind = Catholic Church, the Panther = Church of England); *Alexander's Feast* and *A Song for St Cecilia's Day* (odes on music).

**Plays:** heroic tragedies *The Indian Emperor*, *The Conquest of Granada* (1670), *Aureng-Zebe* (1675, on the Mughal emperor); ***All for Love*** (1677, a blank-verse version of the Antony and Cleopatra story following the **classical unities**); comedies *Marriage à la Mode*.

**Criticism:** ***An Essay of Dramatic Poesy*** (1668) — a dialogue among four speakers (**Crites, Eugenius, Lisideius, Neander** — Neander represents Dryden) comparing ancient and modern, French and English drama, and defending Shakespeare; **prefaces**, especially the *Preface to the Fables* (1700), with its famous praise of Chaucer.

**Translations:** Virgil's ***Aeneid*** and other works (1697); *Fables, Ancient and Modern* (1700) — versions of Chaucer, Boccaccio and Ovid.

::: work
**Absalom and Achitophel (1681).** A political satire using the biblical story of King David's son Absalom's rebellion (2 Samuel) to describe the **Exclusion Crisis**: **David** = **Charles II**; **Absalom** = the King's illegitimate son, the **Duke of Monmouth**, who wanted to be heir; **Achitophel** = the scheming **Earl of Shaftesbury**; **Zimri** = the Duke of Buckingham; **Israel** = England; **Jerusalem** = London. It contains brilliant satiric portraits.

**Mac Flecknoe (1682).** The aged king of Dullness, the poet **Flecknoe**, chooses **Shadwell** ("Mac Flecknoe", son of Flecknoe) as his heir in dullness — a model for Pope's *The Dunciad*.
:::

::: quote
"None but the brave deserves the fair." — *Alexander's Feast*

"Great wits are sure to madness near allied, / And thin partitions do their bounds divide." — *Absalom and Achitophel* (on Achitophel)

"A man so various that he seemed to be / Not one, but all mankind's epitome." — *Absalom and Achitophel* (on Zimri)

"Shadwell alone, of all my sons, is he / Who stands confirmed in full stupidity." — *Mac Flecknoe*

"Here is God's plenty." — *Preface to the Fables* (on Chaucer)

"He was the man who of all modern, and perhaps ancient poets, had the largest and most comprehensive soul." — *Essay of Dramatic Poesy* (on Shakespeare)
:::

### 58.2.5 William Congreve (1670–1729)

::: writer
**Known as:** the greatest writer of **Restoration comedy of manners** · **Plays:** *The Old Bachelor* (1693), *The Double Dealer* (1693), ***Love for Love*** (1695), *The Mourning Bride* (1697, his only tragedy), ***The Way of the World*** (1700)
:::

The **comedy of manners** portrays the fashionable upper class of London with sparkling, witty dialogue, intrigue, cynicism about marriage, and stock characters (the rake, the fop, the witty heroine, the jealous husband). Other writers of the type were **George Etherege** (*The Man of Mode*) and **William Wycherley** (*The Country Wife*, *The Plain Dealer*); later **Vanbrugh** and **Farquhar**. **Jeremy Collier**'s attack, *A Short View of the Immorality and Profaneness of the English Stage* (1698), contributed to its decline.

::: work
**The Way of the World (1700).** The witty, charming **Mirabell** loves the brilliant **Millamant**, but she is the ward of her aunt **Lady Wishfort**, who controls half of Millamant's fortune and hates Mirabell (he once pretended to court her). Mirabell's plot, in which his servant **Waitwell** disguises himself as "Sir Rowland" to woo Lady Wishfort, is exposed by the villainous **Fainall** and his mistress **Mrs. Marwood**, who try to blackmail Lady Wishfort for her daughter's (Mrs. Fainall's) fortune. Mirabell defeats Fainall with a deed of trust signed earlier by Mrs. Fainall, and wins Millamant. The famous "**proviso scene**", in which Mirabell and Millamant set the conditions for their marriage, is a high point of English comedy.

**Characters:** Mirabell, Millamant, Lady Wishfort, Fainall, Mrs. Fainall, Mrs. Marwood, Witwoud, Petulant, Sir Wilfull Witwoud, Waitwell, Foible.
:::

::: quote
"Heaven has no rage like love to hatred turned, / Nor hell a fury like a woman scorned." — *The Mourning Bride* (often misquoted as "Hell hath no fury like a woman scorned")

"Music has charms to soothe a savage breast." — *The Mourning Bride* (opening line)

"I may by degrees dwindle into a wife." — Millamant, *The Way of the World*
:::

### 58.2.6 John Locke (1632–1704)

::: writer
**Field:** philosophy — **Father of Liberalism**; leading British **empiricist** · **Major works:** ***An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*** (1690), ***Two Treatises of Government*** (1689/90), *A Letter Concerning Toleration* (1689), *Some Thoughts Concerning Education* (1693), *The Reasonableness of Christianity* (1695)
:::

**Locke**, a physician and political thinker associated with the Earl of Shaftesbury, fled to Holland and returned after the Glorious Revolution (1688). In ***An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*** he argued that the mind at birth is a **"tabula rasa"** (a blank slate, "white paper") and that all knowledge comes from **experience** (sensation and reflection), rejecting innate ideas. In the ***Two Treatises of Government*** he argued — against Hobbes and the divine right of kings — that government rests on the **consent of the governed**, exists to protect the natural rights of **"life, liberty and property"**, and may be resisted if it becomes tyrannical. His ideas influenced the **American Declaration of Independence** and the **French Enlightenment**.

### 58.2.7 John Bunyan (1628–1688)

::: writer
**Profession:** tinker (mender of pots and pans) and Puritan (Baptist) preacher · **Imprisoned:** for 12 years (1660–1672) in **Bedford** jail for preaching without a licence · **Masterpiece:** ***The Pilgrim's Progress*** (Part I, 1678; Part II, 1684) — the most famous **allegory** in English · **Other works:** *Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners* (1666, spiritual autobiography), *The Life and Death of Mr. Badman* (1680), *The Holy War* (1682)
:::

***The Pilgrim's Progress from This World to That Which Is to Come***, written in prison in simple, Bible-based prose, was for two centuries the most widely read book in England after the Bible, and has been translated into more than 200 languages.

::: work
**Summary (Part I).** The narrator dreams of a man named **Christian** who, carrying a heavy **burden** (of sin) on his back and reading a book (the Bible), learns that his home, the **City of Destruction**, will be destroyed. Guided by **Evangelist**, he leaves his family and sets out for the **Celestial City**. He falls into the **Slough of Despond**, is misled by **Mr. Worldly Wiseman**, passes through the **Wicket Gate**, and at the **Cross** his burden falls away. He climbs the **Hill Difficulty**, stays at the **House Beautiful**, fights the monster **Apollyon** in the **Valley of Humiliation**, and passes through the **Valley of the Shadow of Death**. With his companion **Faithful**, he comes to the town of **Vanity Fair**, where Faithful is tried and martyred. With a new companion, **Hopeful**, Christian is imprisoned in **Doubting Castle** by **Giant Despair**, escaping with the key called **Promise**. They reach the **Delectable Mountains** and the land of **Beulah**, cross the **River of Death**, and enter the **Celestial City**. **Ignorance**, who tries to enter without proper faith, is sent to hell.

**Part II** follows Christian's wife **Christiana** and their children, guided by **Mr. Great-heart**, on the same journey.

**Characters:** Christian, Evangelist, Obstinate, Pliable, Help, Mr. Worldly Wiseman, Goodwill, the Interpreter, Faithful, Hopeful, Apollyon, Giant Despair, Talkative, By-ends, Ignorance, Christiana, Mr. Great-heart.
:::

::: quote
"As I walked through the wilderness of this world…" — *The Pilgrim's Progress* (opening)

"He that is down needs fear no fall, / He that is low, no pride." — *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part II

"Who would true valour see, / Let him come hither." — *The Pilgrim's Progress*, Part II (the hymn "To be a Pilgrim")
:::

### 58.2.8 Aphra Behn (1640–1689)

::: writer
**Titles:** the **first English woman to earn her living by writing**; the first professional woman writer in English · **Career:** spy for Charles II in Antwerp; playwright, poet, novelist, translator · **Major works:** ***Oroonoko, or The Royal Slave*** (1688), plays ***The Rover*** (1677), *The Forced Marriage*, *The Lucky Chance*; *Love-Letters Between a Nobleman and His Sister* · **Buried:** Westminster Abbey
:::

***Oroonoko*** — one of the earliest English novels — tells of **Oroonoko**, a noble African prince of Coramantien, who loves **Imoinda**; he is tricked into slavery and taken to **Surinam** (South America), where he leads a slave revolt, kills Imoinda (at her wish) to save her from dishonour, and is cruelly executed. It is an early work criticising slavery. ***The Rover*** is a lively comedy about English Cavaliers in Naples at carnival time (**Willmore**, **Hellena**, **Angellica Bianca**).

::: quote
"All women together ought to let flowers fall upon the tomb of Aphra Behn … for it was she who earned them the right to speak their minds." — Virginia Woolf, *A Room of One's Own* (1929)
:::
