# Punctuations {#ch44}

[Part A · Grammar]{.chpart}
:::

## 44.1 What Is Punctuation?

**Punctuation** is the use of special marks (stops and signs) in writing to **separate** sentences and their parts and to make the **meaning clear**. Wrong punctuation can change the meaning completely:

::: ex
- *Let's eat, Grandma!* (inviting Grandma to eat) / *Let's eat Grandma!* (a horrifying meaning)
- *The teacher said, "The student is a fool."* / *"The teacher," said the student, "is a fool."*
- *A woman, without her man, is nothing.* / *A woman: without her, man is nothing.*
:::

Punctuation is tested in SSC/HSC ("Punctuate and capitalise the passage"), in BCS Written, bank written and admission tests, and in the SAT Writing section (commas, semicolons, colons, dashes, apostrophes).

## 44.2 The Main Punctuation Marks

| Mark | Name | Main use |
|---|---|---|
| **.** | Full stop / Period | ends a statement or command |
| **,** | Comma | short pause; separates items, clauses, phrases |
| **;** | Semicolon | joins closely related independent clauses; separates complex list items |
| **:** | Colon | introduces a list, explanation, quotation or example |
| **?** | Question mark | ends a direct question |
| **!** | Exclamation mark | ends an exclamation or strong command |
| **' '** / **" "** | Inverted commas / Quotation marks | enclose direct speech and quotations |
| **'** | Apostrophe | shows possession or contraction |
| **-** | Hyphen | joins compound words |
| **—** | Dash | marks a sudden break or emphasis |
| **( )** | Brackets / Parentheses | enclose extra information |
| **[ ]** | Square brackets | editorial insertions inside quotations |
| **…** | Ellipsis | shows omitted words or a trailing thought |
| **/** | Slash (oblique) | alternatives, fractions, dates |
| **CAPITALS** | Capital letters | beginnings, proper nouns, *I*, titles |

## 44.3 Capital Letters

Use a **capital letter** for:

1. The **first word of a sentence**: *He is a doctor.*
2. The **first word of direct speech**: *He said, "**W**e are late."*
3. The pronoun **I** and the interjection **O**: *I know.* / ***O** God!*
4. **Proper nouns and proper adjectives**: *Dhaka, Rahim, the Padma, Bangladesh, Bangladeshi, English, Islam, Christian.*
5. **Days, months and festivals** (not seasons): *Friday, December, Eid-ul-Fitr, Pahela Baishakh* (but *summer, winter*).
6. **Titles** before names: *Dr. Rahman, Professor Ali, President Lincoln, Sheikh Mujibur Rahman.*
7. Important words in **titles** of books, films, newspapers: *The Old Man and the Sea, The Daily Star, Pride and Prejudice.*
8. Names of **God and holy books**: *Allah, God, the Almighty, the Quran, the Bible.*
9. **Historical events, periods, organisations**: *the Liberation War, the Renaissance, the United Nations, Dhaka University.*
10. **Abbreviations and acronyms**: *UNESCO, BCS, NGO, Dr., Mr.*
11. The **first word of each line of traditional poetry**.
12. **Personified** nouns: *O **D**eath, where is thy sting?*

## 44.4 The Full Stop (Period)

1. At the end of an **assertive** sentence and most **imperative** sentences: *He is ill. Shut the door.*
2. After **abbreviations** (BrE often omits it in contractions like *Mr, Dr*; both styles accepted): *B.A., M.A., Dr., Mr., etc., i.e., e.g., a.m., p.m.*
3. At the end of an **indirect question**: *He asked me where I lived.*
4. In **decimals and money**: *3.5 kg, Tk. 10.50.*

## 44.5 The Comma

The comma is the **most frequently used and misused** mark. Learn its main uses:

1. **To separate items in a list** (three or more): *He bought rice, lentils, oil and salt.* (The comma before *and* — the **Oxford/serial comma** — is optional in BrE, common in AmE and on the SAT: *rice, lentils, oil, and salt.*)
2. **Before a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS) joining two independent clauses**: *He was ill, but he came to the office.* / *It was raining, so we stayed in.*
3. **After an introductory word, phrase or clause**: *However, he did not come. / In the morning, we went out. / When he came, I was reading. / Having finished the work, he left.*
4. **To set off non-defining (non-essential) relative clauses and appositives**: *My father, who is a doctor, lives in Sylhet. / Dhaka, the capital of Bangladesh, is crowded.*
5. **To set off parenthetical expressions and interrupters**: *He is, I think, honest. / The plan, however, failed.*
6. **Before and after the person addressed (vocative)**: *Rahim, come here. / Come here, Rahim. / I am sure, sir, that he will come.*
7. **Before direct speech** (after the reporting verb) and inside the quotation when the reporting clause follows: *He said, "I am busy." / "I am busy," he said.*
8. **In dates, addresses and numbers**: *26 March, 1971 / Friday, 16 December 1971 / Mirpur, Dhaka / 1,000,000.*
9. **Between coordinate adjectives**: *a long, boring speech* (but not *a bright red car*).
10. **Before a tag question**: *He is a doctor, isn't he?*
11. **After *yes / no / well / oh*** at the start: *Yes, I agree. / Well, let's begin.*
12. **To show omission of a verb** (ellipsis): *Rahim likes tea; Karim, coffee.*
13. **To avoid confusion**: *Inside, the room was dark.* / *To Rahim, Karim was a hero.*
14. **After the salutation and complimentary close of informal letters**: *Dear Rahim, / Yours sincerely,*

::: rule
**Do not use a comma:**

- **between subject and verb**: ✘ *The boy in the red shirt, is my brother.*
- **between a verb and its object**: ✘ *He said, that he was ill.* ✔ *He said that he was ill.*
- **before a defining relative clause**: ✘ *The students, who work hard, pass.* (if you mean only those who work hard)
- **to join two independent clauses on its own** (comma splice): ✘ *It was late, we went home.*
- **before *that* in reported speech**, or after *such as / like* introducing examples.
:::

## 44.6 The Semicolon

1. **To join two closely related independent clauses** without a conjunction: *The night was dark; the wind was howling.*
2. **Before conjunctive adverbs** (*however, therefore, moreover, nevertheless, otherwise, consequently, hence, besides, still*) joining two independent clauses: *He was ill; therefore, he did not come.*
3. **To separate items in a list when the items already contain commas**: *The committee had members from Dhaka, Bangladesh; Kolkata, India; and Kathmandu, Nepal.*
4. **To balance contrasting clauses**: *Some men are born great; some achieve greatness; and some have greatness thrust upon them.* (Shakespeare, *Twelfth Night*)

## 44.7 The Colon

1. **Before a list** (after a complete clause): *You need three things: patience, practice and perseverance.* (✘ *You need: patience…* — no colon after a verb.)
2. **Before an explanation, result or summary**: *He had one aim: to become a doctor.* / *The reason was clear: he had not studied.*
3. **Before a long or formal quotation**: *Shakespeare wrote: "All the world's a stage."*
4. **In time, ratios, titles, references**: *10:30 a.m., 3:1, Quran 2:255, English Grammar: A Complete Guide.*
5. **After the salutation in formal (AmE) letters**: *Dear Sir:* (BrE uses a comma or nothing).

::: tip
**Exam Tip — colon vs semicolon:** A **semicolon** joins two **complete sentences** of equal weight. A **colon** introduces something that **explains or completes** what comes before it; the part **before** a colon must be a complete sentence.
:::

## 44.8 The Question Mark

1. At the end of a **direct question**: *Where do you live?*
2. After a **tag question**: *You are ready, aren't you?*
3. Inside quotation marks when the quoted words are a question: *He asked, "Are you ready?"*
4. **Not** after an indirect question: ✘ *He asked me where I lived?* ✔ *He asked me where I lived.*
5. In brackets to show doubt: *Chaucer was born in 1343 (?).*

## 44.9 The Exclamation Mark

1. After **interjections and exclamatory sentences**: *Alas! He is dead. / What a beautiful scene!*
2. After **strong commands or wishes**: *Get out! / May you be happy!*
3. Avoid more than one, and avoid it in formal writing.

## 44.10 Inverted Commas (Quotation Marks)

1. **To enclose the exact words of a speaker** (direct speech): *He said, "I am ill."*
2. **To enclose quotations** from books and speeches: *Tagore said, "Where the mind is without fear…"*
3. **For titles of short works** (poems, articles, chapters, songs): *"Bidrohi", "Ode to a Nightingale"*. (Long works — books, films, newspapers — are *italicised* or underlined: *Gitanjali, Hamlet.*)
4. **For words used in a special sense** (irony, technical terms): *His "help" made things worse.*
5. **Quotation within a quotation**: use single inside double (AmE) or double inside single (BrE): *He said, "My teacher told me, 'Never give up.'"*

### 44.10.1 Punctuation with Quotation Marks

- A **comma** separates the reporting verb from the quotation: *He said, "Come in."*
- The **first word** of the quotation begins with a **capital**.
- The closing **full stop / question mark / exclamation mark** of the quotation goes **inside** the inverted commas: *"Where are you going?" he asked.*
- When the quotation is broken by the reporting clause, the second part begins with a small letter if it continues the same sentence: *"I am sure," he said, "that he will come."*

## 44.11 The Apostrophe

1. **Possession**: *Rahim's book, the boys' hostel, children's park, James's (James') car, my brother-in-law's house* (Chapter 02).
2. **Contraction (omitted letters)**: *don't (do not), it's (it is / it has), I'm, can't, won't, o'clock (of the clock), '71 (1971).*
3. **Plurals of letters, figures and signs** (traditional): *Mind your p's and q's. / Two 5's.* (Modern style often omits it for figures and abbreviations: *1990s, MPs.*)

::: rule
**No apostrophe in possessive pronouns:** *its, yours, hers, ours, theirs, whose.* ✘ *it's tail* ✔ *its tail*; ✘ *your's* ✔ *yours*. And **no apostrophe for ordinary plurals**: ✘ *apple's for sale* ✔ *apples for sale*.
:::

## 44.12 The Hyphen

1. **Compound words**: *mother-in-law, passer-by, editor-in-chief, forget-me-not, self-confidence, ex-president.*
2. **Compound adjectives before a noun**: *a well-known writer, a five-year plan, a ten-year-old boy, an up-to-date list.* (Not after the noun: *The writer is well known.*)
3. **Numbers from 21 to 99 and fractions**: *twenty-one, ninety-nine, two-thirds.*
4. **Prefixes before proper nouns or to avoid confusion**: *anti-British, pre-Liberation, re-cover (cover again) vs recover (get well), re-sign vs resign.*
5. **Word division at the end of a line** (between syllables).

## 44.13 The Dash

A dash (—) is longer than a hyphen and is used:

1. **To show a sudden break or change of thought**: *I was going to tell him — but never mind.*
2. **To add emphasis or an explanation**: *He had only one goal — success.*
3. **In pairs, like brackets, for an interruption**: *My brother — who never reads — bought ten books.*
4. **Before a summarising word**: *Honesty, hard work, patience — these are the keys to success.*

## 44.14 Brackets (Parentheses) and Square Brackets

- **Round brackets ( )** enclose extra information, explanations, dates, references: *Kazi Nazrul Islam (1899–1976) is our national poet.*
- **Square brackets [ ]** enclose words added by an editor inside a quotation: *"He [Tagore] won the Nobel Prize in 1913."*

## 44.15 Ellipsis

Three dots (…) show that words have been **omitted** from a quotation, or that a sentence **trails off**: *"To be, or not to be…" / I wonder if…*

## 44.16 Punctuating a Passage — Worked Examples

**Example 1**

*Unpunctuated:* the teacher said to the boys why are you making a noise dont you know that the headmaster is in his room

*Punctuated:* The teacher said to the boys, "Why are you making a noise? Don't you know that the Headmaster is in his room?"

**Example 2**

*Unpunctuated:* alas said the old man my son is dead what shall i do now

*Punctuated:* "Alas!" said the old man. "My son is dead. What shall I do now?"

**Example 3**

*Unpunctuated:* dhaka the capital of bangladesh is a densely populated city it has many mosques schools colleges and universities

*Punctuated:* Dhaka, the capital of Bangladesh, is a densely populated city. It has many mosques, schools, colleges and universities.

**Example 4**

*Unpunctuated:* rabindranath tagore who wrote gitanjali won the nobel prize in 1913 he was the first asian to win it

*Punctuated:* Rabindranath Tagore, who wrote *Gitanjali*, won the Nobel Prize in 1913. He was the first Asian to win it.

**Example 5**

*Unpunctuated:* i have three wishes said rahim good health a good job and a happy family

*Punctuated:* "I have three wishes," said Rahim: "good health, a good job and a happy family." (or: *Rahim said, "I have three wishes: good health, a good job and a happy family."*)

## 44.17 Common Punctuation Errors

::: errs
1. ✘ He said that, he was ill. ✔ He said that he was ill.
2. ✘ He asked me where I lived? ✔ He asked me where I lived.
3. ✘ It was late, we went home. ✔ It was late, **so** we went home. / It was late**;** we went home.
4. ✘ The dog wagged it's tail. ✔ The dog wagged **its** tail.
5. ✘ This book is your's. ✔ This book is **yours**.
6. ✘ The girls's hostel. ✔ The **girls'** hostel.
7. ✘ He said "I am busy". ✔ He said, "I am busy."
8. ✘ My father who is a doctor lives in Sylhet (if you have only one father). ✔ My father**,** who is a doctor**,** lives in Sylhet.
9. ✘ You need: pen, paper and ink. ✔ You need pen, paper and ink. / You need three things**:** pen, paper and ink.
10. ✘ He was ill, however he came. ✔ He was ill**;** however**,** he came.
11. ✘ i am a student of dhaka college. ✔ **I** am a student of **Dhaka College**.
12. ✘ We celebrate victory day on 16 december. ✔ We celebrate **Victory Day** on 16 **December**.
13. ✘ A well known writer. ✔ A **well-known** writer.
14. ✘ What a lovely day it is. ✔ What a lovely day it is**!**
15. ✘ Apple's for sale. ✔ **Apples** for sale.
:::

::: gre
**SAT / GRE / GMAT Corner — Punctuation on the SAT:** The digital SAT's "Standard English Conventions" questions test: (1) **comma splices and run-ons** (fix with a period, semicolon, or comma + FANBOYS); (2) **semicolons** (only between two independent clauses or in complex lists); (3) **colons** (only after a complete clause, before a list/explanation); (4) **dashes** (in matching pairs around an interruption, or one dash before an explanation); (5) **commas around non-essential elements** (both commas or none); (6) **no comma between subject and verb**; and (7) **apostrophes** (*its/it's, their/they're/there, whose/who's*, singular vs plural possessives).
:::
