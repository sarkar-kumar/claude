## 60.1 Background (1832/1837–1901)

The **Victorian Period** takes its name from **Queen Victoria**, who reigned from **1837 to 1901**, the longest reign in British history before Elizabeth II. Many literary histories begin the period in **1832**, the year of the first **Reform Act** and the death of Scott. It was an age of:

- **Industrial and economic growth**, railways, steamships, the telegraph, and the **Great Exhibition (1851)**;
- the **British Empire** at its height, including India (under the Crown from 1858 after the Sepoy Mutiny / War of Independence of 1857);
- **social problems**: factory labour, child labour, slums, poverty (the "**Condition of England**" question), Chartism, the Corn Laws;
- **reform**: extension of the vote, the Factory Acts, education acts (1870);
- **science and doubt**: geology (Lyell), **Darwin's *On the Origin of Species* (1859)**, the conflict between science and faith;
- **Victorian morality**: respectability, earnestness, duty, the home, "Victorian compromise".

### 60.1.1 Characteristics of Victorian Literature

1. The **novel** became the dominant form, often published in **monthly instalments (serialisation)** and read aloud in families; it dealt with society, class, money, marriage, crime and moral growth (Dickens, Thackeray, the Brontës, George Eliot, Trollope, Hardy).
2. **Realism** and social criticism; later **Naturalism** (Hardy, Gissing).
3. Poetry combined Romantic feeling with **moral seriousness** and reflection on doubt and faith (Tennyson, Browning, Arnold); the **dramatic monologue** became a major form (Browning).
4. The **Pre-Raphaelite Brotherhood** (1848) — D. G. Rossetti, Christina Rossetti, William Morris, Swinburne — sought vivid colour, medieval themes and sensuous beauty.
5. **Aestheticism** and **Decadence** at the end of the century (Pater, Wilde: "art for art's sake").
6. The "**Victorian sages**" — Carlyle, Ruskin, Mill, Arnold, Newman — wrote prose on society, religion, art and culture.

## 60.2 Lord Alfred Tennyson (1809–1892)

::: writer
**Born:** Somersby, Lincolnshire (son of a rector) · **Education:** Trinity College, Cambridge, where he formed a deep friendship with **Arthur Henry Hallam**, who died suddenly in Vienna in 1833 · **Titles:** **Poet Laureate (1850–1892)**, succeeding Wordsworth — the longest-serving laureate; **the representative poet of the Victorian Age**; created **Baron Tennyson (1884)** — the first English poet raised to the peerage for his poetry · **Buried:** Westminster Abbey · **Major works:** *Poems, Chiefly Lyrical* (1830), *Poems* (1832/1842), ***In Memoriam A.H.H.*** (1850), ***Maud*** (1855), ***Idylls of the King*** (1859–1885), ***Enoch Arden*** (1864), *The Princess* (1847), *Ulysses*, *The Lotos-Eaters*, *The Lady of Shalott*, *Tithonus*, *Locksley Hall*, *Morte d'Arthur*, *Break, Break, Break*, *Tears, Idle Tears*, ***The Charge of the Light Brigade*** (1854), ***Crossing the Bar*** (1889)
:::

**Tennyson** expressed the hopes, doubts and moral earnestness of his age in poetry of great **musical beauty** and careful craftsmanship. His poems range from lyrics and idylls to elegy, dramatic monologue and Arthurian epic. Queen Victoria said that, after Prince Albert's death, *In Memoriam* was her greatest comfort after the Bible.

::: work
**In Memoriam A.H.H. (1850).** A long elegy of **131 sections** (plus prologue and epilogue), written over **17 years** after the death of his friend **Arthur Henry Hallam** (1833). Written in the "**In Memoriam stanza**" (iambic tetrameter quatrains rhyming *abba*), it records the poet's grief, his struggle with **religious doubt** caused by geology and evolution ("**Nature, red in tooth and claw**"), the passing of three Christmases, and his slow recovery of faith in God, love and immortality. It ends with the marriage of his sister Cecilia, looking forward to a higher race of men.

**Ulysses (1833/1842).** A **dramatic monologue**: the aged **Ulysses**, back in Ithaca as king of "a savage race", is restless and longs to sail again with his old mariners "beyond the sunset" to seek new knowledge and experience; he resolves "**To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield**."

**The Lady of Shalott (1832/1842).** The Lady, under a curse, lives in a tower on an island near Camelot, weaving a web of the images she sees in a **mirror**; when she sees **Sir Lancelot** and looks directly out of the window, the mirror cracks; she floats down the river to Camelot in a boat and dies singing.

**Idylls of the King (1859–1885).** Twelve narrative poems retelling the **Arthurian legend** (from Malory): the founding of the Round Table, the love of Lancelot and Guinevere, the quest for the Grail, and the decline and fall of Arthur's ideal kingdom — an allegory of the soul's war with sense and of the decay of an ideal society. Includes *The Coming of Arthur*, *Gareth and Lynette*, *Lancelot and Elaine*, *The Holy Grail*, *Guinevere*, *The Passing of Arthur*.

**Enoch Arden (1864).** The fisherman **Enoch Arden**, shipwrecked and missing for ten years, returns to find his wife **Annie** happily married to his childhood friend **Philip Ray**; out of love, he never reveals himself and dies of a broken heart.

**The Charge of the Light Brigade (1854).** On the disastrous charge of British cavalry at **Balaclava** in the **Crimean War**, following a mistaken order: "*Theirs not to reason why, / Theirs but to do and die*."

**Crossing the Bar (1889).** A short lyric on death as a peaceful voyage out to sea; Tennyson asked that it be placed at the end of all editions of his poems.
:::

## 60.3 Charles Dickens (1812–1870)

::: writer
**Born:** Portsmouth (Landport), 7 February 1812 · **Childhood:** his father **John Dickens** was imprisoned for debt in the **Marshalsea**; at 12 Charles worked in **Warren's Blacking Factory** — experiences reflected in *David Copperfield* and *Little Dorrit* · **Career:** law clerk, parliamentary reporter, editor (*Household Words*, *All the Year Round*); celebrated public readings in Britain and America · **Pen-name:** "**Boz**" (*Sketches by Boz*, 1836) · **Titles:** the greatest novelist of the Victorian Age; "the Inimitable"; creator of **Dickensian** characters · **Died:** Gad's Hill, Kent, 9 June 1870; buried in Westminster Abbey (Poets' Corner)
:::

**Dickens** wrote vivid, humorous and passionate novels that exposed the **social evils** of his time — the workhouse and the Poor Law, debtors' prisons, child labour, bad schools, the slow Court of Chancery, slums and the cruelty of utilitarian economics — while creating hundreds of unforgettable, often **caricatured** characters. His novels were published in **monthly or weekly instalments**, with cliff-hanging endings.

**Oliver Twist (1837–39).** **Oliver**, an orphan born in a workhouse, dares to ask for more gruel ("**Please, sir, I want some more**") and is sent away to an undertaker. He runs away to London, where he falls into the hands of the criminal **Fagin**, who trains boys as pickpockets (the **Artful Dodger**, Charley Bates). Arrested by mistake, he is rescued by the kind **Mr. Brownlow**, but the burglar **Bill Sikes** and Fagin recapture him. During a burglary, Oliver is shot and taken in by **Mrs. Maylie** and her niece **Rose**. **Nancy**, Sikes's companion, reveals the plot of Oliver's evil half-brother **Monks**; Sikes brutally murders Nancy, and dies while fleeing; Fagin is hanged. Oliver is found to be the son of Mr. Brownlow's friend, Rose is his aunt, and Mr. Brownlow adopts him. Other characters: **Mr. Bumble** (the beadle), Mrs. Corney, Noah Claypole, Mr. Sowerberry.

**A Christmas Carol (1843).** On Christmas Eve, the cold, mean miser **Ebenezer Scrooge** ("**Bah! Humbug!**"), who underpays his clerk **Bob Cratchit**, is visited by the ghost of his dead partner **Jacob Marley**, dragging chains, and then by three spirits: the **Ghost of Christmas Past**, the **Ghost of Christmas Present** (showing the poor but happy Cratchit family and the sick child **Tiny Tim**) and the **Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come** (showing Scrooge's lonely, unmourned death). Scrooge wakes on Christmas morning a changed, generous man. Tiny Tim's words: "**God bless us, every one!**"

**David Copperfield (1849–50).** The most autobiographical of Dickens's novels, told in the first person. **David**'s happy childhood with his widowed mother and the nurse **Peggotty** ends when his mother marries the cruel **Mr. Murdstone**. Sent to work in a London bottling warehouse, David lodges with the always-hopeful, always-penniless **Mr. Micawber** ("something will turn up"). He runs away to his eccentric aunt **Betsey Trotwood** in Dover, is educated, works for the lawyer Mr. Wickfield, and marries the charming but childish **Dora Spenlow**. After Dora's death, he marries Wickfield's wise daughter **Agnes**. The scheming, "'umble" clerk **Uriah Heep** is exposed by Micawber. Other characters: **Steerforth**, Little Em'ly, Ham, Mr. Dick, Traddles.

**A Tale of Two Cities (1859).** Set in **London and Paris** before and during the **French Revolution**. **Dr. Manette**, released after 18 years in the **Bastille**, is restored to his daughter **Lucie**, who marries **Charles Darnay**, a French aristocrat who has given up his cruel family name (St. Evrémonde). The dissolute English lawyer **Sydney Carton**, who looks exactly like Darnay, also loves Lucie. During the Terror, Darnay returns to Paris to help a servant and is condemned to the **guillotine**, largely through the vengeful **Madame Defarge**, who knits the names of victims. Carton takes Darnay's place in prison and goes to the guillotine to save him for Lucie. Other characters: **Mr. Jarvis Lorry**, **Jerry Cruncher**, **Miss Pross**, **Monsieur Defarge**.

**Great Expectations (1860–61).** **Philip Pirrip ("Pip")**, an orphan raised by his harsh sister and her kind husband, the blacksmith **Joe Gargery**, helps an escaped convict on the marshes. He visits the strange **Miss Havisham**, who was jilted on her wedding day and still wears her wedding dress with all the clocks stopped, and falls in love with her cold, beautiful ward **Estella**. Suddenly Pip receives "**great expectations**" — a fortune from an unknown benefactor — and becomes a snobbish gentleman in London, ashamed of Joe. The benefactor turns out to be the convict **Abel Magwitch**, who returns illegally from Australia; Pip learns gratitude and humility. Magwitch is recaptured and dies; Estella is Magwitch's daughter. Pip loses his fortune but becomes a better man. Other characters: **Herbert Pocket**, **Mr. Jaggers** (lawyer), **Wemmick**, **Biddy**, Compeyson, Orlick, Pumblechook.

**Hard Times (1854).** In the industrial town of **Coketown**, the utilitarian **Thomas Gradgrind** brings up his children **Louisa** and **Tom** on "**Facts**" alone, crushing their imagination; Louisa marries the boastful mill-owner **Josiah Bounderby**; Tom robs a bank; the honest worker **Stephen Blackpool** is wrongly suspected; the circus girl **Sissy Jupe** represents warmth and fancy. A critique of utilitarianism and industrial capitalism.
:::

## 60.4 Robert Browning (1812–1889)

::: writer
**Born:** Camberwell, London · **Education:** largely self-taught from his father's huge library · **Wife:** the poet **Elizabeth Barrett** — they eloped in 1846 and lived in **Florence, Italy**, until her death (1861) · **Titles:** master of the **dramatic monologue**; the "**poet of optimism**"; an "obscure" poet (his difficult early poem *Sordello*) · **Died:** Venice, 1889; buried in Westminster Abbey (on the day his last book *Asolando* was published) · **Major works:** *Pauline* (1833), *Paracelsus* (1835), *Sordello* (1840), ***Bells and Pomegranates*** (1841–46, including *Pippa Passes* and *Dramatic Lyrics*), ***Men and Women*** (1855), ***Dramatis Personae*** (1864), ***The Ring and the Book*** (1868–69), *Asolando* (1889)
:::

**Browning**'s great achievement is the **dramatic monologue** — a poem in which a single speaker, not the poet, addresses a silent listener at a critical moment, revealing his or her own character, often unintentionally. His style is rough, colloquial and compressed, and his interest is in **psychology** — especially of artists, lovers, criminals and churchmen of the Italian Renaissance. His philosophy is robustly **optimistic**: life is a struggle and a test, and "a man's reach should exceed his grasp".

**Famous poems:** ***My Last Duchess***, ***Porphyria's Lover***, *Soliloquy of the Spanish Cloister*, ***The Pied Piper of Hamelin***, *How They Brought the Good News from Ghent to Aix*, *Home-Thoughts, from Abroad*, *Meeting at Night*, *Parting at Morning*, ***Andrea del Sarto***, *Fra Lippo Lippi*, *The Bishop Orders His Tomb at Saint Praxed's Church*, *Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came*, ***Rabbi Ben Ezra***, *A Grammarian's Funeral*, *Prospice*, *Love Among the Ruins*, *The Last Ride Together*, *Abt Vogler*, *Caliban upon Setebos*.

::: work
**My Last Duchess (1842).** A **dramatic monologue** set in Renaissance Ferrara. The **Duke of Ferrara**, showing a portrait of his late wife (painted by "Fra Pandolf") to an envoy who is arranging his next marriage to a Count's daughter, reveals — without meaning to — his jealousy and cruelty: his young Duchess smiled too freely at everyone and was too easily pleased; he "gave commands; / Then all smiles stopped together" — implying he had her killed. He then turns casually to discuss the dowry and points out a bronze statue of Neptune taming a sea-horse.

**The Ring and the Book (1868–69).** A long poem (over 21,000 lines, 12 books) based on a real Roman murder trial of **1698**, found in an "old yellow book" in Florence: Count **Guido Franceschini** murdered his young wife **Pompilia** and her adoptive parents after she fled with the priest **Caponsacchi**. The story is told again and again by different speakers — Guido, Pompilia, Caponsacchi, the lawyers, the Pope — showing how truth is seen from many viewpoints.

**The Pied Piper of Hamelin.** A children's poem: the town of Hamelin is plagued by rats; a mysterious piper in a coat of red and yellow pipes them into the river Weser; when the Mayor refuses to pay the promised thousand guilders, the Piper pipes away all the children of the town into a mountain.
:::

## 60.5 Matthew Arnold (1822–1888)

::: writer
**Father:** Dr. Thomas Arnold, famous headmaster of Rugby School · **Career:** **Inspector of Schools** for 35 years; **Professor of Poetry at Oxford** (1857–1867) · **Titles:** poet of Victorian doubt; the leading critic of the age; "the apostle of culture" · **Poems:** *The Strayed Reveller* (1849), *Empedocles on Etna* (1852), ***Dover Beach*** (1867), ***The Scholar-Gipsy*** (1853), *Thyrsis* (1866, elegy for Arthur Hugh Clough), ***Sohrab and Rustum*** (1853), *The Forsaken Merman*, *Stanzas from the Grande Chartreuse*, *Shakespeare* (sonnet), *Rugby Chapel*, *Requiescat* · **Prose:** ***Culture and Anarchy*** (1869), ***Essays in Criticism*** (1865, 1888), *On Translating Homer*, *Literature and Dogma*, *The Study of Poetry*, *The Function of Criticism at the Present Time*
:::

**Arnold** was a poet of **melancholy, doubt and the loss of faith** in a changing world, and a critic who sought to give literature and culture the role once played by religion. In ***Culture and Anarchy*** he defined **culture** as "**the best which has been thought and said in the world**", the pursuit of "**sweetness and light**" (a phrase from Swift), and divided English society into **Barbarians** (the aristocracy), **Philistines** (the narrow-minded middle class) and the **Populace** (the working class). He called poetry "**a criticism of life**", proposed the "**touchstone**" method (comparing passages with lines from the great masters), and demanded "**high seriousness**" (which he found lacking in Chaucer).

::: work
**Dover Beach (1867).** On a calm moonlit night at Dover, looking across the Channel to France, the speaker hears the "grating roar" of pebbles drawn back by the waves — an "eternal note of sadness" that Sophocles heard on the Aegean. The "**Sea of Faith**" was once full, but now he hears only "its melancholy, long, withdrawing roar". He turns to his beloved: in a world without joy, love, light, certitude, peace or help, all they can do is "**be true to one another**", for "we are here as on a darkling plain … / Where ignorant armies clash by night."

**Sohrab and Rustum (1853).** An episode from the Persian epic **Shahnameh** of **Firdausi**, told in blank verse: the great Persian warrior **Rustum** fights a young champion of the Tartars, **Sohrab**, not knowing he is his own son; he mortally wounds him and discovers the truth by a seal pricked on Sohrab's arm; the poem ends with the majestic flow of the river Oxus.

**The Scholar-Gipsy (1853).** Based on a legend of a poor Oxford scholar who left the university to join the gypsies and learn their secrets; he still wanders the Oxford countryside, untouched by "this strange disease of modern life, / With its sick hurry, its divided aims".
:::

::: quote
"Ah, love, let us be true / To one another!" — *Dover Beach*

"And we are here as on a darkling plain / Swept with confused alarms of struggle and flight, / Where ignorant armies clash by night." — *Dover Beach*

"Others abide our question. Thou art free." — *Shakespeare*

"Wandering between two worlds, one dead, / The other powerless to be born." — *Stanzas from the Grande Chartreuse*

"Culture … the best which has been thought and said in the world." — *Culture and Anarchy*
:::
