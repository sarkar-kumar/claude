## 49.1 Foreign Expressions in English

English has borrowed thousands of words and ready-made phrases from **Latin, French, Greek, Italian, German, Spanish, Arabic, Persian, Sanskrit/Hindi and Bengali**. Some are fully naturalised (*agenda, alibi, bonus, café*); others keep their foreign look and are usually printed in *italics* (*ad hoc, bona fide, coup d'état, raison d'être*). Questions such as *"What does 'sine die' mean?"* or *"'Persona non grata' means…"* appear frequently in BCS Preliminary, Bank and university admission tests, and many of these phrases occur in GRE/SAT reading passages and in legal and diplomatic English.

The lists below give the phrase, its origin, its meaning (with a Bangla equivalent) and an example.

::: gre
**SAT / GRE / GMAT Corner:** GRE passages regularly use *de facto, ad hoc, per se, a priori, ipso facto, bona fide, status quo, quid pro quo, sine qua non, laissez-faire, zeitgeist, hubris, nemesis, panacea, fait accompli* — usually without explanation. Learn their exact meanings: "The reform was no **panacea**" (no cure for everything); "The court's decision created a **de facto** ban" (a ban in practice, not in law).
:::
