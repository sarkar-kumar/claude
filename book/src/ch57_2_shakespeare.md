## 57.4 William Shakespeare (1564–1616)

::: writer
**Born:** Stratford-upon-Avon, Warwickshire; baptised **26 April 1564** (birthday traditionally **23 April**) · **Died:** **23 April 1616**, Stratford · **Buried:** Holy Trinity Church, Stratford · **Parents:** John Shakespeare (glover, alderman) and Mary Arden · **Wife:** **Anne Hathaway** (married 1582; she was 26, he 18) · **Children:** Susanna (1583), twins **Hamnet** and Judith (1585); Hamnet died in 1596, aged 11 · **Titles:** **The Bard of Avon**, the **Swan of Avon** (Ben Jonson), **National Poet of England**, "the myriad-minded" (Coleridge), "Soul of the Age" (Jonson) · **Works:** 37 plays (38–39 counting collaborations), 154 sonnets, 2 long narrative poems and a few short poems
:::

### 57.4.1 Life and Career

Shakespeare probably attended the King's New School at Stratford, where he learned Latin grammar and read Ovid, Virgil and Seneca — the "**small Latin and less Greek**" Ben Jonson teased him about. After his marriage, little is known of him between **1585 and 1592** (the "**lost years**"). By 1592 he was known in London as an actor and playwright — Robert Greene attacked him that year as an "upstart Crow". In **1594** he became a sharer in the **Lord Chamberlain's Men**, the leading acting company, with the actor **Richard Burbage**. The company built the **Globe Theatre** on the south bank of the Thames in **1599**; under **James I** (1603) it became the **King's Men**, and from 1608 it also used the indoor **Blackfriars** theatre. Shakespeare prospered, bought **New Place**, the second-largest house in Stratford (1597), and retired there around 1611–1613. In his will he left his wife his "**second-best bed**".

Seven years after his death, his fellow actors **John Heminges and Henry Condell** published the **First Folio (1623)**, containing **36 plays** (18 printed for the first time), with a famous engraved portrait by Martin Droeshout and Ben Jonson's tribute poem.

::: quote
"He was not of an age, but for all time!" — Ben Jonson, *To the Memory of My Beloved, the Author, Mr. William Shakespeare* (First Folio, 1623)

"Sweet Swan of Avon!" — Ben Jonson (same poem)

"Good friend for Jesus' sake forbear, / To dig the dust enclosed here." — inscription on Shakespeare's grave
:::

### 57.4.2 The Four Periods of His Work

| Period | Dates | Character | Main works |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Early / Apprenticeship** | c. 1589–1594 | experiment, imitation of Marlowe and the classics | *Henry VI* (1–3), *Richard III*, *The Comedy of Errors*, *Titus Andronicus*, *The Taming of the Shrew*, *The Two Gentlemen of Verona*, *Love's Labour's Lost*; poems *Venus and Adonis* (1593), *The Rape of Lucrece* (1594) |
| **2. Growth / Romantic comedies and histories** | c. 1594–1600 | joyful comedy, mature history | *Romeo and Juliet*, *A Midsummer Night's Dream*, *Richard II*, *King John*, *The Merchant of Venice*, *Henry IV* (1–2), *Much Ado About Nothing*, *Henry V*, *Julius Caesar*, *As You Like It*, *Twelfth Night*, *The Merry Wives of Windsor* |
| **3. Maturity / Great tragedies and problem plays** | c. 1600–1608 | darkness, evil, suffering | *Hamlet*, *Troilus and Cressida*, *All's Well That Ends Well*, *Measure for Measure*, *Othello*, *King Lear*, *Macbeth*, *Antony and Cleopatra*, *Coriolanus*, *Timon of Athens* |
| **4. Final / Romances** | c. 1608–1613 | reconciliation, forgiveness, restoration | *Pericles*, *Cymbeline*, *The Winter's Tale*, *The Tempest*, *Henry VIII* and *The Two Noble Kinsmen* (with John Fletcher) |

(The critic **Edward Dowden** named these periods: "In the workshop", "In the world", "Out of the depths", "On the heights".)

::: tip
**Exam facts:** First play — generally ***Henry VI, Part 1*** (some books: *The Comedy of Errors* or *Love's Labour's Lost*). Last complete play written alone — ***The Tempest*** (c. 1611). Longest play — ***Hamlet***. Shortest play — ***The Comedy of Errors***. Only play set in contemporary England — ***The Merry Wives of Windsor***. Play with Falstaff — *Henry IV* (1–2), *The Merry Wives of Windsor* (his death is reported in *Henry V*). Shakespeare's only tragedy of young love — *Romeo and Juliet*. Play whose title character dies in Act III — *Julius Caesar*. Shakespeare's "farewell to the stage" — *The Tempest* (Prospero breaking his staff).

### 57.4.4 Hamlet, Prince of Denmark (c. 1600–01)

::: work
**Summary.** **King Hamlet** of Denmark has died suddenly, and within two months his brother **Claudius** has taken the throne and married the widowed queen **Gertrude**. The young **Prince Hamlet**, a student at Wittenberg, is plunged into melancholy. The **Ghost** of his father appears on the battlements of **Elsinore** and tells him that Claudius murdered him by pouring poison into his ear as he slept in the orchard; it commands revenge.

Hamlet puts on an "**antic disposition**" (feigned madness). He rejects **Ophelia**, daughter of the old counsellor **Polonius**, whom he loved. Uncertain whether the Ghost is honest, he arranges for a troupe of travelling players to perform ***The Murder of Gonzago*** (which he renames ***The Mousetrap***) with a scene resembling the murder; Claudius's reaction confirms his guilt. Hamlet finds Claudius at prayer but spares him, not wanting to send his soul to heaven. In his mother's closet, he kills Polonius, who is hiding behind the **arras**, mistaking him for Claudius. Claudius sends Hamlet to England with his old schoolfellows **Rosencrantz and Guildenstern**, carrying a letter ordering his death; Hamlet discovers the plot, substitutes their names and escapes back to Denmark when pirates attack the ship.

Meanwhile Ophelia goes mad with grief and drowns. Her brother **Laertes** returns from France for revenge, and Claudius plots with him: in a fencing match, Laertes will use an unblunted, **poisoned foil**, and Claudius will offer Hamlet a **poisoned cup**. In the graveyard Hamlet meditates on death over the skull of **Yorick**, the old court jester, and then struggles with Laertes at Ophelia's grave. In the final duel, Gertrude drinks the poisoned wine and dies; Laertes wounds Hamlet, the foils are exchanged, and Hamlet wounds Laertes, who confesses the plot. Hamlet kills Claudius at last. Dying, he asks his loyal friend **Horatio** to tell his story. The Norwegian prince **Fortinbras** arrives and takes the throne.

**Characters:** Hamlet, Claudius, Gertrude, the Ghost, Polonius, Ophelia, Laertes, Horatio, Rosencrantz, Guildenstern, Osric, Marcellus, Bernardo, Francisco, Reynaldo, the Gravediggers, Yorick (skull), Fortinbras, the Players.

**Themes:** revenge and delay; appearance vs reality; madness; corruption ("something is rotten in the state of Denmark"); mortality; action vs thought. Hamlet's **delay** is one of the most debated problems in literature.
:::

::: quote

"The time is out of joint: O cursed spite, / That ever I was born to set it right!" (Hamlet, I.v)

"Brevity is the soul of wit." (Polonius, II.ii)

"The lady doth protest too much, methinks." (Gertrude, III.ii)

### 57.4.5 Macbeth (c. 1606)

::: work
**Summary.** Returning from victory over rebels and Norwegian invaders, the Scottish generals **Macbeth** (Thane of Glamis) and **Banquo** meet **three witches** (the **Weird Sisters**) on a heath. They hail Macbeth as **Thane of Cawdor** and **king hereafter**, and tell Banquo that his descendants will be kings. When King **Duncan** immediately makes Macbeth Thane of Cawdor, his ambition is awakened. Urged on by his wife, **Lady Macbeth**, who mocks his hesitation, Macbeth murders Duncan in his sleep at their castle (Inverness). Duncan's sons **Malcolm** and **Donalbain** flee to England and Ireland, and Macbeth is crowned.

Fearing the prophecy about Banquo, Macbeth has him murdered, but Banquo's son **Fleance** escapes; at a banquet, **Banquo's ghost** appears to Macbeth. He visits the witches again; apparitions warn him to "**beware Macduff**", assure him that "**none of woman born**" can harm him, and that he will not be vanquished until "**Great Birnam Wood to high Dunsinane Hill**" comes against him. Hearing that **Macduff** has fled to England, Macbeth has Macduff's wife and children slaughtered.

Lady Macbeth, tormented by guilt, walks in her sleep trying to wash imaginary blood from her hands ("**Out, damned spot!**"), and later dies, probably by suicide. Malcolm and Macduff return with an English army; the soldiers carry branches cut from **Birnam Wood** as camouflage. In the final fight, Macduff reveals that he was "**from his mother's womb untimely ripp'd**" (born by Caesarean birth). He kills Macbeth, and Malcolm becomes king.

**Characters:** Macbeth, Lady Macbeth, Duncan, Malcolm, Donalbain, Banquo, Fleance, Macduff, Lady Macduff, Ross, Lennox, Seyton, the Porter, the three Witches, Hecate, Siward.

**Themes:** ambition and its corruption; fate vs free will; guilt and conscience; appearance vs reality ("Fair is foul, and foul is fair"); the disorder of nature after regicide. **Shakespeare's shortest tragedy.**
:::

::: quote
"Fair is foul, and foul is fair." (Witches, I.i)

"Look like the innocent flower, / But be the serpent under't." (Lady Macbeth, I.v)

"Is this a dagger which I see before me?" (Macbeth, II.i)

"Macbeth does murder sleep." (Macbeth, II.ii)

"Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" (Macbeth, II.ii)

"Double, double toil and trouble; / Fire burn and cauldron bubble." (Witches, IV.i)

"Out, damned spot! out, I say!" (Lady Macbeth, V.i)

"All the perfumes of Arabia will not sweeten this little hand." (Lady Macbeth, V.i)

"Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow, / Creeps in this petty pace from day to day… / Life's but a walking shadow… / it is a tale / Told by an idiot, full of sound and fury, / Signifying nothing." (Macbeth, V.v)
:::

### 57.4.6 Othello, the Moor of Venice (c. 1603–04)

::: work
**Summary.** **Othello**, a noble **Moorish general** in the service of Venice, secretly marries **Desdemona**, daughter of the senator **Brabantio**. His ensign (standard-bearer) **Iago** hates him — partly because Othello promoted the young **Michael Cassio** as lieutenant instead of him, and partly from suspicion that Othello has slept with his wife **Emilia**. With the foolish **Roderigo**, who loves Desdemona, Iago wakes Brabantio, but Othello defends his marriage before the Duke, and Desdemona confirms her love.

In **Cyprus**, where Othello is sent to resist a Turkish fleet, Iago gets Cassio drunk so that he loses his position, then advises him to ask Desdemona to plead for him. At the same time he poisons Othello's mind, hinting that Desdemona is unfaithful with Cassio. The key "proof" is a **handkerchief** — Othello's first gift to Desdemona — which Emilia picks up and gives to Iago, who plants it in Cassio's room. Consumed by jealousy ("the green-eyed monster"), Othello strangles Desdemona in her bed. Emilia reveals Iago's villainy; Iago kills her. Realising the truth, Othello stabs himself, dying "upon a kiss". Iago is taken away to be tortured; Cassio becomes governor of Cyprus.

**Characters:** Othello, Desdemona, Iago, Emilia, Michael Cassio, Bianca, Roderigo, Brabantio, the Duke of Venice, Lodovico, Gratiano, Montano.

**Themes:** jealousy, deception, racial prejudice and the outsider, trust, honour, "motiveless malignity" (Coleridge's phrase for Iago).
:::

::: quote
"I am not what I am." (Iago, I.i)

"Put money in thy purse." (Iago, I.iii)

"O, beware, my lord, of jealousy; / It is the green-eyed monster which doth mock / The meat it feeds on." (Iago, III.iii)

"Who steals my purse steals trash… / But he that filches from me my good name / Robs me of that which not enriches him / And makes me poor indeed." (Iago, III.iii)

"Then must you speak / Of one that loved not wisely but too well." (Othello, V.ii)

"Put out the light, and then put out the light." (Othello, V.ii)
:::

### 57.4.7 King Lear (c. 1605–06)

::: work
**Summary.** The aged **King Lear** of Britain decides to divide his kingdom among his three daughters according to how much they say they love him. **Goneril** (wife of the Duke of **Albany**) and **Regan** (wife of the Duke of **Cornwall**) flatter him extravagantly; the youngest, **Cordelia**, honestly says she loves him "according to my bond; no more nor less". In a rage, Lear disinherits her; the **King of France** marries her without a dowry. The loyal Earl of **Kent** protests and is banished, but returns in disguise (as "Caius") to serve Lear.

Lear soon finds that Goneril and Regan treat him with contempt; they reduce his train of knights and shut him out of doors. In a terrible **storm on the heath**, accompanied by his **Fool**, Lear goes mad and learns humility and compassion for "poor naked wretches".

In a parallel **sub-plot**, the Earl of **Gloucester** is deceived by his illegitimate son **Edmund** into believing that his legitimate son **Edgar** plots against him. Edgar flees and disguises himself as **Poor Tom**, a mad beggar. When Gloucester helps Lear, Cornwall and Regan **blind** him. Edgar, still disguised, guides his blind father and saves him from despair at the "cliffs of Dover".

Cordelia lands with a French army to rescue her father; Lear and Cordelia are reconciled but captured. Edmund orders Cordelia to be hanged. Goneril poisons Regan (both love Edmund) and then kills herself; Edgar kills Edmund in single combat. Too late, Edmund repents and tries to save Cordelia. Lear enters carrying Cordelia's dead body and dies of grief. Albany, Kent and Edgar remain; Edgar is left to rule.

**Characters:** Lear, Goneril, Regan, Cordelia, the Duke of Albany, the Duke of Cornwall, the King of France, the Duke of Burgundy, the Earl of Kent, the Fool, the Earl of Gloucester, Edgar, Edmund, Oswald.

**Themes:** filial ingratitude; madness and wisdom; blindness and insight; justice and the gods; suffering and redemption; nature (natural vs unnatural bonds).
:::

::: quote
"Nothing will come of nothing: speak again." (Lear, I.i)

"How sharper than a serpent's tooth it is / To have a thankless child!" (Lear, I.iv)

"Blow, winds, and crack your cheeks! rage! blow!" (Lear, III.ii)

"I am a man / More sinned against than sinning." (Lear, III.ii)

"As flies to wanton boys are we to the gods, / They kill us for their sport." (Gloucester, IV.i)

"Men must endure / Their going hence, even as their coming hither: / Ripeness is all." (Edgar, V.ii)
:::

### 57.4.8 Romeo and Juliet (c. 1595)

::: work
**Summary.** In **Verona**, two noble families, the **Montagues** and the **Capulets**, are bitter enemies. **Romeo**, son of Montague, attends a Capulet feast in disguise and falls in love at first sight with **Juliet**, Capulet's thirteen-year-old daughter. That night, in the famous **balcony scene**, they declare their love, and the next day **Friar Laurence** secretly marries them, hoping to end the feud. Juliet's hot-tempered cousin **Tybalt** challenges Romeo; Romeo refuses to fight, but his friend **Mercutio** fights and is killed. Romeo then kills Tybalt and is **banished to Mantua**.

Juliet's parents arrange her marriage to **Count Paris**. To avoid it, Friar Laurence gives her a potion that will make her seem dead for 42 hours; he sends a letter to tell Romeo, but it fails to reach him (Friar John is quarantined). Romeo hears only that Juliet is dead, buys poison, and goes to the Capulet tomb, where he kills Paris and drinks the poison beside Juliet. She wakes, finds Romeo dead, and stabs herself with his dagger. The grieving families are reconciled.

**Characters:** Romeo, Juliet, Montague and Lady Montague, Capulet and Lady Capulet, the Nurse, Friar Laurence, Mercutio, Benvolio, Tybalt, Paris, Prince Escalus, Balthasar, Friar John, the Apothecary.

**Themes:** young love vs hatred; fate ("star-cross'd lovers"); haste; light and darkness.
:::

::: quote
"A pair of star-cross'd lovers take their life." (Prologue)

"O Romeo, Romeo! wherefore art thou Romeo?" (Juliet, II.ii)

"What's in a name? that which we call a rose / By any other name would smell as sweet." (Juliet, II.ii)

"Good night, good night! parting is such sweet sorrow." (Juliet, II.ii)

"A plague o' both your houses!" (Mercutio, III.i)
:::

### 57.4.9 Julius Caesar (1599)

::: work
**Summary.** **Julius Caesar** returns to Rome in triumph after defeating Pompey's sons. A soothsayer warns him to "**Beware the Ides of March**" (15 March). Fearing that Caesar will become king and destroy the Republic, **Cassius** persuades the noble **Marcus Brutus** to join a conspiracy. Ignoring the warnings of his wife **Calpurnia**, who has dreamed of his murder, Caesar goes to the Senate and is stabbed by the conspirators; seeing Brutus among them, he cries "**Et tu, Brute?**" Brutus explains to the people that he killed Caesar for the sake of Rome; but **Mark Antony**, permitted to speak at the funeral, turns the crowd against the conspirators with his masterly speech ("**Friends, Romans, countrymen**"), reading Caesar's will. Civil war follows. Antony and **Octavius** defeat Brutus and Cassius at **Philippi**; Caesar's ghost appears to Brutus. Cassius and Brutus both kill themselves. Antony praises Brutus as "**the noblest Roman of them all**".

**Characters:** Julius Caesar, Calpurnia, Mark Antony, Octavius Caesar, Lepidus, Marcus Brutus, Portia (Brutus's wife), Cassius, Casca, Cinna, Decius Brutus, Trebonius, the Soothsayer, Cicero.
:::

::: quote
"Beware the ides of March." (Soothsayer, I.ii)

"The fault, dear Brutus, is not in our stars, / But in ourselves, that we are underlings." (Cassius, I.ii)

"Cowards die many times before their deaths; / The valiant never taste of death but once." (Caesar, II.ii)

"Et tu, Brute? Then fall, Caesar!" (Caesar, III.i)

"Friends, Romans, countrymen, lend me your ears; / I come to bury Caesar, not to praise him." (Antony, III.ii)

"This was the most unkindest cut of all." (Antony, III.ii)

"There is a tide in the affairs of men, / Which, taken at the flood, leads on to fortune." (Brutus, IV.iii)

"This was the noblest Roman of them all." (Antony, V.v)
:::

### 57.4.10 Antony and Cleopatra (c. 1606)

::: work
**Summary.** **Mark Antony**, one of the three rulers (triumvirs) of the Roman world, neglects his duties in Egypt because of his passion for **Cleopatra**, Queen of Egypt. Called back to Rome, he marries **Octavia**, sister of **Octavius Caesar**, to heal a quarrel, but soon returns to Cleopatra. At the naval **Battle of Actium**, Cleopatra's ships flee and Antony follows her, losing the battle. Falsely told that Cleopatra is dead, Antony falls on his sword and dies in her arms. Cleopatra, refusing to be led in triumph through Rome, dies by the bite of an **asp** (a poisonous snake). **Characters:** Antony, Cleopatra, Octavius Caesar, Octavia, Enobarbus, Charmian, Iras, Lepidus, Pompey.
:::

::: quote
"Age cannot wither her, nor custom stale / Her infinite variety." (Enobarbus, II.ii)
:::

### 57.4.11 The Merchant of Venice (c. 1596–97)

::: work
**Summary.** **Bassanio**, a young Venetian, needs money to woo the rich heiress **Portia** of **Belmont**. His friend, the merchant **Antonio**, whose wealth is tied up in ships at sea, borrows **3,000 ducats** for him from the Jewish moneylender **Shylock**, who hates Antonio for insulting him. Shylock demands as security a "**pound of flesh**" from Antonio's body if the loan is not repaid in three months. At Belmont, suitors must choose among **gold, silver and lead caskets**; Bassanio chooses the **lead** casket and wins Portia. His friend **Gratiano** marries Portia's maid **Nerissa**. Shylock's daughter **Jessica** elopes with **Lorenzo**, taking her father's money.

Antonio's ships are reported lost, and Shylock demands his bond. In court, **Portia**, disguised as a young lawyer, **Balthazar**, pleads for mercy ("**The quality of mercy is not strain'd**"); when Shylock refuses, she rules that he may take exactly a pound of flesh but **not one drop of blood**. Shylock is defeated, loses half his wealth and is forced to become a Christian. In the comic ring plot, Portia and Nerissa test their husbands with the rings they gave them. Antonio's ships return safely.

**Characters:** Antonio, Bassanio, Portia, Nerissa, Gratiano, Shylock, Jessica, Lorenzo, Launcelot Gobbo, Tubal, Salerio, Solanio, the Duke of Venice, the Prince of Morocco, the Prince of Arragon.
:::

::: quote
"All that glisters is not gold." (Scroll in the gold casket, II.vii)

"If you prick us, do we not bleed?" (Shylock, III.i)

"The quality of mercy is not strain'd, / It droppeth as the gentle rain from heaven." (Portia, IV.i)

"How far that little candle throws his beams! / So shines a good deed in a naughty world." (Portia, V.i)
:::

### 57.4.12 As You Like It (c. 1599)

::: work
**Summary.** **Duke Senior** has been banished by his usurping younger brother **Duke Frederick** and lives in the **Forest of Arden** with loyal lords, including the melancholy **Jaques**. His daughter **Rosalind** remains at court with her cousin **Celia**, Frederick's daughter, until Frederick banishes her too. Rosalind falls in love with **Orlando**, who defeats the court wrestler but is persecuted by his elder brother **Oliver**. Rosalind (disguised as a young man, **Ganymede**) and Celia (as **Aliena**) flee to Arden with the clown **Touchstone**. There Rosalind, in disguise, "cures" Orlando of love by teaching him to woo her. Oliver repents and marries Celia; Frederick is converted by a hermit and restores the dukedom. Four couples (including Touchstone and Audrey, Silvius and Phebe) are married. Based on Lodge's *Rosalynde*.

**Characters:** Rosalind, Orlando, Celia, Duke Senior, Duke Frederick, Oliver, Jaques, Touchstone, Adam, Silvius, Phebe, Audrey, Corin, William.
:::

::: quote
"All the world's a stage, / And all the men and women merely players: / They have their exits and their entrances; / And one man in his time plays many parts, / His acts being seven ages." (Jaques, II.vii)

"Sweet are the uses of adversity." (Duke Senior, II.i)
:::

### 57.4.13 Twelfth Night, or What You Will (c. 1601)

::: work
**Summary.** Shipwrecked on the coast of **Illyria**, **Viola** believes her twin brother **Sebastian** has drowned. Disguised as a young man, **Cesario**, she serves **Duke Orsino**, who is in love with the Countess **Olivia**; Viola secretly falls in love with Orsino. Sent to woo Olivia for the Duke, Viola is loved by Olivia instead. In the comic sub-plot, Olivia's uncle **Sir Toby Belch**, the foolish **Sir Andrew Aguecheek** and the maid **Maria** trick the pompous steward **Malvolio** with a forged letter into believing Olivia loves him; he appears in **yellow stockings, cross-gartered**, and is locked up as mad. Sebastian arrives, is mistaken for Cesario and marries Olivia; the twins are reunited and Orsino marries Viola. The clown **Feste** sings throughout.

**Characters:** Viola, Sebastian, Orsino, Olivia, Malvolio, Sir Toby Belch, Sir Andrew Aguecheek, Maria, Feste, Antonio, Fabian.
:::

::: quote
"If music be the food of love, play on." (Orsino, I.i)

"Some are born great, some achieve greatness, and some have greatness thrust upon 'em." (Malvolio reading the letter, II.v)
:::

### 57.4.14 A Midsummer Night's Dream (c. 1595)

::: work
**Summary.** In Athens, Duke **Theseus** prepares to marry **Hippolyta**, Queen of the Amazons. **Hermia** loves **Lysander**, but her father wants her to marry **Demetrius**, who is loved by **Helena**. Hermia and Lysander flee into the wood, followed by Demetrius and Helena. In the wood, **Oberon**, King of the Fairies, quarrelling with his queen **Titania**, orders the mischievous **Puck (Robin Goodfellow)** to use a love-juice from a magic flower; Puck mistakes the lovers, causing comic confusion. Titania, under the spell, falls in love with the weaver **Bottom**, whom Puck has given an **ass's head**. Order is restored; the couples are paired correctly (Lysander–Hermia, Demetrius–Helena), and Bottom and his fellow workmen ("the rude mechanicals") perform the comic play of **Pyramus and Thisbe** at the triple wedding.

**Characters:** Theseus, Hippolyta, Hermia, Lysander, Helena, Demetrius, Egeus, Oberon, Titania, Puck, Bottom, Peter Quince, Flute, Snout, Snug, Starveling.
:::

::: quote
"The course of true love never did run smooth." (Lysander, I.i)

"Lord, what fools these mortals be!" (Puck, III.ii)
:::

### 57.4.15 Much Ado About Nothing (c. 1598–99)

::: work
**Summary.** In Messina, the young lord **Claudio** loves **Hero**, daughter of **Leonato**. The villain **Don John** tricks Claudio into believing Hero is unfaithful, and Claudio denounces her at the wedding. She is reported dead while her innocence is proved by the bumbling constable **Dogberry**'s watchmen. Meanwhile the witty, quarrelling **Beatrice and Benedick**, each tricked by friends into believing the other is in love, discover their love. Hero is "revived" and marries Claudio.

**Characters:** Beatrice, Benedick, Hero, Claudio, Don Pedro, Don John, Leonato, Dogberry, Borachio, Margaret.
:::

### 57.4.16 The Tempest (c. 1610–11)

::: work
**Summary.** **Prospero**, the rightful **Duke of Milan**, was overthrown twelve years earlier by his brother **Antonio** with the help of **Alonso**, King of Naples, and set adrift with his baby daughter **Miranda**. They reached an island, where Prospero, through his books of **magic**, freed the spirit **Ariel** (imprisoned by the witch **Sycorax**) and enslaved Sycorax's son **Caliban**. Now, when his enemies sail near the island, Prospero raises a **tempest** that wrecks their ship, scattering them unharmed on the island. Alonso's son **Ferdinand** meets Miranda and they fall in love; Prospero tests Ferdinand with hard labour. Antonio and **Sebastian** (Alonso's brother) plot to kill Alonso; Caliban plots with the drunken **Stephano** and **Trinculo** against Prospero. Ariel foils all the plots. Prospero finally **forgives** his enemies, recovers his dukedom, blesses the marriage of Ferdinand and Miranda, frees Ariel, **breaks his staff and drowns his book**, and asks the audience for release in the Epilogue — often read as Shakespeare's **farewell to the stage**.

**Characters:** Prospero, Miranda, Ariel, Caliban, Ferdinand, Alonso, Antonio, Sebastian, Gonzalo, Stephano, Trinculo, Sycorax (mentioned).
:::

::: quote
"O brave new world, / That has such people in't!" (Miranda, V.i) — source of Huxley's title *Brave New World*

"We are such stuff / As dreams are made on, and our little life / Is rounded with a sleep." (Prospero, IV.i)

"Full fathom five thy father lies." (Ariel's song, I.ii)

"Misery acquaints a man with strange bedfellows." (Trinculo, II.ii)
:::

### 57.4.18 Poems and Sonnets

- ***Venus and Adonis*** (1593) — narrative poem (six-line stanzas); dedicated to **Henry Wriothesley, Earl of Southampton**; his first published work.
- ***The Rape of Lucrece*** (1594) — narrative poem in **rhyme royal**; also dedicated to Southampton.
- ***The Phoenix and the Turtle*** (1601) — a short allegorical poem on ideal love.
- ***Sonnets*** (published **1609** by Thomas Thorpe) — **154 sonnets** in the **Shakespearean (English) form**: three quatrains and a couplet (*abab cdcd efef gg*). Sonnets **1–126** are addressed to a beautiful young man (**the Fair Youth**), urging him to marry and celebrating poetry's power to make him immortal; **127–152** concern a **Dark Lady** who is unfaithful; some mention a **Rival Poet**. The book is dedicated to the mysterious "**Mr. W. H.**", the "onlie begetter" — perhaps Henry Wriothesley or William Herbert, Earl of Pembroke. *A Lover's Complaint* was printed with them.

::: quote
"Shall I compare thee to a summer's day? / Thou art more lovely and more temperate." (Sonnet 18)

"So long as men can breathe or eyes can see, / So long lives this, and this gives life to thee." (Sonnet 18)

"When, in disgrace with fortune and men's eyes, / I all alone beweep my outcast state." (Sonnet 29)

"Not marble, nor the gilded monuments / Of princes, shall outlive this powerful rhyme." (Sonnet 55)

"Let me not to the marriage of true minds / Admit impediments. Love is not love / Which alters when it alteration finds." (Sonnet 116)

"My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun." (Sonnet 130)
:::

### 57.4.19 Shakespeare's Contribution and Critical Opinions

- He enriched English with about **1,700 words** first recorded in his works (*assassination, bedroom, lonely, critic, eyeball, swagger, gloomy*) and countless phrases (*break the ice, wild-goose chase, in my mind's eye, the be-all and end-all, a sorry sight, love is blind, too much of a good thing, vanish into thin air, dead as a doornail*).
- He perfected **blank verse**, mixed **tragic and comic** elements, created deep **psychological characters**, and used **soliloquies** to reveal inner thought.
- **Dr. Johnson**: Shakespeare is "the poet of nature … that holds up to his readers a faithful mirror of manners and of life." **Coleridge**: "myriad-minded". **Milton**: "Sweetest Shakespeare, Fancy's child, / Warble his native wood-notes wild" (*L'Allegro*). **Matthew Arnold**: "Others abide our question. Thou art free." **Carlyle**: "Indian Empire will go … but this Shakespeare does not go."
- **Authorship question**: Some have claimed that Bacon, the Earl of Oxford or Marlowe wrote his plays; scholars reject these theories.
