# Classification of Sentence (By Structure) {#ch33}

[Part A · Grammar]{.chpart}
:::

## 33.1 The Four Structural Types

According to **structure** (the number and kind of clauses), sentences are of four kinds:

| Kind | Structure | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Simple** | one independent clause (one subject + one finite verb) | He is poor. / Being ill, he could not come. |
| **Compound** | two or more independent (coordinate) clauses joined by a coordinating conjunction or semicolon | He is poor, **but** he is honest. |
| **Complex** | one independent (main) clause + one or more subordinate clauses | **Though** he is poor, he is honest. |
| **Compound-Complex (Mixed)** | two or more independent clauses + at least one subordinate clause | He is poor **but** he is honest **because** his parents taught him well. |

## 33.2 The Simple Sentence

A **simple sentence** has **only one finite verb** (one main clause). It may have phrases (infinitive, participial, gerund, prepositional), compound subjects and compound predicates, but no subordinate clause.

::: ex
- The sun **rises** in the east.
- **Rahim and Karim** went to school. (compound subject — still one clause)
- **Seeing the police**, the thief ran away. (participial phrase)
- **In spite of his illness**, he attended the class. (prepositional phrase)
- He is **too weak to walk**. (infinitive phrase)
- **Having finished the work**, he went home.
:::

::: note
**Compound predicate.** In *He* **came** *and* **sat** *beside me*, one subject has two finite verbs joined by *and*. Modern grammars call this a simple sentence with a compound predicate, but traditional Bangladeshi exam keys usually treat it as a **compound sentence** (two finite verbs = two clauses, the second subject being understood).
:::

::: tip
**Exam Tip — count the finite verbs.** Underline every finite verb (a verb that shows tense and agrees with a subject). One finite verb (one clause) → simple. Infinitives (*to go*), gerunds and participles (*going, gone, having gone*) are **not** finite and do not count.
:::

## 33.3 The Compound Sentence

A **compound sentence** consists of **two or more independent (principal/coordinate) clauses**, each of which can stand alone. They are joined by:

1. **Coordinating conjunctions (FANBOYS):** *for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so*
2. **Correlatives:** *either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also, both…and*
3. **Conjunctive adverbs with a semicolon:** *; however, ; therefore, ; otherwise, ; moreover, ; still, ; else, ; hence, ; consequently*
4. **A semicolon alone**

::: ex
- He worked hard, **and** he passed.
- She is rich, **but** she is not happy.
- Hurry up, **or** you will miss the train.
- He was tired, **so** he went to bed.
- He is poor, **yet** he is honest.
- **Not only** is he a poet, **but** he is **also** a painter.
- **Either** you come here **or** I go there.
- He was ill; **therefore**, he could not attend.
- The night was dark; the wind was howling.
:::

### 33.3.1 Kinds of Compound Sentences (by Conjunction)

| Kind | Conjunctions | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Cumulative (addition)** | and, both…and, not only…but also, as well as | He is clever **and** he is diligent. |
| **Adversative (contrast)** | but, yet, still, nevertheless, however, whereas | He is rich, **yet** he is unhappy. |
| **Alternative (choice)** | or, either…or, neither…nor, otherwise, else | Work hard, **otherwise** you will fail. |
| **Illative (result/inference)** | so, therefore, hence, for, consequently | He was ill, **so** he did not come. |

## 33.4 The Complex Sentence

A **complex sentence** consists of **one principal (main) clause** and **one or more subordinate clauses**. The subordinate clause is introduced by a **subordinating conjunction** or a **relative pronoun/adverb**.

::: ex
- **Though he is poor**, he is honest. (adverb clause — concession)
- I know **that he is honest**. (noun clause)
- The man **who came here** is my uncle. (adjective clause)
- **If you work hard**, you will succeed. (adverb clause — condition)
- He is so weak **that he cannot walk**. (adverb clause — result)
- **When I reached the station**, the train had left. (adverb clause — time)
- This is the house **where I was born**. (adjective clause)
- **What he said** is true. (noun clause)
- I waited **until he came** **because I wanted to see him**. (two subordinate clauses)
:::

### 33.4.1 Identifying the Principal Clause

The **principal clause** can stand alone and make sense: in *Though he is poor, he is honest*, the principal clause is *he is honest*. The subordinate clause (*Though he is poor*) cannot stand alone.

## 33.5 The Compound-Complex (Mixed) Sentence

A **compound-complex sentence** has **at least two independent clauses** and **at least one subordinate clause**.

::: ex
- **He is poor, but he is happy** **because he is honest**.
  (Independent: *He is poor*, *he is happy*; Subordinate: *because he is honest*)
- **I went to the station when he called me, but the train had already left.**
- **The man who lives next door is a doctor, and his wife is a teacher.**
- **If you study hard, you will pass, and your parents will be proud of you.**
:::

## 33.6 Clause Analysis — Step by Step

To analyse a sentence:

1. **Underline all finite verbs.** Each finite verb = one clause.
2. **Find the conjunctions / relative words** that introduce each clause.
3. **Separate independent clauses** (joined by coordinators or standing alone) from **dependent clauses** (introduced by subordinators or relatives).
4. **Decide the type:** 1 independent = simple; 2+ independent = compound; 1 independent + subordinate(s) = complex; 2+ independent + subordinate(s) = compound-complex.
5. For each subordinate clause, state its **kind** (noun/adjective/adverb) and its **function**.

::: ex
**Sentence:** *When the bell rang, the students who were waiting outside entered the hall, and the examination began.*

- Finite verbs: *rang, were waiting, entered, began* → 4 clauses.
- *the students … entered the hall* — independent clause 1.
- *the examination began* — independent clause 2 (joined by *and*).
- *When the bell rang* — subordinate adverb clause of time, modifying *entered*.
- *who were waiting outside* — subordinate adjective clause, qualifying *students*.
- **Type: compound-complex.**
:::

## 33.7 Identification Practice

| Sentence | Type | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| He came here to see me. | Simple | one finite verb (*came*); *to see* is an infinitive |
| He came here and saw me. | Compound (exam key) | two finite verbs joined by *and* |
| He came here so that he might see me. | Complex | *so that* introduces a subordinate clause |
| He came here, and he saw me, though I was busy. | Compound-complex | two independent + one subordinate |
| Being tired, he slept early. | Simple | *being* is a participle |
| As he was tired, he slept early. | Complex | *as* + clause |
| He was tired, so he slept early. | Compound | *so* (coordinating) |
| In spite of being rich, he is unhappy. | Simple | phrase, no subordinate clause |
| Although he is rich, he is unhappy. | Complex | *although* + clause |
| He is rich, but he is unhappy. | Compound | *but* |
| I know the man who stole the bag. | Complex | relative clause |
| I know the thief. | Simple | one clause |
| Do or die. | Compound | two imperative clauses with *or* |
| Unless you work hard, you will fail. | Complex | *unless* clause |
| Work hard, or you will fail. | Compound | *or* |
| By working hard, you will succeed. | Simple | gerund phrase |

## 33.8 Common Mistakes in Structure

::: errs
1. ✘ **Although** he is poor, **but** he is honest. (mixes complex and compound markers) ✔ **Although** he is poor, he is honest. / He is poor, **but** he is honest.
2. ✘ **Because** he was ill, **so** he did not come. ✔ **Because** he was ill, he did not come. / He was ill, **so** he did not come.
3. ✘ He was ill, he did not come. (comma splice) ✔ He was ill**;** he did not come. / He was ill, **so** he did not come.
4. ✘ When I reached home. (fragment) ✔ When I reached home, I found my mother waiting.
5. ✘ **Since** it was late **therefore** we left. ✔ **Since** it was late, we left.
:::

::: gre
**SAT / GRE / GMAT Corner — Sentence Structure and Clarity:** GMAT Sentence Correction rewards **clear structure**: a single main clause with well-placed modifiers is usually better than a chain of loosely joined clauses. Too many *and*s ("and … and … and") or a misplaced subordinate clause can make an answer choice wrong even if every word is grammatical. The SAT tests **joining clauses** (comma + FANBOYS, semicolon, colon) and **avoiding fragments**. Knowing exactly how many independent clauses a sentence has is the key skill for both.
:::
