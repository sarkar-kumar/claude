## 55.1 Background (c. 450–1066)

In the fifth century, after the Romans withdrew from Britain (c. 410), Germanic tribes — the **Angles, Saxons and Jutes** — crossed the North Sea and settled in the island. Their language, **Old English** (*Englisc*, also called **Anglo-Saxon**), is the ancestor of modern English, and the land came to be called *Engla-land* ("land of the Angles"). Christianity arrived with **St Augustine of Canterbury (597)** in the south and Irish missionaries in the north, bringing Latin learning and writing.

Old English literature was at first **oral**: poems were composed and recited by professional minstrels called **scops** (court poets) and **gleemen**. Only later were they written down by monks. Most surviving Old English poetry is found in **four manuscripts**:

| Manuscript | Contents |
|---|---|
| **The Beowulf Manuscript** (Nowell Codex, Cotton Vitellius A.xv, British Library) | *Beowulf*, *Judith* |
| **The Exeter Book** (Exeter Cathedral) | *The Wanderer*, *The Seafarer*, *Widsith*, *Deor*, *The Wife's Lament*, *The Ruin*, riddles, Cynewulf's *Christ II* and *Juliana* |
| **The Vercelli Book** (Vercelli, Italy) | *The Dream of the Rood*, Cynewulf's *Elene* and *The Fates of the Apostles*, *Andreas* |
| **The Junius Manuscript** (Bodleian Library, Oxford) | *Genesis*, *Exodus*, *Daniel*, *Christ and Satan* (once wrongly called "Caedmonian") |

### 55.1.1 Features of Old English Poetry

- **Alliteration** instead of rhyme: each line has four stressed syllables, divided by a **caesura** (pause) into two half-lines linked by alliteration.
- **Kennings**: compound metaphors — *whale-road* or *swan-road* (sea), *bone-house* (body), *battle-light* (sword), *word-hoard* (vocabulary), *sky-candle* (sun).
- **Heroic ideals**: loyalty of the warrior (*thane*) to his lord, courage, glory, generosity of the "ring-giver" king, revenge, and *wyrd* (fate).
- **Elegiac mood**: lament for transience, exile and lost glory (*ubi sunt* — "where are they now?").
- **Christian colouring** added to older pagan material.

## 55.2 Caedmon (fl. 658–680)

::: writer
**Known as:** the first English poet known by name · **Monastery:** Whitby, Northumbria (under Abbess Hild) · **Work:** *Caedmon's Hymn* · **Source:** Bede's *Ecclesiastical History*, Book IV
:::

**Caedmon** was an illiterate cowherd at the monastery of **Whitby**. According to **Bede**, he used to leave the feast whenever the harp was passed round, ashamed that he could not sing. One night an angel (a "man") appeared in a dream and commanded, *"Caedmon, sing me something."* When Caedmon protested that he could not, he was told to sing **"the beginning of created things"**, and he at once composed verses in praise of God the Creator. Abbess **Hild** recognised the gift as divine; Caedmon became a monk and turned scripture stories into English verse.

Only one poem certainly by him survives — the nine-line **Caedmon's Hymn**, the **oldest recorded poem in English**. Other biblical poems once attributed to him (*Genesis, Exodus, Daniel*) are now called "Caedmonian" only by tradition.

::: quote
"Nu sculon herigean heofonrices weard…" — "Now we must praise the Guardian of the heavenly kingdom, the might of the Creator and His purpose…" (*Caedmon's Hymn*)
:::

## 55.3 Cynewulf (fl. late 8th–9th century)

::: writer
**Known as:** the only Old English poet who **signed** his work · **Signature:** his name woven into the poems in **runic letters** · **Signed poems:** *Elene*, *Juliana*, *The Fates of the Apostles*, *Christ II (The Ascension)*
:::

**Cynewulf** is the second Old English poet known by name. We know almost nothing about his life, but he inserted his name in **runes** near the end of four religious poems, asking readers to pray for him. His poetry is Christian and learned:

- ***Elene*** — the finding of the True Cross by **St Helena** (Elene), mother of Emperor Constantine; the most accomplished of his poems.
- ***Juliana*** — the martyrdom of St Juliana, who resists a pagan suitor and the devil.
- ***The Fates of the Apostles*** — short account of how the twelve apostles died.
- ***Christ II (The Ascension)*** — on Christ's ascension into heaven.

Other poems such as *The Dream of the Rood*, *Andreas* and *Guthlac* were once attributed to the "school of Cynewulf".

## 55.4 The Venerable Bede (c. 673–735)

::: writer
**Known as:** the Venerable Bede; **Father of English History** · **Monastery:** Jarrow (Northumbria) · **Language of works:** Latin · **Masterpiece:** *Historia Ecclesiastica Gentis Anglorum* (*The Ecclesiastical History of the English People*, 731)
:::

**Bede** was a monk-scholar who spent nearly all his life at the twin monasteries of **Wearmouth and Jarrow**. He wrote about sixty works on scripture, science, time-reckoning and history — in **Latin**. His ***Ecclesiastical History of the English People*** (completed **731**) traces the history of England from Julius Caesar's invasion to 731, with special attention to the spread of Christianity. It is our main source for early English history and preserves the story of Caedmon. Bede popularised the practice of dating events from the birth of Christ (**AD / Anno Domini**). He was translated into Old English in King Alfred's time. He is buried in Durham Cathedral and was declared a Doctor of the Church in 1899.

::: quote
"The present life of man upon earth … seems to me like the swift flight of a sparrow through the hall where you sit at supper in winter … flying in at one door and immediately out at another." — Bede, *Ecclesiastical History* (a counsellor's words to King Edwin)
:::

::: note
**Don't confuse:** ***Adam Bede*** (1859) is a **novel by George Eliot** (Victorian period), about a carpenter named Adam Bede, Hetty Sorrel, Arthur Donnithorne and the preacher Dinah Morris. It has no connection with the Venerable Bede. Exams sometimes place them together to test this distinction.
:::

## 55.5 Alfred the Great (849–899)

::: writer
**Reign:** King of Wessex, 871–899 · **Known as:** Father of English Prose (Old English) · **Achievements:** resisted the Danish (Viking) invasions; promoted learning; translated Latin works into English; the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* began under him
:::

**King Alfred** is the only English king called "the Great". After defeating the Danes at the **Battle of Edington (878)** and reaching the treaty that created the Danelaw, he set out to restore learning, which had declined because of Viking raids. He gathered scholars at his court and either translated or sponsored the translation of important Latin books into English, believing that people should be able to read in their own language:

- Pope Gregory the Great's ***Pastoral Care*** (*Cura Pastoralis*) — with Alfred's famous preface on the decline of learning.
- **Boethius**'s ***The Consolation of Philosophy*** (*De Consolatione Philosophiae*).
- **Orosius**'s ***History against the Pagans*** — with added accounts of the voyages of Ohthere and Wulfstan.
- Bede's ***Ecclesiastical History*** (Old English version, made in his time).
- St Augustine's ***Soliloquies*** and the first fifty **Psalms**.

The ***Anglo-Saxon Chronicle***, a year-by-year record of English history, was begun around **890** in his reign and continued in various monasteries until **1154** (the Peterborough Chronicle). Because of this work Alfred is called the **Father of English Prose**.

## 55.6 Beowulf

::: writer
**Type:** Old English heroic epic · **Length:** 3,182 lines · **Author:** unknown · **Date:** composed between c. 700 and c. 1000 (manuscript c. 1000) · **Setting:** Scandinavia (Denmark and Geatland, southern Sweden) · **Manuscript:** Cotton Vitellius A.xv (Nowell Codex), British Library
:::

***Beowulf*** is the **oldest surviving epic in English** and the greatest work of Old English literature. Although composed in England, it tells a story of the **Danes** and the **Geats** of southern Scandinavia in the sixth century. It blends pagan heroic values with Christian comment.

::: work
**Summary.**

**Part 1 — Grendel.** **Hrothgar**, King of the Danes, builds a magnificent mead-hall called **Heorot**. The sounds of feasting anger **Grendel**, a monster descended from **Cain**, who lives in the marshes. For **twelve years** Grendel attacks the hall at night and devours Hrothgar's warriors. **Beowulf**, a young warrior of the **Geats** and nephew of King **Hygelac**, sails with fourteen companions to help. At a feast he is taunted by **Unferth** about a swimming contest with **Breca**, and replies proudly. That night Grendel attacks; Beowulf, fighting **without weapons**, tears off the monster's arm, and Grendel flees to die.

**Part 2 — Grendel's Mother.** Grendel's mother comes to Heorot to avenge her son and kills Hrothgar's dear counsellor **Aeschere**. Beowulf follows her to her lair beneath a dark mere (lake). Unferth lends him the sword **Hrunting**, but it fails; Beowulf kills her with a **giant's sword** found in the cave, then cuts off the head of Grendel's corpse. He returns in triumph, is richly rewarded, and Hrothgar warns him against pride.

**Part 3 — The Dragon.** Beowulf returns home, later becomes **King of the Geats** and rules well for **fifty years**. A thief steals a cup from a dragon's hoard, and the **dragon** burns the land. The aged Beowulf fights it; all his companions flee except the young **Wiglaf**. Together they kill the dragon, but Beowulf is fatally wounded. He dies, giving Wiglaf his armour. His body is burned on a funeral pyre and a barrow (mound) is built on a headland, where the treasure is buried with him. The poem ends with praise of Beowulf as *"the kindest of men … and most eager for fame."*

**Characters:** Beowulf, Hrothgar (King of the Danes), Wealhtheow (his queen), Hygelac (King of the Geats), Unferth, Aeschere, Grendel, Grendel's mother, the Dragon, Wiglaf, Breca, Hrunting (sword), Heorot (hall).

**Themes:** heroism and loyalty; fame and mortality; the struggle of good against evil; the role of fate (*wyrd*) and God; the transience of earthly glory.
:::

::: quote
"Wyrd oft nereð / unfǣgne eorl, / þonne his ellen dēah." — "Fate often saves an undoomed man when his courage is good." (*Beowulf*)

"Each of us must abide the end of our life on this earth; let him who can win glory before death." (*Beowulf*, Beowulf to Hrothgar)
:::

::: note
**Translations to know:** Seamus Heaney's verse translation of *Beowulf* (1999) won the Whitbread Book of the Year award; J. R. R. Tolkien's lecture "*Beowulf: The Monsters and the Critics*" (1936) changed how scholars read the poem.
:::

## 55.7 Works of Unknown Poets of the Old English Period

| Work | Type | Content |
|---|---|---|
| ***Widsith*** | heroic catalogue | A wandering scop ("far-traveller") lists the kings and peoples he has visited; one of the oldest English poems. |
| ***Deor*** | lyric / elegy | A scop who has lost his place at court consoles himself with examples of others' misfortunes; refrain: *"Þæs ofereode, þisses swa mæg"* ("That passed away; so may this"). Often called the first English lyric. |
| ***The Wanderer*** | elegy | A lordless exile mourns his dead lord and comrades; the *ubi sunt* theme: "Where has the horse gone? Where the rider?" |
| ***The Seafarer*** | elegy | A sailor describes the hardships of the sea and his longing for it; ends in Christian reflection. (Translated by Ezra Pound, 1911.) |
| ***The Wife's Lament*** | elegy | A woman separated from her husband laments her exile. |
| ***The Ruin*** | elegy | Describes a ruined Roman city (probably Bath) and the passing of glory. |
| ***The Dream of the Rood*** | Christian dream-vision | The Cross (*rood*) speaks, describing the Crucifixion; Christ appears as a young heroic warrior. Lines from it are carved on the **Ruthwell Cross**. |
| ***The Battle of Maldon*** | heroic poem | The defeat of Byrhtnoth and the English by Vikings in **991**; famous for loyalty to the death. |
| ***The Battle of Brunanburh*** | heroic poem | King **Athelstan**'s victory over Scots and Norsemen in **937**; preserved in the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*. |
| ***Judith*** | biblical epic | Judith beheads the Assyrian general Holofernes. |
| ***Genesis, Exodus, Daniel*** | biblical paraphrases | In the Junius Manuscript (once "Caedmonian"). |
| **Riddles** | Exeter Book | About 90 riddles on everyday objects and nature. |

### 55.7.1 Old English Prose after Alfred

- **Ælfric** (c. 955–1010), abbot of Eynsham — the greatest prose writer of the period: *Catholic Homilies*, *Lives of Saints*, a Latin grammar (*Ælfric's Grammar*) and a *Colloquy*.
- **Wulfstan** (d. 1023), Archbishop of York — *Sermo Lupi ad Anglos* (*Sermon of the Wolf to the English*, 1014), a fiery sermon blaming the Danish invasions on English sins.
- ***The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*** — continued to 1154.

::: quote
"Hwær cwom mearg? Hwær cwom mago? Hwær cwom maþþumgyfa?" — "Where has the horse gone? Where the young warrior? Where the giver of treasure?" (*The Wanderer*)
:::

**SAT / GRE Corner:** The Old English word stock is the core of everyday English (*man, wife, child, house, eat, drink, love, good, bad, God, heaven, earth*), while Latin and French borrowings dominate the learned vocabulary tested in the GRE. Knowing that **kenning**, **alliteration**, **caesura** and **elegy** come from this tradition helps with literary-terms questions (Chapter 63).
