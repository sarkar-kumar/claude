## 58.3 B. The Augustan Period (1700–1745)

### 58.3.1 Background

The name **Augustan** compares the reign of **Queen Anne** (1702–1714) and the early Hanoverian kings with the golden age of Latin literature under the Roman emperor **Augustus**, when Virgil, Horace and Ovid wrote. The writers admired classical order and correctness, and they saw themselves as guardians of good taste. **Satire** flourished, because party politics (Whigs and Tories) was fierce and writers were often party propagandists. The **coffee-houses** of London were centres of literary and political discussion, and the **periodical essay** (*The Tatler*, *The Spectator*) was born. The **novel** emerged with Defoe and Richardson, written for the growing middle-class reading public. The **Scriblerus Club** (Pope, Swift, Gay, Arbuthnot, Parnell) satirised pedantry and false learning.

### 58.3.2 Alexander Pope (1688–1744)

::: writer
**Titles:** the greatest poet of the Augustan Age (**Age of Pope**); master of the **heroic couplet** · **Life:** born a Roman Catholic (excluded from university and public office); a childhood illness (Pott's disease, tuberculosis of the spine) left him only 4 ft 6 in tall and in constant pain; lived at **Twickenham**, where he built a famous grotto · **Wealth:** the first English poet to live comfortably on his earnings (from his translations of Homer)
:::

**Major works:**

| Work | Year | Type |
|---|---|---|
| *Pastorals* | 1709 | four pastoral poems |
| ***An Essay on Criticism*** | 1711 | didactic poem on the rules of good writing and criticism |
| ***The Rape of the Lock*** | 1712 (2 cantos); 1714 (5 cantos) | **mock-heroic (mock-epic)** poem |
| *Windsor Forest* | 1713 | topographical poem |
| *Eloisa to Abelard*; *Elegy to the Memory of an Unfortunate Lady* | 1717 | emotional poems |
| Translations of Homer's ***Iliad*** (1715–20) and ***Odyssey*** (1725–26) | — | heroic couplets |
| ***The Dunciad*** | 1728; revised 1742–43 | mock-epic satire on dull writers ("dunces") |
| ***An Essay on Man*** | 1733–34 | philosophical poem in four epistles |
| *Moral Essays*; *Imitations of Horace*; ***Epistle to Dr. Arbuthnot*** | 1731–35 | satires and verse letters (Arbuthnot contains the portrait of Addison as "**Atticus**" and of Lord Hervey as "**Sporus**") |

::: work
**The Rape of the Lock — Summary.** Based on a real incident: Lord **Petre** cut off a lock of hair of **Arabella Fermor**, causing a quarrel between their Catholic families; Pope's friend John Caryll asked him to write a poem to laugh them into peace. In the poem, the beautiful **Belinda** wakes, protected by her guardian sylph **Ariel**, who warns her of some dreadful event. She dresses at her toilet like an epic hero arming for battle, and sails up the Thames to **Hampton Court**. There, during a game of cards (**ombre**) and coffee, the **Baron**, with a pair of scissors lent by **Clarissa**, cuts off one of her two curls. Belinda's fury is described in epic terms; the gnome **Umbriel** brings a bag of sighs and a vial of tears from the **Cave of Spleen**. A mock battle of beaux and belles follows; in the confusion, the lock disappears and rises to the heavens as a new **star**, so that it will be immortal — through Pope's poem.

**Features:** epic machinery (supernatural **sylphs** and gnomes based on Rosicrucian theory), invocation, arming of the hero, epic battle and journey to the underworld — all applied to a trivial social quarrel, satirising the vanity of fashionable society while delighting in its beauty.

**Characters:** Belinda, the Baron, Ariel, Umbriel, Clarissa, Thalestris, Sir Plume.
:::

::: quote
"What dire offence from amorous causes springs, / What mighty contests rise from trivial things." — *The Rape of the Lock* (opening)

"A little learning is a dangerous thing; / Drink deep, or taste not the Pierian spring." — *An Essay on Criticism*

"To err is human, to forgive divine." — *An Essay on Criticism*

"Fools rush in where angels fear to tread." — *An Essay on Criticism*

"True wit is nature to advantage dressed, / What oft was thought, but ne'er so well expressed." — *An Essay on Criticism*

"Know then thyself, presume not God to scan; / The proper study of mankind is man." — *An Essay on Man*

"Hope springs eternal in the human breast." — *An Essay on Man*

"Whatever is, is right." — *An Essay on Man*

"Damn with faint praise, assent with civil leer." — *Epistle to Dr. Arbuthnot* (on Atticus)

"Nature and Nature's laws lay hid in night: / God said, Let Newton be! and all was light." — *Epitaph intended for Sir Isaac Newton*
:::

### 58.3.3 Daniel Defoe (c. 1660–1731)

::: writer
**Titles:** often called the **Father of the English Novel** (with Richardson and Fielding); a pioneer of realistic fiction and of journalism · **Career:** merchant (went bankrupt), Dissenter, political pamphleteer, government spy; pilloried and imprisoned (1703) for the satirical pamphlet *The Shortest Way with the Dissenters*; founded the journal ***The Review*** (1704–1713) · **Novels:** ***Robinson Crusoe*** (1719), *Captain Singleton* (1720), ***Moll Flanders*** (1722), *A Journal of the Plague Year* (1722), *Colonel Jack* (1722), *Roxana* (1724) · **Other:** *The True-Born Englishman* (1701, verse satire), *A Tour Thro' the Whole Island of Great Britain*
:::

Defoe began writing fiction in his late fifties. His novels present themselves as **true first-person memoirs**, full of precise, practical details (lists, accounts, inventories), which give them extraordinary **realism**.

::: work
**Robinson Crusoe (1719).** Based partly on the real experience of the Scottish sailor **Alexander Selkirk**, who was marooned on an island off Chile for four years. **Robinson Crusoe** of York, against his father's advice, goes to sea. After adventures — including slavery in North Africa and life as a planter in Brazil — he is shipwrecked on a deserted island near the mouth of the Orinoco, the only survivor. By hard work and ingenuity, he salvages tools from the wreck, builds a shelter, grows corn, tames goats, makes pottery and keeps a journal and a calendar. He finds religious faith through reading the Bible. After many years he sees a **footprint in the sand**, discovers that **cannibals** visit the island, and rescues one of their prisoners, whom he names **Friday** and makes his servant, teaching him English and Christianity. After **28 years**, Crusoe helps the captain of an English ship put down a mutiny and returns to England. The novel is read as a celebration of individual enterprise, the Protestant work ethic and, by modern critics, as a story of colonialism.

**Characters:** Robinson Crusoe, Friday, Xury (a boy who escapes with him from slavery), the Portuguese captain, the Spaniard, Friday's father, the English captain.

**Moll Flanders (1722).** The life of **Moll Flanders**, born in **Newgate prison**, who goes through five marriages (one unknowingly to her own brother), becomes a skilled **thief**, is caught and sentenced to death, is transported to **Virginia** with her favourite husband, the highwayman **Jemy**, and ends rich and penitent. An early "rogue's progress" (picaresque) novel.
:::

::: quote
"It happened one day, about noon, going towards my boat, I was exceedingly surprised with the print of a man's naked foot on the shore." — *Robinson Crusoe*
:::

### 58.3.4 Jonathan Swift (1667–1745)

::: writer
**Titles:** the greatest **prose satirist** in English · **Born:** Dublin, of English parents · **Career:** secretary to Sir William Temple at Moor Park, where he met **Esther Johnson** ("**Stella**"); Anglican clergyman; Tory propagandist (*The Examiner*); **Dean of St Patrick's Cathedral, Dublin** (1713–1745); Irish patriot · **Women:** "Stella" (*Journal to Stella*) and Esther Vanhomrigh ("**Vanessa**" — *Cadenus and Vanessa*) · **Major works:** ***A Tale of a Tub*** (1704), ***The Battle of the Books*** (1704), ***Gulliver's Travels*** (1726), ***A Modest Proposal*** (1729), *The Drapier's Letters* (1724), *Journal to Stella*, *Verses on the Death of Dr. Swift* · **Epitaph (Latin):** "where savage indignation can no longer tear his heart" (*ubi saeva indignatio ulterius cor lacerare nequit*)
:::

**A Tale of a Tub** satirises corruption in religion through the allegory of three brothers — **Peter** (Roman Catholic Church), **Martin** (Anglican Church) and **Jack** (Dissenters/Calvinists) — and their father's will (the Bible). **The Battle of the Books** is a mock-heroic prose battle in St James's Library between ancient and modern books, defending the Ancients (with the famous fable of the **Spider** — modern, self-centred — and the **Bee**, which brings "**sweetness and light**"). ***A Modest Proposal*** is a brilliant piece of **irony** suggesting, with calm logic, that the poverty of Ireland could be solved by selling the babies of the poor as food for the rich.

::: work
**Gulliver's Travels (1726) — Summary.** Full title: *Travels into Several Remote Nations of the World, by Lemuel Gulliver*. **Lemuel Gulliver**, a ship's surgeon, describes four voyages:

**Part I — Lilliput.** Shipwrecked, Gulliver wakes to find himself tied down by the tiny **Lilliputians** (six inches tall). He helps their Emperor by capturing the fleet of the enemy island **Blefuscu**, and puts out a palace fire by urinating on it. Lilliputian politics is a satire on English politics: parties are distinguished by **high heels** and **low heels** (Tories and Whigs), the war with Blefuscu began over which end to break eggs (**Big-Endians vs Little-Endians** — Catholics and Protestants), and ministers win office by rope-dancing. Accused of treason, Gulliver escapes.

**Part II — Brobdingnag.** In a land of **giants**, Gulliver is now tiny. He is exhibited as a curiosity, then lives at court as the pet of the Queen and the young girl **Glumdalclitch**. When he proudly describes England to the wise **King of Brobdingnag**, the King concludes that the English are "**the most pernicious race of little odious vermin that nature ever suffered to crawl upon the surface of the earth**".

**Part III — Laputa, Balnibarbi, Luggnagg, Glubbdubdrib and Japan.** The flying island of **Laputa** is ruled by absent-minded scientists and mathematicians; the **Academy of Lagado** conducts absurd experiments (extracting sunbeams from cucumbers) — a satire on the Royal Society and impractical theory. In Glubbdubdrib he talks with ghosts of the great dead; in Luggnagg he meets the **Struldbrugs**, who are immortal but grow endlessly old and miserable.

**Part IV — The Country of the Houyhnhnms.** Gulliver comes to a land ruled by **Houyhnhnms** — wise, rational, virtuous horses — who keep as beasts of burden the **Yahoos**, filthy, greedy, savage creatures in human shape. Gulliver comes to admire the Houyhnhnms and loathe humankind; expelled, he returns home, but cannot bear the company of his family and prefers to talk to his horses in the stable.

**Characters:** Lemuel Gulliver, the Emperor of Lilliput, Flimnap, Reldresal, Glumdalclitch, the King and Queen of Brobdingnag, the Houyhnhnm master, the Yahoos, the Struldbrugs, Captain Pedro de Mendez.
:::

::: quote
"Satire is a sort of glass, wherein beholders do generally discover everybody's face but their own." — *The Battle of the Books* (Preface)

"We are so constituted that we have just religion enough to make us hate, but not enough to make us love one another." — *Thoughts on Various Subjects*

"When a true genius appears in the world, you may know him by this sign, that the dunces are all in confederacy against him." — *Thoughts on Various Subjects*

"Whoever could make two ears of corn or two blades of grass to grow upon a spot of ground where only one grew before, would deserve better of mankind…" — *Gulliver's Travels*, Part II
:::

### 58.3.5 Samuel Richardson (1689–1761)

::: writer
**Profession:** London printer · **Known as:** a Father of the English Novel; creator of the **epistolary novel** (novel in letters) and the psychological novel of sentiment · **Novels:** ***Pamela, or Virtue Rewarded*** (1740), ***Clarissa, or The History of a Young Lady*** (1747–48; one of the **longest novels in English**), ***Sir Charles Grandison*** (1753–54)
:::

Richardson began *Pamela* while preparing a book of model letters for ordinary people. His novels, told entirely through **letters**, explore the inner feelings and moral struggles of young women with minute detail.

::: work
**Pamela (1740).** **Pamela Andrews**, a fifteen-year-old maidservant, resists the repeated attempts of her young master, **Mr. B.**, to seduce her after his mother's death. He kidnaps her and keeps her prisoner at his Lincolnshire estate under the cruel housekeeper **Mrs. Jewkes**. Moved by reading her letters and journal, he reforms and **marries** her — hence the subtitle *Virtue Rewarded*. The novel was hugely popular and was parodied by Fielding in *Shamela* and ***Joseph Andrews***.

**Clarissa (1747–48).** The virtuous **Clarissa Harlowe**, pressed by her greedy family to marry the odious **Mr. Solmes**, runs away with the charming rake **Robert Lovelace**, who keeps her in a brothel and finally drugs and rapes her. Clarissa refuses to marry him and dies a saintly death; Lovelace is killed in a duel by her cousin Colonel Morden. Told through the letters of Clarissa, her friend **Anna Howe**, Lovelace and his friend **Belford**, it is a profound tragic novel.
:::

### 58.3.6 Joseph Addison (1672–1719) and Sir Richard Steele (1672–1729)

::: writer
**Joint achievement:** founders of the **periodical essay** and of modern English prose essay-journalism · **Steele founded** ***The Tatler*** (1709–1711, under the pen-name "**Isaac Bickerstaff**", borrowed from Swift) · **Together they founded** ***The Spectator*** (1 March 1711 – December 1712; revived by Addison in 1714) · **Aim:** "to enliven morality with wit, and to temper wit with morality"
:::

**Joseph Addison**, a Whig statesman (Secretary of State, 1717), wrote the classical tragedy ***Cato*** (1713) and the poem *The Campaign* (on Marlborough's victory at Blenheim), and was famous for his elegant, balanced prose style — Dr. Johnson advised: "Whoever wishes to attain an English style, familiar but not coarse, and elegant but not ostentatious, must give his days and nights to the volumes of Addison." Pope satirised him as "**Atticus**".

**Richard Steele**, an Irish-born soldier, playwright and journalist, wrote sentimental comedies (*The Conscious Lovers*, 1722; *The Tender Husband*). His style is warmer and more spontaneous than Addison's.

***The Spectator*** appeared **daily** (except Sundays) in 555 numbers. It was written by an imaginary "**Mr. Spectator**", a silent, observant gentleman who belongs to a club whose members represent different classes of English society:

| Member of the Spectator Club | Represents |
|---|---|
| **Sir Roger de Coverley** | an old-fashioned, kindly Tory country squire (the most famous creation, mainly by Addison) |
| **Sir Andrew Freeport** | a Whig merchant of London |
| **Captain Sentry** | the army |
| **Will Honeycomb** | a fashionable, ageing man about town |
| A **Templar** (law student) | the law and the theatre-goer |
| A **clergyman** | the Church |

The *Spectator* papers on Sir Roger at his country estate (**Coverley Hall**), at church, at the assizes, at the theatre and at Westminster Abbey are among the most charming character sketches in English, and anticipate the novel.

::: quote
"I shall endeavour to enliven morality with wit, and to temper wit with morality." — Addison, *The Spectator*, No. 10

"Reading is to the mind what exercise is to the body." — Steele, *The Tatler*, No. 147

"I have brought philosophy out of closets and libraries, schools and colleges, to dwell in clubs and assemblies, at tea-tables and in coffee-houses." — Addison, *The Spectator*, No. 10
:::
