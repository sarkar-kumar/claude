## 57.5 C. The Jacobean Period (1603–1625)

### 57.5.1 Background

On Elizabeth's death in 1603, **James VI of Scotland** became **James I of England** (Latin *Jacobus* — hence "Jacobean"). The national mood darkened: religious and political tensions grew (the **Gunpowder Plot**, 1605), and the confident optimism of the Elizabethans gave way to scepticism, melancholy and satire. Literature of the period includes Shakespeare's great tragedies and romances, Jonson's satiric comedies, the dark **revenge tragedies** of Webster, Tourneur and Middleton, **Metaphysical poetry**, Bacon's later prose, and the ***Authorised (King James) Version of the Bible (1611)***, whose rhythms shaped English prose for centuries.

### 57.5.2 Metaphysical Poets and Metaphysical Poetry

The term **"Metaphysical Poets"** was given by **Dr. Samuel Johnson** in his ***Life of Cowley*** (1779), following **Dryden**, who had said that Donne "**affects the metaphysics**" even in his love poems. Johnson criticised them: "*The metaphysical poets were men of learning, and to show their learning was their whole endeavour*", and their wit was "**a combination of dissimilar images**" in which "**the most heterogeneous ideas are yoked by violence together**". In the twentieth century, **T. S. Eliot**'s essay "**The Metaphysical Poets**" (1921) praised them for a "**unified sensibility**" — the fusion of thought and feeling ("*A thought to Donne was an experience*") — and introduced the idea of a later "**dissociation of sensibility**".

**Characteristics of Metaphysical poetry:**

- **Metaphysical conceit** — an elaborate, startling comparison between very unlike things (lovers' souls compared to the legs of a compass; a flea as a marriage bed).
- **Wit and intellect**: argument, logic, paradox and learned references (science, theology, geography, alchemy).
- **Colloquial, dramatic openings** ("*For God's sake hold your tongue, and let me love*").
- **Compressed, rough, irregular rhythms** rather than smooth Elizabethan music.
- Themes of **love** (secular) and **religion** (sacred), often treated with the same intensity.

**The Metaphysical poets:** **John Donne** (leader), **George Herbert**, **Richard Crashaw**, **Henry Vaughan**, **Andrew Marvell**, **Abraham Cowley**, **Thomas Traherne**, Lord Herbert of Cherbury, Thomas Carew (partly).

### 57.5.3 John Donne (1572–1631)

::: writer
**Titles:** Leader / father of the Metaphysical school · **Career:** born a Roman Catholic; studied at Oxford and Lincoln's Inn; sailed with Essex to Cadiz (1596); secretary to Sir Thomas Egerton; secret marriage to Anne More (1601) ruined his career ("**John Donne, Anne Donne, Un-done**"); became an Anglican priest (1615); **Dean of St Paul's Cathedral, London (1621–1631)** — the greatest preacher of his age · **Major works:** *Songs and Sonnets* (published 1633), *Holy Sonnets* / *Divine Poems*, *Elegies*, *Satires*, *The Anniversaries*, *Devotions upon Emergent Occasions* (1624), *Sermons*
:::

**Love poems (*Songs and Sonnets*):** *The Good-Morrow*, *The Sun Rising*, *The Canonization*, *The Flea*, *A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning* (the famous **compass conceit**), *The Ecstasy*, *The Relic*, *Song* ("Go and catch a falling star"), *The Anniversary*, *Twicknam Garden*, *Lovers' Infiniteness*.

**Religious poems:** *Holy Sonnets* (*"Death, be not proud"*, *"Batter my heart, three-person'd God"*, *"At the round earth's imagined corners"*), *A Hymn to God the Father*, *Good Friday, 1613. Riding Westward*.

**Prose:** ***Devotions upon Emergent Occasions*** (1624), written during a serious illness, contains Meditation XVII ("**No man is an island**"); his sermons are masterpieces of English prose.

::: work
**A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning.** Written for his wife before a journey abroad (1611), the poem asks her not to weep at parting, because their love is spiritual, not physical; their two souls are "*like gold to airy thinness beat*" — or like the two legs of a **pair of compasses**: her soul is the fixed foot at the centre, which leans and follows as his soul moves round, and her firmness makes his circle complete and brings him home.

**The Flea.** The speaker points to a flea that has bitten both him and his beloved, mingling their blood, and argues wittily that, since this is no sin, their union would be no sin either.
:::

::: quote
"No man is an island, entire of itself; every man is a piece of the continent, a part of the main… any man's death diminishes me, because I am involved in mankind; and therefore never send to know for whom the bell tolls; it tolls for thee." — *Devotions upon Emergent Occasions*, Meditation XVII

"Death, be not proud, though some have called thee / Mighty and dreadful, for thou art not so… / And death shall be no more; Death, thou shalt die." — *Holy Sonnet X*

"Batter my heart, three-person'd God." — *Holy Sonnet XIV*

"For God's sake hold your tongue, and let me love." — *The Canonization*

"Busy old fool, unruly Sun, / Why dost thou thus, / Through windows, and through curtains, call on us?" — *The Sun Rising*

"Thy firmness makes my circle just, / And makes me end where I begun." — *A Valediction: Forbidding Mourning*

"Go and catch a falling star, / Get with child a mandrake root." — *Song*
:::

::: note
Hemingway's novel ***For Whom the Bell Tolls*** (1940) takes its title from Donne's *Devotions*.
:::

### 57.5.4 Ben Jonson (1572–1637)

::: writer
**Titles:** **Father of English comedy (comedy of humours)**; first (unofficial) **Poet Laureate** (royal pension 1616); leader of the "**Tribe of Ben**" / "**Sons of Ben**" · **Career:** bricklayer's stepson; educated at Westminster School under William Camden; soldier in Flanders; actor and playwright; killed an actor (Gabriel Spencer) in a duel (1598) and escaped hanging by "benefit of clergy" · **Buried:** Westminster Abbey, upright; epitaph "**O rare Ben Jonson**" · **Major works:** *Every Man in His Humour* (1598), *Every Man out of His Humour* (1599), ***Volpone*** (1606), *Epicoene, or The Silent Woman* (1609), ***The Alchemist*** (1610), *Bartholomew Fair* (1614); tragedies *Sejanus* and *Catiline*; court **masques** (with Inigo Jones); poems *Epigrams*, *The Forest*, *Underwoods*; prose *Timber, or Discoveries*
:::

Jonson was a friend and rival of Shakespeare (Shakespeare acted in *Every Man in His Humour*), and the most learned dramatist of the age. He followed **classical rules** (the unities of time, place and action) and developed the **comedy of humours** — based on the medieval theory that the body's four fluids or "humours" (blood, phlegm, yellow bile, black bile) govern temperament; each character is dominated by one ruling passion or obsession (greed, jealousy, vanity), which is exposed and ridiculed. His comedies are sharp **satires** of greed and folly in London society.

::: work
**Volpone, or The Fox (1606).** In Venice, the rich, childless **Volpone** ("the fox") pretends to be dying so that greedy legacy-hunters will shower him with gifts in hope of being named his heir. His clever servant **Mosca** ("the fly") manages the trick on **Voltore** ("the vulture", a lawyer), **Corbaccio** ("the raven", an old miser, who disinherits his son **Bonario**), and **Corvino** ("the crow", a merchant, who even offers his virtuous wife **Celia** to Volpone). An English sub-plot features the foolish **Sir Politic Would-Be** and **Lady Would-Be**. Finally Mosca tries to cheat his master; Volpone reveals the truth in court, and all the rogues are severely punished.

**The Alchemist (1610).** During an outbreak of plague, the master **Lovewit** leaves London; his butler **Face** joins the con-man **Subtle** (the "alchemist") and the prostitute **Dol Common** to swindle a series of greedy, gullible clients — the lawyer's clerk **Dapper**, the tobacconist **Abel Drugger**, the voluptuous knight **Sir Epicure Mammon** (who dreams of the philosopher's stone), the Puritans **Ananias** and **Tribulation Wholesome**, and the angry boy **Kastril**. When Lovewit returns unexpectedly, Face outwits his partners and wins his master's pardon by finding him a rich widow.
:::

::: quote
"Drink to me only with thine eyes, / And I will pledge with mine." — *Song: To Celia*

"He was not of an age, but for all time!" — on Shakespeare

"Rest in soft peace, and, asked, say here doth lie / Ben Jonson his best piece of poetry." — *On My First Son*

"Good morning to the day; and next, my gold! / Open the shrine, that I may see my saint." — *Volpone* (opening lines)
:::

### 57.5.5 John Webster (c. 1580–c. 1632)

::: writer
**Known for:** dark, poetic **revenge tragedies** set in corrupt Italian courts · **Major works:** ***The White Devil*** (1612), ***The Duchess of Malfi*** (c. 1613–14) · **T. S. Eliot** wrote that Webster "was much possessed by death / And saw the skull beneath the skin" ("Whispers of Immortality")
:::

::: work
**The Duchess of Malfi.** The young widowed **Duchess of Malfi** secretly marries her honest steward **Antonio**, against the wishes of her brothers — her twin **Duke Ferdinand** of Calabria and the corrupt **Cardinal** — who want her to remain unmarried (partly to inherit her wealth). They plant the malcontent **Bosola** in her household as a spy. When the marriage is discovered, the Duchess is imprisoned and tormented with madmen and waxwork figures of her "dead" family; she meets death with great dignity ("**I am Duchess of Malfi still**") and is strangled with her children and her maid **Cariola**. Ferdinand, overwhelmed by guilt, goes mad (lycanthropy — he thinks he is a wolf). Bosola, repentant, turns against the brothers; in the bloody final act, Bosola accidentally kills Antonio, kills the Cardinal and is killed by Ferdinand, whom he also kills.

**Characters:** the Duchess, Antonio Bologna, Ferdinand, the Cardinal, Daniel de Bosola, Cariola, Delio, Julia.

**The White Devil.** **Vittoria Corombona**, a beautiful Venetian, becomes the mistress of the Duke of **Brachiano**; her brother **Flamineo** helps them murder their spouses; all die violently. Vittoria's brilliant defence at her trial is famous.
:::

::: quote
"Cover her face; mine eyes dazzle: she died young." — *The Duchess of Malfi* (Ferdinand, IV.ii)

"I am Duchess of Malfi still." — *The Duchess of Malfi* (IV.ii)

"We are merely the stars' tennis-balls, struck and banded / Which way please them." — *The Duchess of Malfi* (Bosola, V.iv)
:::

## 57.6 Andrew Marvell (1621–1678)

::: writer
**Position:** Metaphysical poet of the Commonwealth and Restoration years · **Career:** tutor to the daughter of Lord Fairfax; **Latin Secretary** assisting **Milton** under Cromwell (1657); **MP for Hull** (1659–1678); after the Restoration, a political satirist (*The Rehearsal Transpros'd*) who helped save Milton from punishment · **Major poems:** *To His Coy Mistress*, *The Garden*, *An Horatian Ode upon Cromwell's Return from Ireland*, *The Definition of Love*, *Bermudas*, *Upon Appleton House*, *The Mower* poems, *A Dialogue between the Soul and Body* · **Published:** *Miscellaneous Poems* (1681, posthumous)
:::

::: work
**To His Coy Mistress.** The finest **carpe diem** ("seize the day") poem in English, built as a logical **syllogism** in three parts: (1) *If* we had "world enough and time", I would woo you slowly for centuries, praising each part of your beauty; (2) *but* time is short — "Time's winged chariot" hurries near, and in the grave your beauty and "long-preserved virginity" will be wasted on worms; (3) *therefore* let us enjoy love now, and though we cannot make the sun stand still, "yet we will make him run".

**The Garden.** A meditative poem praising the solitude of a garden over the busy world of ambition; the mind withdraws into itself, "Annihilating all that's made / To a green thought in a green shade."

**An Horatian Ode upon Cromwell's Return from Ireland (1650).** A balanced political poem praising Cromwell's energy while honouring the dignity of the executed Charles I ("He nothing common did or mean / Upon that memorable scene").
:::

::: quote
"Had we but world enough and time, / This coyness, lady, were no crime." — *To His Coy Mistress*

"But at my back I always hear / Time's wingèd chariot hurrying near." — *To His Coy Mistress*

"The grave's a fine and private place, / But none, I think, do there embrace." — *To His Coy Mistress*

"Annihilating all that's made / To a green thought in a green shade." — *The Garden*
:::

## 57.7 Abraham Cowley (1618–1667)

::: writer
**Reputation:** the most popular poet of his own time; a late Metaphysical poet · **Major works:** *Poetical Blossoms* (1633, at 15), *The Mistress* (1647, love poems), ***Davideis*** (unfinished biblical epic on King David), ***Pindarique Odes*** (1656), *Essays* (prose) · **Contribution:** introduced the irregular **Pindaric ode** into English · **Buried:** Westminster Abbey, next to Chaucer and Spenser
:::

Cowley was a precocious royalist poet who served the exiled Queen Henrietta Maria in France as a cipher secretary. His ***Pindarique Odes*** imitated the Greek poet **Pindar** (as he understood him) with irregular stanzas and line lengths — a form later used by Dryden, Gray, Wordsworth (*Ode: Intimations of Immortality*) and others. Dr. Johnson's ***Life of Cowley*** contains the famous discussion of the "metaphysical poets". Cowley's graceful prose essays (*Of Myself*, *Of Solitude*, *Of Obscurity*) anticipate the polished essay of the eighteenth century.

## 57.8 Henry Vaughan (1621–1695)

::: writer
**Called:** "**the Silurist**" (from the ancient Silures tribe of his native Brecknockshire, Wales) · **Profession:** physician · **Major work:** ***Silex Scintillans*** ("The Flashing Flint", 1650; enlarged 1655) · **Influence:** a disciple of George Herbert; anticipates Wordsworth
:::

Vaughan was a Welsh **religious Metaphysical poet** who turned to devotional poetry under the influence of **George Herbert** after a spiritual crisis. His poems express a mystical vision of eternity, nostalgia for the innocence of **childhood** and a love of **nature** as God's creation.

::: work
**The Retreat.** The speaker longs to return to his "angel infancy", when his soul was close to heaven and saw "some shadows of eternity" in nature, before it learned sin. The poem is often compared with **Wordsworth's *Ode: Intimations of Immortality***, which develops the same idea of the child's heavenly vision.

**The World.** A visionary poem opening with a vision of eternity as a ring of light, contrasted with the follies of worldly men.
:::

::: quote
"Happy those early days! when I / Shined in my angel infancy." — *The Retreat*

"I saw Eternity the other night, / Like a great ring of pure and endless light." — *The World*

"They are all gone into the world of light!" — *Silex Scintillans*
:::

## 57.9 D. The Caroline Period (1625–1649)

### 57.9.1 Background and the Cavalier Poets

The reign of **Charles I** (*Carolus*) was marked by conflict between King and Parliament, leading to the **Civil War (1642–1651)** and the King's **execution (1649)**. The royalist supporters of the King were called **Cavaliers** (the Parliamentarians were **Roundheads**).

The **Cavalier poets** were courtier-poets loyal to Charles I and followers of Ben Jonson ("**Sons of Ben**"). Their poetry is **light, graceful, witty and elegant**, dealing with love, beauty, loyalty, honour, wine and the fleeting pleasures of life (**carpe diem**). The main Cavalier poets are **Robert Herrick**, **Thomas Carew**, **Richard Lovelace** and **Sir John Suckling** (Edmund Waller is sometimes included).

::: quote
"Stone walls do not a prison make, / Nor iron bars a cage." — Richard Lovelace, *To Althea, from Prison*

"I could not love thee, dear, so much, / Loved I not honour more." — Richard Lovelace, *To Lucasta, Going to the Wars*

"Why so pale and wan, fond lover? / Prithee, why so pale?" — Sir John Suckling, *Song*

"Go, lovely rose!" — Edmund Waller, *Song*
:::

### 57.9.2 Robert Herrick (1591–1674)

::: writer
**Group:** the greatest of the **Cavalier poets**; a "Son of Ben" · **Profession:** clergyman, vicar of **Dean Prior, Devon** (expelled by the Puritans in 1647, restored in 1662) · **Major work:** ***Hesperides*** (1648) — over 1,100 short poems, with the religious section ***Noble Numbers***
:::

Herrick wrote graceful, musical lyrics on love, country festivals, flowers, imaginary mistresses (**Julia**, Anthea, Corinna, Electra) and the brevity of life. Influenced by the classical poets **Horace** and **Catullus** and by Jonson, his poems combine pagan delight with Christian feeling.

**Famous poems:** *To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time*; *Corinna's Going A-Maying*; *Upon Julia's Clothes*; *Delight in Disorder*; *To Daffodils*; *The Argument of His Book*; *Cherry-Ripe*; *His Prayer to Ben Jonson*; *To Anthea, Who May Command Him Anything*.

::: quote
"Gather ye rosebuds while ye may, / Old Time is still a-flying; / And this same flower that smiles today / Tomorrow will be dying." — *To the Virgins, to Make Much of Time*

"Whenas in silks my Julia goes, / Then, then, methinks, how sweetly flows / That liquefaction of her clothes." — *Upon Julia's Clothes*

"A sweet disorder in the dress / Kindles in clothes a wantonness." — *Delight in Disorder*

"Fair daffodils, we weep to see / You haste away so soon." — *To Daffodils*
:::

### 57.9.3 George Herbert (1593–1633)

::: writer
**Group:** **religious Metaphysical poet** (Caroline period) · **Career:** Public Orator of Cambridge University; MP; gave up worldly ambition to become an Anglican priest — rector of **Bemerton**, near Salisbury (1630–1633); known as "**holy Mr. Herbert**" · **Major work:** ***The Temple*** (1633, posthumous, published by his friend Nicholas Ferrar) · **Prose:** *A Priest to the Temple, or The Country Parson* · **Biography:** by Izaak Walton
:::

Herbert's poems record the conflicts and the peace of the soul in its relation to God. They are simple in diction but intricate in structure, using homely images, Metaphysical conceits and remarkable **pattern (shaped) poems** whose printed shape mirrors the subject — ***Easter Wings*** (shaped like wings) and ***The Altar*** (shaped like an altar).

**Famous poems:** *The Collar* (rebellion against God, ending in submission: "*Methought I heard one calling, Child! / And I replied, My Lord*"), *The Pulley* (God withholds "rest" from man so that weariness may toss him to God's breast), *Virtue*, *Love (III)*, *Jordan (I) and (II)*, *Denial*, *Redemption*, *Easter Wings*, *The Altar*, *Prayer (I)*.

::: quote
"Sweet day, so cool, so calm, so bright, / The bridal of the earth and sky." — *Virtue*

"Love bade me welcome: yet my soul drew back, / Guilty of dust and sin." — *Love (III)*

"I struck the board, and cried, No more; / I will abroad." — *The Collar* (opening)
:::

::: note
Herbert is often called the "**Caroline Metaphysical**" or "**the saint of the Metaphysical school**"; his influence is seen in Vaughan and Crashaw. **Richard Crashaw** (c. 1613–1649), a Catholic convert, wrote ecstatic Baroque religious poetry (*Steps to the Temple*, *The Flaming Heart*, *Hymn to Saint Teresa*).
:::

## 57.10 E. The Commonwealth (Puritan) Period (1649–1660)

### 57.10.1 Background

After the execution of Charles I (30 January 1649), England became a republic — the **Commonwealth** — dominated by **Oliver Cromwell** (Lord Protector, 1653–1658). The Puritans closed the theatres (from **1642** to **1660**), so drama almost ceased. The age produced great **prose** — political, religious and philosophical — and the poetry of Milton (whose great epics, however, were published after 1660), Marvell and Vaughan. The period is also called the **Age of Milton** (Milton is treated in Chapter 58).

### 57.10.2 Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679)

::: writer
**Field:** political philosophy · **Masterpiece:** ***Leviathan*** (1651) · **Other works:** translation of Thucydides (1629), *De Cive* (1642), *De Corpore*, *Behemoth* (history of the Civil War), translation of Homer (in old age) · **Ideas:** materialism, social contract, absolute sovereignty
:::

**Hobbes**, tutor to the Cavendish family and briefly mathematics tutor to the future Charles II in Paris, lived through the Civil War and wrote ***Leviathan*** to explain why people need a strong government. He argues that in the **state of nature**, without government, there is a "**war of every man against every man**" driven by fear and self-interest, and human life is "**solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short**". To escape this, people make a **social contract**, surrendering their rights to an absolute **sovereign** (the "Leviathan", a mighty sea monster from the Bible, symbolising the commonwealth), whose power must be undivided. Hobbes's ideas founded modern political philosophy and were answered later by **John Locke** (Chapter 58) and **Rousseau**.

::: quote
"…and the life of man, solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short." — *Leviathan*, Chapter XIII

"The condition of man … is a condition of war of every one against every one." — *Leviathan*
:::

### 57.10.3 Jeremy Taylor (1613–1667)

::: writer
**Titles:** "**the Shakespeare of Divines**" (Emerson); "the Spenser of English prose" · **Career:** Anglican clergyman, chaplain to Archbishop Laud and Charles I; imprisoned several times under the Commonwealth; after the Restoration, **Bishop of Down and Connor** (Ireland) · **Major works:** ***The Liberty of Prophesying*** (1647), ***The Rule and Exercises of Holy Living*** (1650), ***The Rule and Exercises of Holy Dying*** (1651), *The Golden Grove*, *Ductor Dubitantium*, sermons
:::

**Jeremy Taylor** was the greatest devotional prose writer of the century. ***The Liberty of Prophesying*** is an early plea for **religious toleration**. ***Holy Living*** and ***Holy Dying*** — practical guides to Christian life and preparation for death — are written in a rich, rhythmical, imaginative prose full of poetic images, which earned him comparison with Shakespeare and Spenser.

::: quote
"As our life is very short, so it is very miserable; and therefore it is well it is short." — *Holy Dying*
:::
