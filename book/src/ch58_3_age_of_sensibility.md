## 58.4 C. The Age of Sensibility (1745–1798)

### 58.4.1 Background

The second half of the eighteenth century is called the **Age of Johnson** (after its dominant literary figure), the **Age of Sensibility** (Northrop Frye's term, because writers increasingly valued **feeling, sympathy and emotional response**), or the **Age of Transition** (because it bridges Neo-Classicism and Romanticism). While Johnson upheld classical standards, poets such as **Gray, Collins, Cowper, Burns and Blake** turned towards nature, melancholy, the common man, the medieval past and imagination — the "**Pre-Romantics**". The **novel** reached maturity with **Fielding, Richardson, Smollett and Sterne**; the **Gothic novel** began with Horace Walpole's *The Castle of Otranto* (1764); **comedy** was revived by Goldsmith and **Sheridan**; and great works of history (**Gibbon**), biography (**Boswell**) and political thought (**Burke**) appeared. European thinkers — **Voltaire** and **Rousseau** of the French **Enlightenment**, and **Goethe** of German Romanticism — shaped the intellectual climate.

### 58.4.2 Dr. Samuel Johnson (1709–1784)

::: writer
**Titles:** **Dr. Johnson**; "the Great Cham of literature" (Smollett); the dominant man of letters of his age (**Age of Johnson**) · **Born:** Lichfield, Staffordshire · **Education:** Pembroke College, Oxford (left without a degree for lack of money) · **Honorary degrees:** Doctor of Laws from Trinity College Dublin (1765) and Oxford (1775) — hence "Dr." · **Founded:** **The Literary Club** (1764) with Joshua Reynolds — members included Burke, Goldsmith, Garrick, Boswell, Gibbon, Adam Smith · **Biographer:** **James Boswell** · **Buried:** Westminster Abbey
:::

Johnson was a poor bookseller's son who struggled for years as a hack writer in London before achieving fame. His conversation was legendary; much of what we know of him comes from **Boswell's *Life of Samuel Johnson*** (1791), the greatest biography in English.

**Major works:**

| Work | Year | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| *London* | 1738 | verse satire imitating Juvenal |
| *Life of Mr Richard Savage* | 1744 | biography of his friend, a poet |
| ***The Vanity of Human Wishes*** | 1749 | verse satire imitating Juvenal's Tenth Satire |
| *Irene* | 1749 | tragedy (produced by Garrick) |
| ***The Rambler*** | 1750–52 | twice-weekly periodical essays; also ***The Idler*** (1758–60) |
| ***A Dictionary of the English Language*** | **1755** | compiled almost single-handed in about nine years; about **42,000** words with 114,000 illustrative quotations; the first great English dictionary |
| ***Rasselas, Prince of Abyssinia*** | **1759** | philosophical romance (oriental tale), written in a week to pay for his mother's funeral |
| ***The Plays of William Shakespeare*** (edition with **Preface**) | 1765 | the *Preface to Shakespeare* is a landmark of criticism |
| *A Journey to the Western Islands of Scotland* | 1775 | travel book (tour with Boswell) |
| ***The Lives of the English Poets*** | 1779–81 | critical biographies of 52 poets (Cowley to Gray), including the famous discussion of the **Metaphysical poets** in the *Life of Cowley* |

::: work
**Rasselas (1759).** **Rasselas**, a son of the Emperor of **Abyssinia** (Ethiopia), lives in the **Happy Valley**, a paradise where every pleasure is provided, but he is discontented. With his sister **Nekayah**, her maid **Pekuah**, and the poet-philosopher **Imlac**, he escapes through a mountain tunnel to search the world (mainly **Egypt**) for the secret of happiness — the "**choice of life**". They examine every condition — pastoral life, the hermit, the philosopher, the rich, the powerful, marriage and celibacy — and find that no state brings perfect happiness. The book's last chapter is titled "**The Conclusion, in Which Nothing Is Concluded**"; they decide to return to Abyssinia. The book shares its theme — the vanity of human wishes — with Voltaire's *Candide* (published the same year).

**The Dictionary.** Famous for its witty definitions: *Lexicographer*: "**a writer of dictionaries; a harmless drudge**"; *Oats*: "**a grain, which in England is generally given to horses, but in Scotland supports the people**"; *Patron*: "commonly a wretch who supports with insolence, and is paid with flattery." His **Letter to Lord Chesterfield** (1755), rejecting the earl's late offer of patronage, is a famous declaration of the independence of the man of letters.
:::

::: quote
"When a man is tired of London, he is tired of life; for there is in London all that life can afford." — quoted in Boswell's *Life*

"Patriotism is the last refuge of a scoundrel." — quoted in Boswell's *Life*

"No man but a blockhead ever wrote, except for money." — quoted in Boswell's *Life*

"Let observation with extensive view, / Survey mankind, from China to Peru." — *The Vanity of Human Wishes* (opening)

"Human life is everywhere a state in which much is to be endured, and little to be enjoyed." — *Rasselas*

"Shakespeare is above all writers… the poet of nature; the poet that holds up to his readers a faithful mirror of manners and of life." — *Preface to Shakespeare*

"Is not a patron, my Lord, one who looks with unconcern on a man struggling for life in the water, and, when he has reached ground, encumbers him with help?" — *Letter to Lord Chesterfield*
:::

### 58.4.3 Henry Fielding (1707–1754)

::: writer
**Titles:** **Father of the English Novel** (most commonly in exam keys); "the Prose Homer of Human Nature" (Byron) · **Career:** playwright of political satires (his attacks on Walpole led to the **Licensing Act of 1737**, which ended his theatre career); lawyer; **magistrate** at Bow Street, London, where he and his half-brother John founded the **Bow Street Runners** (an early police force) · **Novels:** *Shamela* (1741, parody), ***Joseph Andrews*** (1742), *Jonathan Wild* (1743), ***Tom Jones*** (1749), ***Amelia*** (1751) · **Died:** Lisbon, 1754
:::

Fielding described his novel as "**a comic epic poem in prose**" (Preface to *Joseph Andrews*). Unlike Richardson's intense letters, his novels have a **wide panorama** of English society, an **intrusive, witty narrator**, carefully constructed plots, and a generous, humorous view of human nature that values **good-heartedness** over rigid morality.

::: work
**Joseph Andrews (1742).** Begun as a parody of Richardson's *Pamela*: **Joseph Andrews**, the virtuous footman brother of Pamela, resists the advances of his mistress **Lady Booby** and is dismissed. Travelling home to his sweetheart **Fanny Goodwill**, he is joined by the good-hearted, absent-minded parson **Abraham Adams**, one of the great comic characters of English fiction. After many adventures on the road, Joseph is discovered to be the son of the respectable **Mr. Wilson**, and marries Fanny.

**Tom Jones (1749) — The History of Tom Jones, a Foundling.** **Tom Jones**, a foundling, is brought up by the kind, wealthy **Squire Allworthy** in Somerset alongside Allworthy's nephew, the hypocritical **Blifil**. Tom, warm-hearted but impulsive, loves **Sophia Western**, daughter of the boisterous, fox-hunting neighbour **Squire Western**, who wants her to marry Blifil. Blifil's lies get Tom expelled. Tom and Sophia separately travel to London, with many comic and romantic adventures on the road and in town (including Tom's affair with **Lady Bellaston** and the seeming horror that **Mrs. Waters** is his mother). Finally Blifil's treachery is exposed, Tom is revealed to be the son of Allworthy's sister **Bridget**, and he marries Sophia. Tom's tutors are the philosopher **Square** and the clergyman **Thwackum**; his loyal companion is the barber **Partridge**.
:::

### 58.4.4 Thomas Gray (1716–1771)

::: writer
**Group:** **Graveyard poets** / **Pre-Romantic** poets · **Career:** scholar at Cambridge (Professor of Modern History); declined the **Poet Laureateship** (1757) · **Masterpiece:** ***Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard*** (1751) · **Other poems:** *Ode on a Distant Prospect of Eton College* (1747), *Ode on the Death of a Favourite Cat, Drowned in a Tub of Gold Fishes*, *The Progress of Poesy* and *The Bard* (Pindaric odes, 1757), *Sonnet on the Death of Richard West*, *Hymn to Adversity*
:::

Gray wrote little — a small but perfect body of verse. His ***Elegy***, set in the churchyard at **Stoke Poges** (Buckinghamshire), is one of the most loved and quoted poems in English. It is written in **heroic quatrains** (iambic pentameter, *abab*).

::: work
**Elegy Written in a Country Churchyard.** At evening, as the curfew tolls, the poet sits in a village churchyard and meditates on the humble villagers buried there — the "rude forefathers of the hamlet". Death levels all: the proud and the poor alike come to the grave. These poor people never had the chance to develop their gifts; among them there may lie a "mute inglorious Milton" or a village Hampden, whose talents were hidden by poverty, just as they were also kept from great crimes. The poem ends with an imagined epitaph for a young poet (perhaps Gray himself), "A youth to Fortune and to Fame unknown". General **Wolfe** is said to have recited it before capturing Quebec (1759), saying he would rather have written it than take the city.
:::

::: quote
"The curfew tolls the knell of parting day, / The lowing herd wind slowly o'er the lea." — *Elegy* (opening)

"The paths of glory lead but to the grave." — *Elegy*

"Full many a gem of purest ray serene, / The dark unfathom'd caves of ocean bear: / Full many a flower is born to blush unseen, / And waste its sweetness on the desert air." — *Elegy*

"Some mute inglorious Milton here may rest." — *Elegy*

"Far from the madding crowd's ignoble strife." — *Elegy* (title source for Hardy's novel)

"Where ignorance is bliss, / 'Tis folly to be wise." — *Ode on a Distant Prospect of Eton College*
:::

### 58.4.5 Oliver Goldsmith (c. 1728–1774)

::: writer
**Born:** Ireland (Pallas, County Longford) · **Versatility:** wrote in almost every form — novel, play, poem, essay, history, biography · **Johnson's epitaph (Latin):** "*Nullum quod tetigit non ornavit*" — "**He touched nothing that he did not adorn**" · **Major works:** ***The Vicar of Wakefield*** (novel, 1766), ***She Stoops to Conquer*** (comedy, 1773), *The Good-Natured Man* (comedy, 1768), ***The Deserted Village*** (poem, 1770), *The Traveller* (poem, 1764), ***The Citizen of the World*** (essays, 1762 — letters of an imaginary Chinese philosopher, Lien Chi Altangi, visiting London)
:::

Goldsmith was a kindly, careless, generous man, always in debt; Johnson sold the manuscript of *The Vicar of Wakefield* for him for £60 to save him from arrest for rent. His comedies opposed the fashionable "sentimental comedy" and revived the laughing comedy.

::: work
**The Vicar of Wakefield (1766).** The good, simple and slightly vain **Dr. Charles Primrose**, Vicar of Wakefield, tells the story of his family's misfortunes, which he bears with Job-like patience. He loses his fortune and moves to a humble parish, where his landlord, the villainous **Squire Thornhill**, seduces his elder daughter **Olivia** with a fake marriage; his house burns down and he is thrown into debtors' prison, where he preaches to the prisoners. The kindly eccentric "**Mr. Burchell**" turns out to be **Sir William Thornhill**, the squire's uncle, who rescues the family; the marriage of Olivia is found to be valid, the younger daughter **Sophia** marries Sir William, and the son **George** marries **Arabella Wilmot**. Also: Mrs. Deborah Primrose, Moses, Ephraim Jenkinson (a swindler).

**She Stoops to Conquer, or The Mistakes of a Night (1773).** The young **Charles Marlow**, shy with ladies of his own class but bold with servant girls, travels with his friend **George Hastings** to visit **Mr. Hardcastle**, whose daughter **Kate** he is to marry. The mischievous **Tony Lumpkin** (Mrs. Hardcastle's son) tells them that Hardcastle's house is an inn; Marlow treats Hardcastle rudely as an innkeeper. Kate, discovering his weakness, "stoops to conquer" by pretending to be a barmaid, and wins his love. Hastings elopes with Tony's cousin **Constance Neville** after many comic confusions.

**The Deserted Village (1770).** A nostalgic poem on the decline of the village of "**Sweet Auburn**", once happy and prosperous, now deserted as its people are driven out by rich landowners' enclosures and the luxury trade; with portraits of the village preacher and the village schoolmaster.
:::

::: quote
"Ill fares the land, to hastening ills a prey, / Where wealth accumulates, and men decay." — *The Deserted Village*

"And still they gazed, and still the wonder grew, / That one small head could carry all he knew." — *The Deserted Village* (on the schoolmaster)

"When lovely woman stoops to folly…" — *The Vicar of Wakefield* (Olivia's song)
:::

### 58.4.6 Voltaire (François-Marie Arouet, 1694–1778)

::: writer
**Nationality:** French · **Movement:** the **Enlightenment** · **Pen-name:** Voltaire · **Masterpiece:** ***Candide, ou l'Optimisme*** (1759) · **Other works:** *Letters on the English* (*Lettres philosophiques*, 1733), *Zadig* (1747), *Micromégas*, *The Age of Louis XIV*, *Philosophical Dictionary* (1764), *Treatise on Tolerance* (1763), tragedies (*Zaïre*, *Oedipe*) · **Lived in England** 1726–1728
:::

Voltaire was the greatest champion of **reason, tolerance, freedom of speech and religious liberty** in eighteenth-century Europe, and a fierce critic of the Catholic Church and of injustice. He was twice imprisoned in the **Bastille** and spent years in exile; he admired English liberty, Newton and Locke.

::: work
**Candide (1759).** **Candide**, a naive young man, is brought up in the castle of **Baron Thunder-ten-tronckh** in Westphalia and taught by the philosopher **Dr. Pangloss** that "**all is for the best in the best of all possible worlds**" (a satire on Leibniz's optimism). Expelled for kissing the Baron's daughter **Cunégonde**, he wanders the world and suffers or witnesses every possible disaster — war, the **Lisbon earthquake (1755)**, the Inquisition, shipwreck, slavery, robbery. In the utopian land of **El Dorado** he finds wealth and reason, but leaves it. Accompanied by the valet **Cacambo** and the pessimist **Martin**, he finally settles on a small farm near Constantinople with his friends, where they find contentment in simple work. The book ends: "**Il faut cultiver notre jardin**" — "**We must cultivate our garden.**"
:::

::: quote
"If God did not exist, it would be necessary to invent him." — Voltaire, *Épître à l'auteur du livre des Trois imposteurs*

"I disapprove of what you say, but I will defend to the death your right to say it." — often attributed to Voltaire; actually written by his biographer **Evelyn Beatrice Hall** (1906) to sum up his attitude
:::

### 58.4.7 Jean-Jacques Rousseau (1712–1778)

::: writer
**Nationality:** born in **Geneva** (Swiss), wrote in French · **Titles:** "**Father of Romanticism**"; a thinker of the Enlightenment whose ideas fed the **French Revolution** · **Major works:** *Discourse on the Arts and Sciences* (1750), *Discourse on the Origin of Inequality* (1755), ***Julie, or the New Heloise*** (novel, 1761), ***The Social Contract*** (1762), ***Émile, or On Education*** (1762), ***Confessions*** (autobiography, published 1782–89), *Reveries of a Solitary Walker*
:::

Rousseau argued that man is **naturally good** (the idea of the "**noble savage**", though he did not use the phrase) and is corrupted by civilisation, property and society. In ***The Social Contract*** he maintained that legitimate political authority rests on the "**general will**" of the people, and that sovereignty belongs to the people — an idea central to democracy and to the French Revolution (*Liberté, Égalité, Fraternité*). ***Émile*** proposed a natural, child-centred education. His emphasis on **feeling, nature, the individual self and sincerity** profoundly influenced the **Romantic** movement (Wordsworth, Shelley, Byron). His *Confessions* founded modern autobiography.

::: quote
"Man is born free, and everywhere he is in chains." — *The Social Contract* (opening of Book I)

"Everything is good as it leaves the hands of the Author of things; everything degenerates in the hands of man." — *Émile* (opening)
:::

### 58.4.8 Edmund Burke (1729–1797)

::: writer
**Born:** Dublin · **Career:** Whig statesman and MP; orator; political philosopher — the **Father of Modern Conservatism** · **Major works:** ***A Philosophical Enquiry into the Origin of Our Ideas of the Sublime and Beautiful*** (1757), *Speech on Conciliation with America* (1775), speeches in the **impeachment of Warren Hastings** (Governor-General of Bengal, 1788–1795), ***Reflections on the Revolution in France*** (1790)
:::

Burke supported the grievances of the **American colonies** and attacked the misrule of the **East India Company in India**, leading the long impeachment of **Warren Hastings**, in which he described the sufferings of the people of Bengal. Yet he opposed the **French Revolution**: his *Reflections* defended tradition, gradual reform, religion and inherited institutions against abstract revolutionary theory, and provoked replies such as **Thomas Paine's *Rights of Man*** (1791) and **Mary Wollstonecraft's *A Vindication of the Rights of Men*** (1790). His essay on the **Sublime** (awe, terror, vastness) and the **Beautiful** (smallness, smoothness, delicacy) strongly influenced Romantic aesthetics and the Gothic novel.

::: quote
"The only thing necessary for the triumph of evil is for good men to do nothing." — attributed to Burke (not found in his writings)

"Society is indeed a contract … a partnership not only between those who are living, but between those who are living, those who are dead, and those who are to be born." — *Reflections on the Revolution in France*
:::

### 58.4.9 Sir Walter Scott (1771–1832)

::: writer
**Nationality:** Scottish (Edinburgh) · **Titles:** **Father of the Historical Novel**; "the **Wizard of the North**"; "the Great Unknown" (his novels appeared anonymously) · **Period:** strictly belongs to the **Romantic Age** (1798–1832), though some exam guides list him here · **Poems:** *The Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border* (collection of ballads), ***The Lay of the Last Minstrel*** (1805), ***Marmion*** (1808), ***The Lady of the Lake*** (1810) · **Novels ("Waverley Novels"):** ***Waverley*** (1814), *Guy Mannering*, *The Antiquary*, *Old Mortality*, *Rob Roy* (1817), *The Heart of Midlothian* (1818), *The Bride of Lammermoor* (1819), ***Ivanhoe*** (1819), *Kenilworth* (1821), *Quentin Durward* (1823), *The Talisman* (1825) · **Home:** Abbotsford · **Declined:** Poet Laureateship (1813), recommended Southey
:::

Scott first became famous as a narrative poet, but when Byron's popularity eclipsed his, he turned to the novel. In ***Waverley*** (1814) he created the **historical novel** — a story in which fictional characters move among real historical events and people, showing how history shapes ordinary lives. His novels made Scotland's past romantic for all Europe. After the collapse of his publishers (1826), he worked himself to death to pay off huge debts.

::: work
**Ivanhoe (1819).** Set in **twelfth-century England** during the absence of **King Richard the Lionheart** on the Crusades, when Saxons and Normans were still hostile. **Wilfred of Ivanhoe**, disinherited by his Saxon father **Cedric** for loving Cedric's ward **Rowena** and following Richard, returns disguised and wins the great tournament at **Ashby-de-la-Zouch**. With the help of the mysterious **Black Knight** (King Richard in disguise) and the outlaw **Locksley** (**Robin Hood**), Ivanhoe's friends are rescued from the Norman castle of **Torquilstone**. The beautiful Jewish woman **Rebecca**, daughter of **Isaac of York**, who nurses the wounded Ivanhoe, is accused of witchcraft by the Templar **Brian de Bois-Guilbert**; Ivanhoe fights as her champion, and Bois-Guilbert dies. Ivanhoe marries Rowena; Rebecca leaves England.

**Waverley (1814).** **Edward Waverley**, a young English officer, travels to the Scottish Highlands, becomes involved with the **Jacobite rebellion of 1745** of Bonnie Prince Charlie, loves the fiery **Flora MacIvor** but finally marries the gentle **Rose Bradwardine**.
:::

::: quote
"Breathes there the man, with soul so dead, / Who never to himself hath said, / This is my own, my native land!" — *The Lay of the Last Minstrel*

"O, what a tangled web we weave / When first we practise to deceive!" — *Marmion*
:::

### 58.4.10 Johann Wolfgang von Goethe (1749–1832)

::: writer
**Nationality:** German · **Titles:** Germany's greatest writer; a "universal genius" — poet, novelist, dramatist, scientist, statesman (minister at the court of **Weimar**) · **Movements:** **Sturm und Drang** ("Storm and Stress") and **Weimar Classicism** (with Schiller) · **Major works:** ***The Sorrows of Young Werther*** (*Die Leiden des jungen Werthers*, 1774), ***Faust*** (Part I, 1808; Part II, 1832), *Wilhelm Meister's Apprenticeship* (1795–96), *Götz von Berlichingen*, *Egmont*, *Iphigenie auf Tauris*, *Elective Affinities*, *West-östlicher Divan* (poems inspired by the Persian poet Hafez), *Poetry and Truth* (autobiography), *Theory of Colours*
:::

::: work
**The Sorrows of Young Werther (1774).** An epistolary novel. The sensitive young **Werther** falls hopelessly in love with **Lotte (Charlotte)**, who is engaged to and then marries the calm, worthy **Albert**. Unable to bear his despair, Werther shoots himself with pistols borrowed from Albert. The book caused a sensation across Europe ("Werther fever") and is a key work of the **Sturm und Drang** and early Romanticism.

**Faust.** Goethe's lifelong masterpiece, a poetic drama based on the Faust legend (also treated by Marlowe). The scholar **Faust**, dissatisfied with all knowledge, makes a **wager** with the devil **Mephistopheles**: if Faust ever says to a passing moment, "**Stay, thou art so fair!**" (*Verweile doch, du bist so schön*), Mephistopheles may have his soul. In Part I, Faust seduces the innocent **Gretchen (Margarete)**, who kills their child and is condemned to death, but is saved by divine grace. In Part II, Faust's journey through politics, classical myth (marriage to **Helen of Troy**) and land reclamation ends in his death; because of his constant striving, angels carry his soul to heaven. Unlike Marlowe's Faustus, Goethe's Faust is **redeemed**.
:::

::: quote
"Two souls, alas, dwell in my breast." — *Faust*, Part I

"Whoever strives with all his power, we are allowed to save." — *Faust*, Part II (the angels)

"The Eternal Feminine draws us upward." — *Faust*, Part II (last line)
:::

### 58.4.11 William Blake (1757–1827)

::: writer
**Profession:** engraver, painter and poet; created "**illuminated books**", in which he engraved text and pictures together on copper plates and coloured them by hand · **Period:** a **Pre-Romantic**; often counted as the first of the **Romantic** poets (Chapter 59) · **Titles:** mystic, visionary, prophet; "the Father of English Romanticism" in some books · **Major works:** *Poetical Sketches* (1783), ***Songs of Innocence*** (1789), ***The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*** (1790–93), ***Songs of Experience*** (1794; combined as *Songs of Innocence and of Experience: Showing the Two Contrary States of the Human Soul*), *The Book of Thel*, *Visions of the Daughters of Albion*, *America: A Prophecy*, *The Four Zoas*, ***Milton*** (1804–10), ***Jerusalem*** (1804–20)
:::

Blake had visions from childhood (he said he saw angels in a tree on Peckham Rye). He was largely ignored in his lifetime and died poor; his genius was recognised only later. He rejected the rationalism of Newton and Locke, the oppressions of Church, State and industrial society, and celebrated **imagination**, energy and spiritual freedom. In his prophetic books he created his own mythology (Urizen — reason/law; Orc — revolution; Los — imagination).

::: work
**Songs of Innocence and of Experience.** The two collections present "two contrary states of the human soul". The *Songs of Innocence* (the child's view: joy, trust, God's love) include *The Lamb*, *The Chimney Sweeper*, *The Little Black Boy*, *Holy Thursday*, *Infant Joy*, *The Divine Image*, *The Ecchoing Green*. The *Songs of Experience* (the adult's view: fear, oppression, corruption) include ***The Tyger***, *The Chimney Sweeper*, *Holy Thursday*, ***London***, *The Sick Rose*, *A Poison Tree*, *Infant Sorrow*, *The Human Abstract*, *The Clod and the Pebble*. Many poems form **contrasting pairs**: *The Lamb* (gentle Creator) and *The Tyger* (the terrible, energetic Creator): "*Did he who made the Lamb make thee?*"
:::

::: quote
"Tyger Tyger, burning bright, / In the forests of the night; / What immortal hand or eye, / Could frame thy fearful symmetry?" — *The Tyger*

"Little Lamb, who made thee? / Dost thou know who made thee?" — *The Lamb*

"In every cry of every Man, / In every Infants cry of fear, / In every voice: in every ban, / The mind-forg'd manacles I hear." — *London*

"To see a World in a Grain of Sand / And a Heaven in a Wild Flower, / Hold Infinity in the palm of your hand / And Eternity in an hour." — *Auguries of Innocence*

"And did those feet in ancient time / Walk upon England's mountains green?… / Among these dark Satanic Mills?" — Preface to *Milton* (the hymn *Jerusalem*)

"The road of excess leads to the palace of wisdom." — *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell* (Proverbs of Hell)

"If the doors of perception were cleansed every thing would appear to man as it is, infinite." — *The Marriage of Heaven and Hell*
:::

**SAT / GRE Corner — Eighteenth-Century Vocabulary:** Many GRE words come from this age of satire, reason and sentiment: **sardonic, caustic, mordant, acerbic, trenchant, sententious** (fond of moralising — Johnson), **pedantic, urbane, decorous, sublime, picaresque, malapropism** (from Sheridan's Mrs. Malaprop), **Panglossian** (naively optimistic — from Voltaire's Pangloss), **Lilliputian** (tiny, petty), **Brobdingnagian** (gigantic), **Yahoo** (a brutish person), **quixotic**, **Gothic**. GRE reading passages on the Enlightenment often discuss Locke, Rousseau and the "social contract".
:::
