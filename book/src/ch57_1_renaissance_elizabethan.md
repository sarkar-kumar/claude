## 57.1 What Was the Renaissance?

**Renaissance** (French: *rebirth*) refers to the revival of classical Greek and Roman learning, art and values that began in **Italy in the fourteenth century** (Petrarch, Boccaccio, later Leonardo, Michelangelo, Machiavelli) and spread across Europe. The fall of Constantinople (1453) brought Greek scholars and manuscripts west; the **printing press** (Gutenberg, c. 1450; Caxton in England, 1476) spread learning; the voyages of **Columbus (1492)** and **Vasco da Gama (1498)** opened new worlds; **Copernicus** challenged the earth-centred universe; and the **Reformation** (Luther, 1517; Henry VIII's break with Rome, 1534) transformed religion.

In England, the Renaissance arrived later, around **1500**, and flowered under **Queen Elizabeth I (1558–1603)**. Its central idea was **humanism** — a new confidence in human reason and potential, and study of the classics (*studia humanitatis*).

### 57.1.1 Sub-divisions of the English Renaissance

| Sub-period | Dates | Monarch(s) | Key writers |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Preparation for the Renaissance (Early Tudor)** | 1500–1558 | Henry VII, Henry VIII, Edward VI, Mary I | Thomas More, Wyatt, Surrey, Skelton, Udall, Lindsay |
| **B. The Elizabethan Period** | 1558–1603 | Elizabeth I | Spenser, Sidney, Marlowe, Kyd, the University Wits, Shakespeare, Bacon, Norton & Sackville |
| **C. The Jacobean Period** | 1603–1625 | James I (*Jacobus*) | Shakespeare (late), Jonson, Donne, Webster, Beaumont & Fletcher, Bacon; the King James Bible (1611) |
| **D. The Caroline Period** | 1625–1649 | Charles I (*Carolus*) | Cavalier poets (Herrick, Carew, Lovelace, Suckling), George Herbert, Milton (early poems) |
| **E. The Commonwealth (Puritan) Period** | 1649–1660 | Oliver Cromwell (Lord Protector) | Milton (prose), Hobbes, Jeremy Taylor, Marvell, Vaughan; theatres closed (1642–1660) |

### 57.1.2 Characteristics of Renaissance Literature

- Revival of classical forms: **epic, tragedy, comedy, ode, pastoral, satire, epigram**.
- **Humanism** and individualism; love of beauty, adventure and fame.
- Introduction of the **sonnet** (Wyatt and Surrey from Petrarch) and of **blank verse** (Surrey; perfected by Marlowe — "Marlowe's mighty line" — and Shakespeare).
- **Patriotism** and national pride (defeat of the Spanish Armada, 1588).
- The **Golden Age of English drama**: public theatres, professional companies, the fusion of classical and native traditions.
- **Translations** of classical and Italian works (North's Plutarch, Chapman's Homer, Golding's Ovid) and of the **Bible** (Tyndale, Coverdale, the **King James Version, 1611**).

## 57.2 A. Preparation for the Renaissance (1500–1558)

### 57.2.1 Sir Thomas More (1478–1535)

::: writer
**Profession:** lawyer, humanist scholar, Lord Chancellor of England (1529–1532) · **Masterpiece:** ***Utopia*** (1516, written in **Latin**; English translation by Ralph Robinson, 1551) · **Death:** beheaded in 1535 for refusing to accept Henry VIII as Supreme Head of the Church · **Canonised:** 1935 (St Thomas More) · **Friend of:** Erasmus (who dedicated *In Praise of Folly* to him)
:::

***Utopia*** (Greek *ou-topos*, "no place", punning on *eu-topos*, "good place") is a philosophical romance in two books. In **Book I**, More meets the traveller **Raphael Hythloday** in Antwerp, who criticises the injustices of English society — harsh punishments for theft, enclosure of common land ("**sheep devour men**"), and the greed of the rich. In **Book II**, Hythloday describes the ideal island-commonwealth of **Utopia**, where there is **no private property**, everyone works six hours a day, gold is despised (used for chamber-pots and slaves' chains), there is religious toleration, education for all, and government by elected officials. The book gave the English language the word **utopia** (an ideal society) and founded the genre of utopian fiction (later *dystopias*: Huxley's *Brave New World*, Orwell's *Nineteen Eighty-Four*).

Other works: *The History of King Richard III* (source for Shakespeare's play), *A Dialogue of Comfort against Tribulation*.

::: quote
"Your sheep, that were wont to be so meek and tame and so small eaters, now … be become so great devourers and so wild that they eat up and swallow down the very men themselves." — *Utopia*, Book I

"I die the King's good servant, but God's first." — attributed to More on the scaffold
:::

### 57.2.2 Niccolò Machiavelli (1469–1527)

::: writer
**Nationality:** Italian (Florence) · **Profession:** diplomat, political philosopher, historian · **Masterpiece:** ***The Prince*** (*Il Principe*, written 1513, published 1532) · **Other works:** *Discourses on Livy*, *The Art of War*, the comedy *La Mandragola*
:::

Machiavelli served the Florentine Republic as a diplomat until the return of the **Medici** (1512), when he was dismissed, briefly imprisoned and tortured. In retirement he wrote ***The Prince***, dedicated to **Lorenzo de' Medici**, a practical guide for a ruler who wishes to gain and keep power. It argues that a prince must be guided by **necessity**, not conventional morality; that it is "**safer to be feared than loved**" if one cannot be both; that he must be like both the **lion** (strength) and the **fox** (cunning); and that the **ends** (stability of the state) may justify harsh **means**. The model prince is **Cesare Borgia**. The book made "**Machiavellian**" a byword for cunning, unscrupulous politics, and Elizabethan dramatists portrayed the "Machiavel" as a stage villain (Marlowe's *The Jew of Malta* opens with a prologue spoken by "Machevill"; Shakespeare's Richard III and Iago are Machiavellian types).

::: quote
"It is much safer to be feared than loved, if one must choose." — *The Prince*, Chapter XVII

"A prince must be a fox to recognise traps, and a lion to frighten wolves." — *The Prince*, Chapter XVIII
:::

### 57.2.3 Sir David Lindsay (c. 1490–1555)

::: writer
**Nationality:** Scottish · **Post:** Lord Lyon King of Arms (chief herald of Scotland) · **Masterpiece:** ***Ane Pleasant Satyre of the Thrie Estaitis*** (*A Satire of the Three Estates*, performed 1540/1552) · **Other works:** *The Dreme*, *The Testament of the Papyngo* (parrot), *The Monarche*
:::

**Lindsay** was a courtier and poet at the court of King James V of Scotland. His ***Satire of the Three Estates*** is a long **morality play** and the most important Scottish drama before the modern era. It satirises the corruption of the three estates of society — the **clergy (Spiritualitie)**, the **nobility (Temporalitie)** and the **merchants (Merchandis)** — through allegorical characters such as **Rex Humanitas** (the King), **Sensualitie**, **Flattery**, **Falset (Falsehood)**, **Dissait (Deceit)**, **Divyne Correctioun** and the poor man **John the Commonweill**. Its attack on the abuses of the Church helped prepare the Scottish Reformation.

### 57.2.4 Nicholas Udall (1504–1556)

::: writer
**Profession:** schoolmaster (headmaster of Eton, later of Westminster School) · **Work:** ***Ralph Roister Doister*** (c. 1552) — the **first regular English comedy**
:::

***Ralph Roister Doister*** is modelled on the Latin comedies of **Plautus** (*Miles Gloriosus*) and **Terence**, with five acts and rhyming doggerel verse; it was probably written for schoolboys to act. The vain, cowardly braggart **Ralph Roister Doister** woos the rich widow **Dame Christian Custance**, who is engaged to the merchant **Gawin Goodluck**. Prompted by the mischievous parasite **Matthew Merygreeke**, Ralph sends her a love letter which Merygreeke reads aloud with wrong punctuation so that it insults her; his attempt to win her by force is beaten off by her maids with brooms. Goodluck returns, and all ends happily. The play is famous in exam questions as the **first English comedy**. (The second early comedy is ***Gammer Gurton's Needle***, c. 1553, attributed to William Stevenson.)

### 57.2.5 Wyatt and Surrey — The Sonneteers

- **Sir Thomas Wyatt (1503–1542)** introduced the **sonnet** into English, translating and imitating Petrarch ("*Whoso list to hunt*", "*They flee from me*").
- **Henry Howard, Earl of Surrey (1517–1547)** developed the **English (Shakespearean) sonnet** form (three quatrains and a couplet, *abab cdcd efef gg*) and was the **first to use blank verse** in English (translation of Books II and IV of Virgil's *Aeneid*).
- Their poems were published in ***Tottel's Miscellany*** (1557), the first printed anthology of English poetry.
- **John Skelton** (c. 1460–1529), tutor to Henry VIII, wrote short-lined "Skeltonic" verse (*Colin Clout*, *Philip Sparrow*).

## 57.3 B. The Elizabethan Period (1558–1603)

### 57.3.1 Background

**Elizabeth I** reigned for 45 years. Her age was one of national confidence, exploration (Drake's voyage round the world, 1577–80; Raleigh), trade, the defeat of the **Spanish Armada (1588)**, and an extraordinary flowering of poetry and drama. **Ben Jonson** called Shakespeare the "Soul of the Age", and Tennyson spoke of "the spacious times of great Elizabeth".

The first permanent public playhouse, ***The Theatre***, was built by **James Burbage** in 1576; the **Rose** (1587), the **Swan** (1595), and the **Globe** (1599, where Shakespeare's company performed) followed. Companies included the **Lord Chamberlain's Men** (Shakespeare's company; renamed the **King's Men** under James I) and the **Admiral's Men** (Marlowe's plays, with Edward Alleyn).

### 57.3.2 The University Wits

The **University Wits** were a group of young dramatists and pamphleteers, most of them educated at **Oxford or Cambridge**, who prepared the way for Shakespeare in the 1580s and early 1590s. They combined classical learning with popular stage traditions, used blank verse, and wrote on grand themes.

| University Wit | University | Major works |
|---|---|---|
| **Christopher Marlowe** (1564–1593) | Cambridge | *Tamburlaine the Great*, *Doctor Faustus*, *The Jew of Malta*, *Edward II* (greatest of the group) |
| **Robert Greene** (1558–1592) | Cambridge & Oxford | *Friar Bacon and Friar Bungay*, *James IV*, *Pandosto* (source of *The Winter's Tale*); pamphlet *Greene's Groatsworth of Wit* (1592) with the famous attack on Shakespeare as an "**upstart Crow, beautified with our feathers**" |
| **John Lyly** (c. 1554–1606) | Oxford | prose romance ***Euphues: The Anatomy of Wit*** (1578) — gave the name **Euphuism** to an elaborate, balanced, ornamental style; court comedies *Campaspe*, *Endymion* |
| **George Peele** (c. 1556–1596) | Oxford | *The Arraignment of Paris*, *The Old Wives' Tale*, *David and Bethsabe* |
| **Thomas Lodge** (1558–1625) | Oxford | prose romance ***Rosalynde*** (1590) — source of Shakespeare's *As You Like It* |
| **Thomas Nashe** (1567–1601) | Cambridge | ***The Unfortunate Traveller*** (1594) — an early picaresque novel; pamphlets; *Summer's Last Will and Testament* |
| **Thomas Kyd** (1558–1594) | (not a university man, but usually grouped with them) | ***The Spanish Tragedy*** |

### 57.3.3 Thomas Norton (1532–1584) and Thomas Sackville (1536–1608)

::: writer
**Joint work:** ***Gorboduc*** (also called *Ferrex and Porrex*), performed **1561/62** before Queen Elizabeth at the Inner Temple · **Importance:** the **first English tragedy** and the **first English play in blank verse** · **Model:** Seneca
:::

***Gorboduc*** was written by two lawyers of the Inner Temple — **Norton** (Acts I–III) and **Sackville** (Acts IV–V), later Earl of Dorset. It follows the Senecan model: five acts, a chorus, long speeches, violent events reported rather than shown, and **dumb shows** before each act.

::: work
**Summary.** **Gorboduc**, a legendary king of Britain, divides his kingdom during his lifetime between his two sons **Ferrex** and **Porrex**. Jealousy and suspicion lead the younger, Porrex, to kill Ferrex. Their mother **Queen Videna**, who loved Ferrex more, murders Porrex in revenge. The people rise in rebellion and kill Gorboduc and Videna; the nobles crush the rebels, and civil war follows because there is no heir. The play carried a political warning to Elizabeth about the dangers of an **uncertain succession** and a divided kingdom.

**Characters:** Gorboduc, Videna, Ferrex, Porrex, Eubulus (wise counsellor), Fergus (Duke of Albany), Philander, Dordan.
:::

Sackville also wrote the *Induction* to ***A Mirror for Magistrates*** (1563), a collection of verse tragedies of fallen great men.

### 57.3.4 Edmund Spenser (c. 1552–1599)

::: writer
**Titles:** **The Poet's Poet** (Charles Lamb); the "Prince of Poets in his time" (epitaph) · **Masterpiece:** ***The Faerie Queene*** (Books I–III, 1590; IV–VI, 1596) · **Buried:** Westminster Abbey, near Chaucer · **Invented:** the **Spenserian stanza** (nine lines: eight iambic pentameters + an alexandrine, *ababbcbcc*)
:::

**Spenser** was educated at Cambridge, became a friend of **Sir Philip Sidney**, and spent most of his life as a colonial official in **Ireland**, where his castle at Kilcolman was burned during the Tyrone rebellion (1598). He died in poverty in London.

**Major works:**

- ***The Shepheardes Calender*** (1579) — twelve pastoral eclogues, one for each month, dedicated to Sidney; marks the beginning of the new poetry.
- ***The Faerie Queene*** (1590, 1596) — the great allegorical epic.
- ***Amoretti*** (1595) — a sonnet sequence (89 sonnets) celebrating his courtship of **Elizabeth Boyle**, in the **Spenserian sonnet** form (*abab bcbc cdcd ee*).
- ***Epithalamion*** (1595) — a magnificent wedding ode for his own marriage.
- ***Prothalamion*** (1596) — a "spousal verse" with the refrain "*Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song*".
- ***Astrophel*** — elegy on Sidney; *Colin Clouts Come Home Againe*; *Mother Hubberds Tale*; *Fowre Hymnes*; prose: *A View of the Present State of Ireland*.

::: work
**The Faerie Queene — Plan and Summary.** In a letter to **Sir Walter Raleigh**, Spenser explained that the poem would have **twelve books**, each showing a knight who embodies one of the **twelve private moral virtues** (from Aristotle), with **Prince Arthur** representing **Magnificence** (the perfection of all virtues), seeking **Gloriana**, the Faerie Queene, who represents **Glory** and also **Queen Elizabeth I**. Only **six books** (and two cantos of a seventh, the *Mutability Cantos*) were completed.

| Book | Knight | Virtue |
|---|---|---|
| I | **Redcrosse Knight** (St George of England) | Holiness |
| II | **Sir Guyon** | Temperance |
| III | **Britomart** (a female knight) | Chastity |
| IV | **Cambell and Triamond** | Friendship |
| V | **Artegall** | Justice |
| VI | **Sir Calidore** | Courtesy |

**Book I.** The Redcrosse Knight accompanies **Una** (Truth, the true Church) to free her parents from a **dragon**. He defeats the monster **Error**, but is deceived by the magician **Archimago** (Hypocrisy) into abandoning Una, and falls under the spell of the false **Duessa** (Falsehood, the Roman Church). He is imprisoned by the giant **Orgoglio** (Pride) and rescued by Prince Arthur; he nearly yields to **Despair**, is restored in the **House of Holiness**, and finally slays the dragon after a three-day fight and is betrothed to Una.

**Characters:** Gloriana, Prince Arthur, Redcrosse Knight, Una, Duessa, Archimago, Orgoglio, Error, Despair, Guyon, Acrasia and the Bower of Bliss (Book II), Britomart, Artegall, Talus (the iron man), Calidore, the Blatant Beast (slander).
:::

::: quote
"A gentle Knight was pricking on the plaine." — *The Faerie Queene*, I.i.1

"Sleepe after toyle, port after stormie seas, / Ease after warre, death after life, does greatly please." — *The Faerie Queene*, I.ix.40 (Despair's speech)

"Sweet Thames, run softly, till I end my song." — *Prothalamion*

"One day I wrote her name upon the strand, / But came the waves and washed it away." — *Amoretti*, Sonnet 75
:::

### 57.3.5 Thomas Kyd (1558–1594)

::: writer
**Masterpiece:** ***The Spanish Tragedy*** (c. 1587) · **Importance:** established the **revenge tragedy** in England · **Possibly wrote:** the lost *Ur-Hamlet* (an earlier *Hamlet* play)
:::

**Kyd**, the son of a London scrivener, shared lodgings with Marlowe; when heretical papers were found in his room in 1593 he was arrested and tortured, and claimed they belonged to Marlowe. He died the following year in poverty.

::: work
**The Spanish Tragedy — Summary.** The ghost of **Don Andrea**, a Spanish nobleman killed in battle by the Portuguese prince **Balthazar**, watches the action with the spirit of **Revenge**. **Horatio**, son of **Hieronimo** (Knight Marshal of Spain), captures Balthazar and loves **Bel-imperia**, Andrea's former lover. Balthazar, who wants Bel-imperia, and her brother **Lorenzo** murder Horatio and hang his body in the garden. Hieronimo, half-mad with grief, seeks justice; finding none, he plans revenge. He stages a play ("*Soliman and Perseda*") at court in which the actors really kill one another: Hieronimo kills Lorenzo, Bel-imperia kills Balthazar and then herself. Hieronimo bites out his own tongue and kills himself.

**Features:** a ghost, a hesitating avenger, real or feigned madness, a play-within-a-play, a bloody climax — all later used in *Hamlet*.

**Characters:** Hieronimo, Isabella (his wife), Horatio, Bel-imperia, Lorenzo, Balthazar, Don Andrea's Ghost, Revenge, Pedringano, the King of Spain.
:::

::: quote
"O eyes, no eyes, but fountains fraught with tears." — *The Spanish Tragedy* (Hieronimo)
:::

### 57.3.6 Sir Philip Sidney (1554–1586)

::: writer
**Ideal:** the perfect Renaissance gentleman — courtier, soldier, poet, scholar, patron · **Death:** wounded at the Battle of **Zutphen** (1586) in the Netherlands; died aged 31 · **Major works:** ***Astrophel and Stella***, ***Arcadia***, ***An Apology for Poetry*** (*The Defence of Poesy*) · **All published posthumously**
:::

Sidney was the nephew of the Earl of Leicester and a favourite of the court. The story told of his death — that, though dying of thirst, he passed his water to a wounded common soldier saying "**Thy necessity is yet greater than mine**" — made him a national hero; Spenser mourned him in *Astrophel*.

- ***Astrophel and Stella*** (1591) — the first great **Elizabethan sonnet sequence** (108 sonnets and 11 songs), expressing *Astrophel*'s ("star-lover") love for *Stella* ("star"), identified with **Penelope Devereux** (Lady Rich). It started the sonnet vogue of the 1590s.
- ***The Countess of Pembroke's Arcadia*** (1590) — a long **pastoral prose romance** written for his sister Mary, Countess of Pembroke; its princes **Pyrocles** and **Musidorus** fall in love with the princesses **Philoclea** and **Pamela**; the Gloucester sub-plot of *King Lear* comes from it.
- ***An Apology for Poetry*** / ***The Defence of Poesy*** (1595) — the first major work of **English literary criticism**, defending poetry against the Puritan attack (Stephen Gosson's *The School of Abuse*, 1579). Sidney argues that poetry is superior to philosophy and history because it **teaches and delights**, presenting a "golden" ideal world better than nature's "brazen" one.

::: quote
"'Fool,' said my Muse to me, 'look in thy heart, and write.'" — *Astrophel and Stella*, Sonnet 1

"With how sad steps, O Moon, thou climb'st the skies!" — *Astrophel and Stella*, Sonnet 31

"Nature never set forth the earth in so rich tapestry as divers poets have done… Her world is brazen, the poets only deliver a golden." — *An Apology for Poetry*
:::

### 57.3.7 Francis Bacon (1561–1626)

::: writer
**Titles:** **Father of the English Essay**; Father of Empiricism / of modern scientific method · **Offices:** Lord Chancellor (1618), Baron Verulam, Viscount St Albans · **Downfall:** convicted of accepting bribes (1621), fined and briefly imprisoned · **Major works:** ***Essays*** (1597, enlarged 1612 and 1625), ***The Advancement of Learning*** (1605), ***Novum Organum*** (1620, Latin), ***The New Atlantis*** (1626/27), *The History of Henry VII*
:::

**Bacon** belongs to both the Elizabethan and Jacobean ages. A lawyer and statesman, he took "**all knowledge to be my province**" and called for a new method of learning based on **observation and experiment** (induction) instead of reliance on ancient authorities. In *Novum Organum* he described the four **"Idols"** that mislead the mind: Idols of the **Tribe**, the **Cave**, the **Market-place** and the **Theatre**. ***The New Atlantis*** describes a utopian island, **Bensalem**, governed by a scientific institution, **Salomon's House** — a model for the **Royal Society** (founded 1660). He died, it is said, of a chill caught while stuffing a chicken with snow to test refrigeration.

**The Essays** — first ten in 1597, 58 in the final edition of 1625 — are short, aphoristic, practical reflections on life, conduct and success: *Of Truth, Of Death, Of Revenge, Of Adversity, Of Marriage and Single Life, Of Love, Of Great Place, Of Friendship, Of Studies, Of Travel, Of Ambition, Of Riches, Of Gardens, Of Parents and Children, Of Envy, Of Youth and Age, Of Beauty, Of Negotiating.* Their style is condensed and epigrammatic, earning him a reputation as the most quotable English prose writer.

::: quote
"Studies serve for delight, for ornament, and for ability." — *Of Studies*

"Some books are to be tasted, others to be swallowed, and some few to be chewed and digested." — *Of Studies*

"Reading maketh a full man; conference a ready man; and writing an exact man." — *Of Studies*

"What is truth? said jesting Pilate, and would not stay for an answer." — *Of Truth*

"Revenge is a kind of wild justice." — *Of Revenge*

"Men fear death as children fear to go in the dark." — *Of Death*

"He that hath wife and children hath given hostages to fortune." — *Of Marriage and Single Life*

"Knowledge is power." (*Ipsa scientia potestas est*) — *Meditationes Sacrae*
:::

### 57.3.8 Christopher Marlowe (1564–1593)

::: writer
**Born:** Canterbury, 1564 (same year as Shakespeare) · **Education:** Cambridge (Corpus Christi) · **Died:** stabbed at **Deptford** in 1593, aged 29, by Ingram Frizer in a quarrel over the bill ("the reckoning") · **Titles:** **Father of English Tragedy**; "Marlowe's mighty line" (Ben Jonson) · **Plays:** *Tamburlaine the Great* (Parts 1 & 2), *Doctor Faustus*, *The Jew of Malta*, *Edward II*, *Dido, Queen of Carthage*, *The Massacre at Paris* · **Poems:** *Hero and Leander*, "The Passionate Shepherd to His Love"
:::

**Marlowe**, the greatest of the University Wits, transformed English drama by giving it **powerful blank verse** and **tragic heroes driven by limitless ambition** — the "**over-reachers**" — for power (Tamburlaine), knowledge (Faustus) and wealth (Barabas). He was suspected of atheism and of working as a government spy. His early death cut short a career that rivalled Shakespeare's.

::: work
***Tamburlaine the Great*** (c. 1587). The Scythian shepherd **Tamburlaine** rises by sheer will and military genius to conquer vast empires, defeating the Persian kings, the Turkish emperor **Bajazeth** (whom he keeps in a cage) and the Sultan of Egypt, whose daughter **Zenocrate** he loves and marries. In Part 2 he kills his own cowardly son, rages at Zenocrate's death, and finally dies, defeated only by mortality. Famous for the "high astounding terms" of its verse.
:::

::: work
***The Tragical History of Doctor Faustus*** (c. 1592). **Faustus**, a brilliant scholar of Wittenberg, tired of the limits of human learning, turns to **black magic**. He summons the devil **Mephistophilis** and signs a contract in his own blood with **Lucifer**: in return for **twenty-four years** of power and pleasure, with Mephistophilis as his servant, he gives up his soul. The **Good Angel** and **Bad Angel** argue over him; the **Old Man** urges repentance. Faustus wastes his powers on tricks — playing jokes on the Pope, entertaining the Emperor Charles V, fetching grapes out of season — and calls up the spirit of **Helen of Troy**. On his last night, in an agonised soliloquy, he begs time to stand still, but at midnight devils carry him off to hell. The Chorus warns the audience against seeking forbidden knowledge.

**Characters:** Faustus, Mephistophilis, Lucifer, Beelzebub, Good Angel, Bad Angel, Valdes and Cornelius (magicians), Wagner (his servant), the Seven Deadly Sins, the Old Man, Helen of Troy, the Pope, Charles V.
:::

::: work
***The Jew of Malta*** (c. 1590). **Barabas**, a rich Jewish merchant of Malta, whose wealth is seized by the Governor **Ferneze**, takes bloody revenge, poisoning a whole nunnery (including his own daughter **Abigail**), and plots with the Turks; he finally falls into a boiling cauldron he had prepared for others. Its prologue is spoken by "Machevill" (Machiavelli). It influenced Shakespeare's *The Merchant of Venice*.
:::

::: work
***Edward II*** (c. 1592). A historical tragedy of the weak **King Edward II**, whose infatuation with his favourite **Piers Gaveston** angers the barons; his queen **Isabella** and **Mortimer** rebel; Edward is deposed, imprisoned and brutally murdered. It influenced Shakespeare's *Richard II*.
:::

::: quote
"Was this the face that launch'd a thousand ships, / And burnt the topless towers of Ilium? / Sweet Helen, make me immortal with a kiss." — *Doctor Faustus*

"Why, this is hell, nor am I out of it." — *Doctor Faustus* (Mephistophilis)

"Stand still, you ever-moving spheres of heaven, / That time may cease, and midnight never come." — *Doctor Faustus*

"Come live with me and be my love, / And we will all the pleasures prove." — *The Passionate Shepherd to His Love*

"Who ever loved that loved not at first sight?" — *Hero and Leander* (quoted by Shakespeare in *As You Like It*)
:::
