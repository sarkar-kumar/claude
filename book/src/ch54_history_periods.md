## 54.1 A Short History of English Literature

**English literature** is the body of writing produced in the English language, beginning with the Anglo-Saxon (Old English) poetry of the seventh century and continuing to the global literature in English of today. Its history reflects the history of the English people and language: invasions and conquests, religious change, the growth of cities and print, empire, industrialisation, two world wars and decolonisation.

The language itself passed through three main stages, which correspond to the first literary periods:

| Stage of the language | Approximate dates | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Old English (Anglo-Saxon)** | c. 450–1066 | *Hwæt! We Gardena in geardagum…* (opening of *Beowulf*) |
| **Middle English** | 1066–c. 1500 | *Whan that Aprille with his shoures soote…* (Chaucer) |
| **Modern English** (Early Modern from c. 1500) | c. 1500–present | *To be, or not to be: that is the question.* (Shakespeare) |

### 54.1.1 The Main Movements in Outline

1. **Anglo-Saxon (Old English) literature (c. 450–1066)** — oral, heroic and Christian poetry (*Beowulf*, Caedmon, Cynewulf) and prose (Bede's Latin *Ecclesiastical History*, King Alfred's translations, the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*).
2. **Anglo-Norman and Middle English literature (1066–1500)** — after the Norman Conquest, French and Latin dominate; English revives in the fourteenth century with Chaucer, Langland, the *Gawain*-poet and Wycliffe's Bible; Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur* (printed by Caxton, 1485) closes the period.
3. **The Renaissance (c. 1500–1660)** — the revival of classical learning, humanism and the Reformation; More's *Utopia*; the sonnet arrives (Wyatt, Surrey); the great Elizabethan age of drama and poetry (Spenser, Sidney, Marlowe, Shakespeare, Jonson); the Jacobean and Caroline periods (Donne and the Metaphysicals, Webster, the Cavalier poets); the Puritan Commonwealth (Milton's prose, Hobbes).
4. **The Neo-Classical Period (1660–1798)** — Restoration (Dryden, Restoration comedy, Bunyan, Milton's *Paradise Lost*), the Augustan Age (Pope, Swift, Addison and Steele, Defoe), and the Age of Sensibility/Age of Johnson (Johnson, Goldsmith, Gray, the rise of the novel with Richardson and Fielding).
5. **The Romantic Period (1798–1832)** — imagination, nature and the individual: Blake, Wordsworth, Coleridge, Byron, Shelley, Keats, Scott, Jane Austen, Lamb.
6. **The Victorian Period (1832/1837–1901)** — the novel at its height (Dickens, Thackeray, the Brontës, George Eliot, Hardy), poetry (Tennyson, Browning, Arnold), science and doubt (Darwin), social criticism (Carlyle, Ruskin, Mill).
7. **The Modern Period (1901–1945)** — Edwardian and Georgian writing, then Modernism: experiment and fragmentation (Joyce, Woolf, Eliot, Yeats, Lawrence), the shock of the World Wars, the Irish Literary Revival, American Modernism (Frost, Hemingway, Faulkner).
8. **The Post-Modern / Contemporary Period (1945–present)** — absurdist drama (Beckett, Pinter), the Angry Young Men (Osborne), postcolonial literature (Achebe, Soyinka, Rushdie, Walcott), feminist writing (Plath, Lessing, Morrison), and global English literature.

::: note
**Variations in dating.** Different books use slightly different dates: the Renaissance is sometimes dated **1485–1660** or **1558–1660**; the Romantic Age **1785–1830** or **1798–1832**; the Victorian Age **1832–1901** or **1837–1901**. In exams, the dates in the table above are the safest. Remember the **landmark years**: 1066 (Norman Conquest), 1340 (birth of Chaucer, approx.), 1400 (death of Chaucer), 1476 (Caxton's press), 1558 (Elizabeth I's accession), 1603 (James I), 1660 (Restoration), 1798 (*Lyrical Ballads*), 1832 (Reform Act; death of Scott), 1837 (Victoria's accession), 1901 (Victoria's death), 1922 (*Ulysses* and *The Waste Land*), 1945 (end of World War II).

## 54.3 Characteristics of Each Period

### 54.3.1 Old English Period (450–1066)

- Germanic tribes (Angles, Saxons, Jutes) bring their language and oral poetry to Britain.
- Poetry is **alliterative** (no rhyme), with a **caesura** in each line and **kennings** (*whale-road* = sea).
- Two strands: **pagan heroic** (*Beowulf*, *Widsith*) and **Christian** (Caedmon, Cynewulf, *The Dream of the Rood*).
- Prose develops under **King Alfred**; the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* records history.

### 54.3.2 Middle English Period (1066–1500)

- The Norman Conquest (1066) makes **French** the language of the court and **Latin** the language of the Church; English survives among common people.
- **Romances** (Arthurian, chivalric) and **ballads** flourish.
- The fourteenth century brings a revival: **Chaucer** (father of English poetry), **Langland** (*Piers Plowman*), **Gower**, the **Pearl-/Gawain-poet**, **Wycliffe** (Bible translation).
- The fifteenth century is called the **"Barren Age"** or **"Dark Age"** of English literature (few great writers), but it sees **Caxton's printing press (1476)** and **Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur* (1485)**, plus miracle, mystery and morality plays (*Everyman*).

### 54.3.3 The Renaissance Period (1500–1660)

- **Renaissance** means *rebirth* — the revival of classical Greek and Latin learning, beginning in Italy (Petrarch, Dante, Boccaccio, Machiavelli) and reaching England around 1500.
- **Humanism** (Erasmus, Thomas More), the **Reformation** (Henry VIII's break with Rome, 1534), voyages of discovery and a confident nationalism under **Elizabeth I** (defeat of the Spanish Armada, 1588).
- The **sonnet** is introduced (Wyatt, Surrey); **blank verse** first used by Surrey and perfected by Marlowe and Shakespeare.
- **Golden Age of English drama**: the University Wits, Kyd, Marlowe, Shakespeare, Jonson; first public theatre (*The Theatre*, 1576), the Globe (1599).
- Jacobean period: **Metaphysical poetry** (Donne), satiric comedy (Jonson), revenge tragedy (Webster), the **King James Bible (1611)**.
- Caroline period: **Cavalier poets** (Herrick, Lovelace, Suckling, Carew) and religious poets (Herbert).
- Commonwealth: Puritan rule, theatres closed (1642–1660), Milton's political prose, Hobbes's *Leviathan* (1651).

### 54.3.4 The Neo-Classical Period (1660–1798)

- Writers imitate the **classical** (Greek and Roman) models; reason, order, decorum, wit and correctness are valued.
- **Restoration (1660–1700)**: Charles II restored; theatres reopen; **Restoration comedy of manners** (Congreve, Wycherley, Etherege); Dryden (age named after him — **Age of Dryden**); Milton's *Paradise Lost*; Bunyan's *The Pilgrim's Progress*.
- **Augustan Age (1700–1745)** (also **Age of Pope**): satire, the **heroic couplet**, periodical essays (*The Tatler*, *The Spectator*), the rise of the **novel** (Defoe, Swift's satire).
- **Age of Sensibility / Age of Johnson (1745–1798)**: Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755); novels of Richardson, Fielding, Smollett, Sterne; **Transitional/Pre-Romantic poets** (Gray, Collins, Cowper, Burns, Blake) who prepare the way for Romanticism.

### 54.3.5 The Romantic Period (1798–1832)

- Begins with **Wordsworth and Coleridge's *Lyrical Ballads* (1798)**, whose Preface (1800) defines poetry as "the spontaneous overflow of powerful feelings".
- Emphasis on **imagination, emotion, nature, the individual, the common man, the past (medievalism), the supernatural and the exotic**; revolt against neo-classical rules.
- Inspired by the **French Revolution (1789)** and Rousseau.
- **First generation**: Blake, Wordsworth, Coleridge, Southey; **second generation**: Byron, Shelley, Keats.
- Prose: Lamb, Hazlitt, De Quincey (essays); Scott (historical novel), Austen (novel of manners).

### 54.3.6 The Victorian Period (1832/1837–1901)

- Reign of Queen Victoria; industrial revolution, empire, scientific discovery (Darwin's *On the Origin of Species*, 1859), religious doubt, social reform.
- The **novel** dominates: Dickens, Thackeray, Charlotte and Emily Brontë, George Eliot, Trollope, Hardy, Stevenson.
- Poetry: Tennyson (Poet Laureate), Robert Browning (**dramatic monologue**), Elizabeth Barrett Browning, Matthew Arnold, the **Pre-Raphaelites** (D. G. Rossetti, Christina Rossetti, Swinburne, William Morris), Hopkins (sprung rhythm).
- Prose thinkers: Carlyle, Ruskin, Mill, Newman, Arnold, Pater; **Aestheticism** (Wilde: "art for art's sake").

### 54.3.7 The Modern Period (1901–1945)

- Loss of Victorian certainties; influence of Freud, Marx, Einstein, Frazer; the **First World War** (1914–18) and its disillusionment.
- **Modernism**: experiment with form, **stream of consciousness** (Joyce, Woolf), fragmentation and myth (Eliot's *The Waste Land*), Imagism (Pound).
- **Irish Literary Revival** (Yeats, Synge, Lady Gregory); **Georgian poets**; **War poets** (Owen, Sassoon, Brooke).
- Drama of ideas (Shaw); the "problem play" influence of **Ibsen**.

### 54.3.8 The Post-Modern Period (1945–present)

- After the Second World War and the atomic bomb: anxiety, absurdity, loss of grand narratives.
- **Theatre of the Absurd** (Beckett, Pinter, Ionesco); **Angry Young Men** (Osborne, Amis, Wain); **The Movement** poets (Larkin).
- **Postcolonial literature** in English from Africa, the Caribbean and South Asia (Achebe, Soyinka, Naipaul, Walcott, Rushdie, Narayan, Desai).
- **Feminist** and **confessional** writing (Plath, Lessing, Morrison); magical realism; metafiction; globalisation.
