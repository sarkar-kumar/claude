# Inversion {#ch16}

[Part A · Grammar]{.chpart}

## 16.1 What Is Inversion?

In a normal English statement, the **subject comes before the verb**: *He* **is** *happy. I* **have** *never seen such a sight.* **Inversion** means reversing this order — putting the **verb (or the auxiliary) before the subject**.

::: ex
- Normal: I have **never** seen such a sight.
- Inverted: **Never have I** seen such a sight.
:::

There are two types:

1. **Subject–auxiliary inversion** — only the auxiliary (*be, have, do, can, will*…) moves before the subject: **Never have I** *seen…* / **Seldom does he** *come…*
2. **Subject–verb (full) inversion** — the whole main verb moves before the subject (usually with *be* or verbs of place/movement): **Here comes** *the bus.* / **On the hill stood** *an old castle.*

Inversion is used in questions, in some conditionals, after negative/restrictive adverbs placed first, for emphasis and in literary style. It is a regular item in BCS, Bank and university admission tests (often as error detection: *"Seldom he comes here"*), and it is an important topic in SAT/GRE/GMAT sentence correction and reading.

## 16.2 Inversion in Questions

Questions are the most common form of inversion.

::: ex
- **Are you** ready? / **Has he** come? / **Can she** swim? / **Will they** go?
- With ordinary verbs, *do/does/did* is added: **Does he** live here? **Did you** see him?
- Wh-questions: Where **are you** going? What **did he** say? Why **is she** crying?
:::

::: note
**No inversion when the wh-word is the subject:** *Who* **broke** *the glass?* (✘ Who did break…) / *What* **happened**? (✘ What did happen?)

**No inversion in indirect (embedded) questions:** ✘ *I don't know where* **is he**. ✔ *I don't know where* **he is**. (Chapter 37.)
:::

## 16.3 Inversion after Negative and Restrictive Adverbials at the Beginning

When a **negative** or **restrictive** word or phrase is placed **at the beginning** of a sentence for emphasis, **subject–auxiliary inversion** is compulsory. This is the most tested type.

### 16.3.1 The Trigger Words

*Never, never before, seldom, rarely, hardly, scarcely, barely, little, no sooner, not only, not until, not once, nowhere, nor, neither, at no time, in no way, on no account, under no circumstances, in no case, by no means, only then, only after, only when, only by, only in this way, only if, not a single (word), not till, no longer, few times.*

| Normal order | Inverted (emphatic) order |
|---|---|
| I have never seen such a beautiful place. | **Never have I** seen such a beautiful place. |
| He seldom comes here. | **Seldom does he** come here. |
| We rarely meet these days. | **Rarely do we** meet these days. |
| I little knew that he was a thief. | **Little did I** know that he was a thief. |
| He had hardly reached the station when the train left. | **Hardly had he** reached the station **when** the train left. |
| He had scarcely sat down when the phone rang. | **Scarcely had he** sat down **when** the phone rang. |
| He had no sooner arrived than he fell ill. | **No sooner had he** arrived **than** he fell ill. |
| He is not only intelligent but also hard-working. | **Not only is he** intelligent **but** he is **also** hard-working. |
| You must not open this door under any circumstances. | **Under no circumstances must you** open this door. |
| You should not leave the room on any account. | **On no account should you** leave the room. |
| I did not realise my mistake until he told me. | **Not until he told me did I** realise my mistake. |
| I could not find my keys anywhere. | **Nowhere could I** find my keys. |
| I understood the problem only then. | **Only then did I** understand the problem. |
| We can succeed only by working hard. | **Only by working hard can we** succeed. |
| He did not say a single word. | **Not a single word did he** say. |
| I will never trust him again. | **Never again will I** trust him. |
| He was in no way responsible. | **In no way was he** responsible. |
| We were at no time informed. | **At no time were we** informed. |

::: rule
**Correlative pairs after inversion:**

- **No sooner … than** (✘ no sooner … when / then)
- **Hardly / Scarcely / Barely … when / before** (✘ hardly … than)
- **Not only … but also**

✘ *No sooner had he left* **when** *it started raining.* ✔ … **than** it started raining.

✘ *Hardly had I reached home* **than** *it began to rain.* ✔ … **when** it began to rain.

✘ *No sooner* **he had** *left than…* ✔ *No sooner* **had he** *left than…* (inversion is required)
:::

### 16.3.2 *Only* + Time/Manner Expressions

Inversion happens **in the main clause**, not in the *only* clause:

::: ex
- **Only when** the teacher came **did the students** stop talking. (✘ only when did the teacher come)
- **Only after** he had left **did I** realise the truth.
- **Only if** you work hard **will you** succeed.
- **Only in Bangladesh can you** see the Royal Bengal Tiger in mangrove forests.
:::

::: note
*Only* at the start without a time/manner phrase does **not** cause inversion when it modifies the subject: **Only Rahim** *knows the answer.* (✘ Only does Rahim know.)
:::

### 16.3.3 *Not until / Not till*

::: ex
- **Not until** I saw it with my own eyes **did I** believe it.
- **Not till** midnight **did he** return.
:::

## 16.4 Inversion with *So*, *Neither*, *Nor* (Short Additions)

To say that something is also true of another person, use **so** (affirmative) or **neither / nor** (negative) + auxiliary + subject.

::: ex
- I like mangoes. — **So do I**. (✘ So I do — that means "Yes, I really do")
- He can swim. — **So can she**.
- She was tired. — **So were we**.
- I don't like fish. — **Neither do I**. / **Nor do I**.
- He hasn't finished. — **Neither has she**.
- Rahim can't drive, and **nor can** his brother.
- ✘ I don't like tea. — **So do I** ✘ / **Me too** (informal) → ✔ **Neither do I**.
:::

::: tip
**Exam Tip — *So do I* vs *So I do*.** *So do I* (inverted) = "I also do". *So I do* (normal order) = agreement/surprise: "You're working hard." — "**So I am!**" (= Yes, indeed I am).
:::

## 16.5 Inversion in Conditional Sentences (Omitting *If*)

In formal English, *if* can be dropped from a conditional clause beginning with **were, had, should**, and the auxiliary moves before the subject.

| With *if* | Inverted (without *if*) |
|---|---|
| If I were you, I would accept the offer. | **Were I** you, I would accept the offer. |
| If he had worked harder, he would have passed. | **Had he** worked harder, he would have passed. |
| If you should need any help, call me. | **Should you** need any help, call me. |
| If it were not for your help, I would fail. | **Were it not for** your help, I would fail. |
| If it had not been for the doctor, he would have died. | **Had it not been for** the doctor, he would have died. |

::: rule
In the negative inverted conditional, **not** is never contracted: ✘ *Hadn't he come…* ✔ **Had he not** *come, we would have been in trouble.*

Do not use *if* **and** inversion together: ✘ *If had I known…* ✔ *Had I known…*
:::

## 16.6 Inversion after Adverbs of Place and Direction (Full Inversion)

When an adverb or adverbial phrase of **place/direction** comes first, the **main verb** (often *be, come, go, stand, lie, sit, live, run, fly*) is placed before a **noun** subject. This is common in literary style and in SAT/GRE reading passages.

::: ex
- **Here comes** the bus. / **There goes** the train.
- **Down came** the rain. / **Up went** the prices.
- **In the middle of the room stood** a large table.
- **On the hill stands** an old temple.
- **Under the tree sat** an old man.
- **Away flew** the bird.
- **Into the room rushed** the children.
- **Round the corner came** a police car.
:::

::: note
**With a pronoun subject, there is no inversion:** *Here* **it comes** *(✘ Here comes it).* *Away* **they went**. *Here* **he is**. *There* **she goes**.
:::

## 16.7 Inversion with *There* + *be* (Existential Sentences)

*There* is not the real subject; the real subject follows the verb, and the verb agrees with it.

::: ex
- **There is** a book on the table. / **There are** two books on the table.
- **There was** a king. / **There were** many people.
- **There seems to be** a problem. / **There seem to be** some problems.
- **There exist** many theories about this. (literary)
- ✘ **There is** many reasons. ✔ **There are** many reasons.
:::

## 16.8 Inversion after *So* / *Such* + Adjective at the Start

For emphasis, **so + adjective/adverb** or **such + be** may begin a sentence, followed by inversion:

::: ex
- He was **so** weak that he could not stand. → **So weak was he** that he could not stand.
- The storm was **so** violent that trees were uprooted. → **So violent was the storm** that trees were uprooted.
- His anger was such that he could not speak. → **Such was his anger** that he could not speak.
- **So beautifully did she** sing that everyone wept.
:::

## 16.9 Inversion after Comparisons with *as* and *than* (Formal)

::: ex
- She is very hard-working, **as are** her sisters. (= as her sisters are)
- He spent more money **than did** his brother. (formal; also *than his brother did*)
- City dwellers earn more **than do** villagers.
:::

## 16.10 Inversion in Exclamations and Wishes

::: ex
- **May you** live long! / **May Allah** bless you! (optative)
- **Long live** Bangladesh! (full inversion: *Long live* + subject)
- **How beautiful is** the moon tonight! (literary; normal: *How beautiful the moon is tonight!*)
- **Isn't it** lovely! / **Wasn't it** a wonderful show! (exclamatory questions)
:::

## 16.11 Inversion after Direct Speech (Reporting Verb)

In stories, the reporting verb may come before a noun subject after a quotation:

::: ex
- "Where are you going?" **asked the teacher**. (also *the teacher asked*)
- "I am innocent," **said the prisoner**.
- With pronouns: "I am innocent," **he said**. (✘ said he — archaic)
:::

## 16.12 Inversion in Literary Style — Object or Complement First

For emphasis, poets and writers may place the object or complement first:

::: ex
- **Blessed are** the meek. (complement first — Bible)
- **Gone are** the days when we were young.
- **Great was** our joy.
- **Strange are** the ways of fate.
- **Sweet are** the uses of adversity. (Shakespeare, *As You Like It*)
:::

## 16.13 Common Errors in Inversion

::: errs
1. ✘ Never **I have** seen such a thing. ✔ Never **have I** seen such a thing.
2. ✘ Seldom **he comes** here. ✔ Seldom **does he come** here.
3. ✘ No sooner **he had** reached the station than the train left. ✔ No sooner **had he** reached …
4. ✘ No sooner had he reached the station **when** the train left. ✔ … **than** the train left.
5. ✘ Hardly had he gone out **than** it began to rain. ✔ … **when** it began to rain.
6. ✘ Little **he knew** about the danger. ✔ Little **did he know** about the danger.
7. ✘ Not only **he is** rich but also generous. ✔ Not only **is he** rich but he is also generous. / He is not only rich but also generous.
8. ✘ Only then **I realised** my mistake. ✔ Only then **did I realise** my mistake.
9. ✘ Under no circumstances **you should** tell him. ✔ Under no circumstances **should you** tell him.
10. ✘ I don't like coffee. — **So do I**. ✔ **Neither do I**.
11. ✘ He can sing. — **So he can** (meaning *he also*) ✔ **So can she** / **So can I**.
12. ✘ **If had I** known, I would have come. ✔ **Had I** known, I would have come.
13. ✘ **Hadn't he** helped us, we would have failed. ✔ **Had he not** helped us …
14. ✘ Here **comes he**. ✔ Here **he comes**.
15. ✘ I asked him where **was he** going. ✔ I asked him where **he was** going.
16. ✘ Rarely **we see** such honesty. ✔ Rarely **do we see** such honesty.
17. ✘ Not until he apologised **she forgave** him. ✔ Not until he apologised **did she forgive** him.
18. ✘ Scarcely **I had** finished my lunch when he arrived. ✔ Scarcely **had I** finished …
19. ✘ Nowhere **you will** find a better place. ✔ Nowhere **will you** find a better place.
20. ✘ **Were I knew** the answer, I would tell you. ✔ **If I knew** the answer, I would tell you. / **Were I to know** the answer, I would tell you.
:::

::: gre
**SAT / GRE / GMAT Corner — Agreement in Inverted Sentences:** In inverted sentences the subject comes **after** the verb, which makes subject-verb agreement harder to see — a favourite SAT/GMAT trap. ✘ "Among the most important discoveries of the decade **was** the vaccines developed in record time." The subject is *the vaccines* (plural), so ✔ "…**were** the vaccines…". ✘ "There **is** in the archives several letters…" ✔ "There **are** in the archives several letters…". Always find the true subject after the verb before choosing the verb form.
:::
