## 3.1 The Middle English Period at a Glance

The **Middle English Period** is the stretch of English history and literature that lies between the **Old English (Anglo-Saxon) Period** and the **Renaissance**. It begins with the **Norman Conquest of 1066** and ends at about **1485–1500**, when the Tudor kings came to the throne, the printing press had arrived in England, and the new learning of the Renaissance was beginning to reach the country. It is called "Middle" because both the English *language* and English *literature* of these four centuries sit in the middle: they are no longer the Old English of *Beowulf*, but not yet the Modern English of Shakespeare.

Different textbooks give slightly different dates. The most common ones are **1066–1500**, **1066–1485** and **1100–1500**. In examinations, **1066–1500** is the safest answer, and **1066** (the Norman Conquest) is always the starting point.

For easy study, the period is divided into three smaller ages:

| Sub-period | Years | Also called | Leading figures |
|-----|----|-----|------|
| The Anglo-Norman Period | 1066 – 1340 (some books: 1066 – 1350) | The Age of French influence | Geoffrey of Monmouth, Layamon, Orm, the poet of *The Owl and the Nightingale* |
| The Age of Chaucer | 1340 – 1400 | The Golden Age of Middle English | Chaucer, Langland, Gower, Wycliffe, the Pearl Poet, Mandeville |
| The Fifteenth Century | 1400 – 1485/1500 | The Age of Transition; the Barren or Dark Age; the Age of Chaucer's Imitators | Lydgate, Hoccleve, the Scottish Chaucerians, Malory, Caxton |

::: note
**Remember:** The Middle English Period is named after the *language* (Middle English). The middle sub-period is named after its greatest *writer* (the Age of Chaucer). The year **1340** is used because Chaucer was born around then, and **1400** because he died in that year.
:::

### 3.1.1 The Norman Conquest (1066)

In January 1066 the English king **Edward the Confessor** died without a son. The English nobles crowned **Harold Godwinson** as **King Harold II**. But **William, Duke of Normandy** (a region of northern France), said that Edward had promised the throne to him. In September 1066 Harold defeated a Norwegian invader, **Harald Hardrada**, at the **Battle of Stamford Bridge** in the north. Only a few days later William landed in the south. Harold rushed back, and on **14 October 1066** the two armies met at the **Battle of Hastings**. Harold was killed (according to tradition, by an arrow in the eye) and the English army was defeated. William was crowned king in Westminster Abbey on **Christmas Day 1066** and became known as **William the Conqueror**.

The **Normans** were originally *Norsemen* (Vikings) who had settled in northern France about 150 years earlier. By 1066 they had given up their own language and spoke a dialect of French. So the conquest of England by the Normans brought French language and French culture to the country. William replaced almost all the English (Saxon) nobles, bishops and abbots with Normans. Two famous records of this time are the **Bayeux Tapestry**, a long embroidered cloth showing the story of the Conquest, and the **Domesday Book** (1086), a great survey of the land and property of England ordered by William.

### 3.1.2 England with Three Languages

After the Conquest, England became a country of three languages, each with its own job:

- **Latin** — the language of the Church, of learning, of scholarship and of most official records.
- **French** (the Anglo-Norman dialect) — the language of the king, the court, the nobility, the law courts and polite society. Most literature written for the upper class was in French.
- **English** — the language of the ordinary people: peasants, labourers, servants and small traders. It was spoken everywhere, but for about 150 years very little serious literature was written in it.

Because English was "pushed down" to the common people, it was free to change quickly, without the control of scholars. This is why Middle English is so different from Old English.

### 3.1.3 How the English Language Changed

**1. A flood of French words.** Thousands of French words entered English during this period — perhaps about 10,000, of which roughly three-quarters are still in use. They came especially in the areas the Normans controlled:

- *Government:* government, parliament, crown, state, sovereign, minister, royal, empire.
- *Law:* justice, judge, jury, court, crime, prison, verdict, accuse, plaintiff.
- *Religion:* religion, prayer, sermon, saint, mercy, faith, chapel.
- *War:* army, navy, battle, soldier, enemy, captain, siege.
- *Fashion and fine living:* fashion, dress, robe, jewel, dinner, supper, feast, sauce, roast.

A famous example is the pairs of words for animals and their meat. The **live animal** usually keeps its old English name, because English-speaking peasants looked after it; the **meat** has a French name, because it was served at the tables of the French-speaking lords:

| Live animal (English) | Meat (from French) |
|---|---|
| ox, cow | beef (*boeuf*) |
| swine, pig | pork (*porc*) |
| sheep | mutton (*mouton*) |
| calf | veal (*veau*) |
| deer | venison (*venaison*) |

Sir Walter Scott later made this point in the first chapter of his novel ***Ivanhoe*** (1819), in a conversation between the jester Wamba and the swineherd Gurth.

**2. Simpler grammar.** Old English had many word-endings (inflections) to show case, number and gender, as modern German still does. In Middle English most of these endings were lost. Grammatical gender disappeared, the plural ending *-s* spread to most nouns, and word order (Subject – Verb – Object) became fixed and important.

**3. New spellings.** French-trained scribes changed English spelling. Old English *cw* became *qu* (*cwen* → *queen*), *sc* became *sh* (*scip* → *ship*), *c* became *ch* (*cild* → *child*), and *u* became *ou* (*hus* → *house*). The old letters **þ** (thorn) and **ð** (eth) were slowly replaced by *th*.

**4. Dialects.** Middle English had five main dialects: **Northern, East Midland, West Midland, Southern** and **Kentish**. Writers wrote in their own local dialect; for example, *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight* is in a North-West Midland dialect, while Chaucer wrote in the London dialect. The **East Midland dialect**, the speech of **London**, slowly became the basis of **Standard English**. The reasons were: London was the capital and the centre of trade; the universities of Oxford and Cambridge were in that region; it was the most populous area; Chaucer wrote in it; and later **Caxton** printed his books in it.

### 3.1.4 The Revival of English: Important Dates

Slowly, English won back its place as the language of the whole nation. The main steps were these:

- **1204** — **King John** lost Normandy to the French king. The Norman nobles now had to choose between England and France; those who stayed began to think of themselves as English.
- **1215** — **King John** sealed the **Magna Carta** (the "Great Charter", written in Latin) at **Runnymede**. It limited the power of the king.
- **1258** — The **Provisions of Oxford**: King Henry III issued a proclamation in **English** (as well as French and Latin) — the first official government document in English since the Conquest.
- **1337–1453** — The **Hundred Years' War** with France. French became the language of the enemy, and patriotic feeling favoured English.
- **1348–1350** — The **Black Death** (bubonic plague) killed perhaps a third of the population. Workers became scarce and valuable, so the English-speaking labouring class grew in importance.
- **1362** — The **Statute of Pleading** made English the language of the law courts, and Parliament was opened with a speech in English for the first time.
- **1381** — The **Peasants' Revolt**, led by **Wat Tyler** and the priest **John Ball**, whose famous rhyme asked: *"When Adam delved and Eve span, / Who was then the gentleman?"* (When Adam dug the earth and Eve spun thread, who was the nobleman then?)
- **1385** — **John of Trevisa** recorded that English had replaced French as the language of teaching in the grammar schools.
- **1399** — **Henry IV** became king; he was the first king since the Conquest whose mother tongue was English, and he claimed the throne in an English speech.
- **1476** — **William Caxton** set up the first printing press in England at **Westminster**.
- **1485** — The **Battle of Bosworth** ended the **Wars of the Roses**; **Henry VII** began the **Tudor** dynasty. In the same year Caxton printed Malory's ***Le Morte d'Arthur***. Many historians take 1485 as the end of the Middle Ages in England.

## 3.2 General Features of Middle English Literature

1. **Religious and moral purpose.** The Church controlled learning, so a great deal of the literature is religious: sermons, saints' lives, prayers, Bible stories and moral guides. Even entertaining works usually end with a moral.
2. **Three languages.** Works were written in Latin, French and English. A single poet, **John Gower**, wrote long poems in all three.
3. **The romance.** The most popular kind of story was the **romance**: a tale of knights, chivalry, love and adventure, often with magic and marvels. (See 3.3.3.)
4. **Courtly love.** Many poems describe a knight's devoted, suffering and respectful love for a noble lady. (See 3.3.4.)
5. **Allegory and the dream vision.** Poets often presented ideas as persons (Truth, Reason, Death, Good Deeds) and set their poems inside a dream. Chaucer's early poems and Langland's *Piers Plowman* are dream visions.
6. **Two kinds of verse.** French-style **rhymed, syllabic verse** (used by Chaucer) existed side by side with the old English **alliterative verse** (used by Langland and the Gawain Poet).
7. **Anonymity.** Many works — romances, lyrics, ballads, plays — have no known author.
8. **Growth of realism and humour.** By the time of Chaucer, literature had begun to show real men and women of all classes, with humour and satire.
9. **Drama out of the Church.** Religious plays grew from church services into the great **mystery** and **morality** plays performed in the streets (see 3.12).
10. **Prose develops slowly.** Poetry was the main form, but prose grew steadily, through Wycliffe's Bible, the mystics, Mandeville, and finally Malory.

## 3.3 Literature of the Anglo-Norman Period (1066–1340)

For about a century after the Conquest, very little was written in English. The educated wrote in Latin or French. When English writing returned in about 1200, it was mostly religious. The important works and writers are given below.

### 3.3.1 Chronicles and the Birth of the Arthurian Legend

**The Peterborough Chronicle.** The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* (a year-by-year history begun in King Alfred's time) was continued at Peterborough Abbey until **1154**. Its last entries show Old English changing into Middle English, and it describes the sufferings of the people during the civil war of King Stephen's reign.

**Geoffrey of Monmouth — *Historia Regum Britanniae* (c. 1136).** *The History of the Kings of Britain*, written in **Latin**, is a mostly legendary history of Britain from its supposed founder **Brutus** (a great-grandson of the Trojan hero Aeneas) down to the 7th century. It gave the first full story of **King Arthur**, **Merlin** and Arthur's wife **Guinevere**. It is also the source of the stories of **King Lear** (Leir) and **Cymbeline** later used by **Shakespeare**. Geoffrey also wrote the *Vita Merlini* (Life of Merlin).

**Wace — *Roman de Brut* (1155).** A Norman poet from Jersey, Wace turned Geoffrey's history into French verse and dedicated it to **Queen Eleanor of Aquitaine**. He was the **first writer to mention the Round Table**.

**Layamon — *Brut* (c. 1190–1215).** Layamon (also written **Laȝamon**), a priest of Areley Kings in Worcestershire, translated and expanded Wace's poem into **English alliterative verse** of about 16,000 lines. It is the **first poem in English to tell the story of King Arthur**, and it is the most important English poem of the early Middle English Period.

### 3.3.2 Religious and Moral Works

- ***The Ormulum*** (c. 1200) by **Orm** (Ormin), an Augustinian canon. It is a long series of verse paraphrases of the Gospels with sermons explaining them. It is famous for Orm's peculiar spelling system: he doubled consonants after short vowels, which tells us how English was pronounced in his time.
- ***The Owl and the Nightingale*** (c. 1200). A lively poem of about 1,800 lines in octosyllabic (eight-syllable) rhyming couplets. It is a **debate** between a grave, serious **Owl**, who stands for wisdom, religion and sober poetry, and a cheerful **Nightingale**, who stands for love, joy and song. They agree to let **Master Nicholas of Guildford** judge the argument, and the poem ends before a verdict is given. Nicholas may have been the author. It is the **earliest debate poem in English** and shows real humour and liveliness.
- ***Ancrene Riwle*** or ***Ancrene Wisse*** (*The Rule* or *Guide for Anchoresses*, early 13th century). A book of advice in **prose** written for three sisters who lived as religious recluses (anchoresses). It is gentle, practical and full of homely examples, and is one of the finest pieces of early Middle English prose.
- ***Cursor Mundi*** (*The Runner of the World*, c. 1300). A huge religious poem of about 30,000 lines telling the history of the world from the Creation to Doomsday.
- **Robert Mannyng of Brunne — *Handlyng Synne*** (1303). A collection of stories illustrating the various sins, translated from a French work. Its lively tales give a picture of everyday life.

### 3.3.3 The Romance

The **romance** (from *romanz*, the old word for the French language) was a long story, in verse and later in prose, about knights, their adventures, their battles, their love for noble ladies, and the marvels they met. The French poet **Jean Bodel** (12th century) divided the romances into three great "Matters" (subjects), and English critics later added a fourth:

| The "Matter" | Subject | Examples |
|----|-----|-------|
| Matter of Britain | King Arthur and his knights | Layamon's *Brut*, *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*, Malory's *Le Morte d'Arthur* |
| Matter of France | Charlemagne and his warriors (the Paladins) | *The Song of Roland* (*Chanson de Roland*) |
| Matter of Rome | Stories from ancient Greece and Rome: Troy, Thebes, Alexander | Chaucer's *Troilus and Criseyde*, Lydgate's *Troy Book* |
| Matter of England | English heroes | *King Horn*, *Havelok the Dane*, *Guy of Warwick*, *Bevis of Hampton* |

- ***King Horn*** (c. 1225) — the **earliest surviving romance in English**. Prince Horn is cast out to sea by invaders, wins the love of the princess Rymenhild, and returns to win back his kingdom.
- ***Havelok the Dane*** (c. 1280–1300) — Havelok, the rightful heir to the Danish throne, is brought up in poverty by a fisherman called Grim (the legendary founder of the town of Grimsby) and finally regains his kingdom.
- ***Sir Orfeo*** (c. 1300) — a **Breton lay** (a short romance in the style of Brittany) that retells the Greek myth of Orpheus and Eurydice, but with a happy ending: the king wins his wife back from the Fairy King.
- **Marie de France** (12th century), a French poet living in England, wrote the famous **Lais**, short verse romances of love and magic.

### 3.3.4 Courtly Love

**Courtly love** (in French *amour courtois*, a term made popular by the critic **Gaston Paris** in 1883) was an ideal of love found in the romances and lyrics. The lover is usually a knight; the lady is of high rank and often married to someone else. The knight treats her almost like a goddess, serves and obeys her, suffers sleeplessness and sickness for her sake, and tries to become a better man to deserve her. This ideal came from the troubadour poets of southern France and spread across Europe. It influenced Chaucer's *Troilus and Criseyde*, the love of **Lancelot and Guinevere** in Malory, and, much later, the sonnets of the Elizabethan poets.

### 3.3.5 The Early Lyric

The most famous early English lyric is the **"Cuckoo Song"** (c. 1260), a joyful song to be sung as a round:

::: quotes
- *"Sumer is icumen in, / Lhude sing cuccu!"*
  [Summer has come in; loudly sing, cuckoo!]{.mean} [— "Sumer is icumen in", c. 1260]{.src}
:::

It is the **oldest surviving English song** with words and music together. In the 20th century the poet **Ezra Pound** wrote a comic parody of it called "Ancient Music", which begins *"Winter is icumen in, / Lhude sing Goddamm."* Other early lyrics include "Alysoun" and "Lenten is come with love to toune", found in the Harley Lyrics manuscript (c. 1340).

## 3.4 The Age of Chaucer (1340–1400)

The second half of the 14th century was the **first great age of English literature**. In these sixty years English finally became the language of serious literature, and four great writers appeared at almost the same time:

- **Geoffrey Chaucer** — the court poet; realistic, humorous and full of sympathy for all kinds of people; influenced by French and Italian poetry; wrote in rhymed verse in the London dialect.
- **William Langland** — the poet of the poor; a serious moral and religious reformer; wrote in the old English alliterative metre.
- **John Gower** — a learned moral poet who wrote in English, French and Latin.
- **John Wycliffe** — a religious reformer, who led the first complete translation of the Bible into English and wrote vigorous English prose.

Alongside them worked the unknown **Pearl Poet** (author of *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*), the writer known as **Sir John Mandeville**, the Scottish poet **John Barbour**, and the religious writers called the **mystics**. (Gower, the Pearl Poet and the others are described in 3.10.)

### 3.4.1 The Background of the Age

The literature of this age grew out of a troubled and changing society:

- **The Hundred Years' War** (1337–1453) brought glory at first (the victories of Crécy, 1346, and Poitiers, 1356) and then heavy taxes and defeat.
- **The Black Death** (1348–1350, with later outbreaks) killed huge numbers and shook people's faith and the old social order.
- **The Peasants' Revolt** (1381) showed the anger of the poor against taxes and the landowners.
- **Corruption in the Church.** Many monks, friars, pardoners and summoners were greedy and worldly. From **1378** the **Great Schism** gave Europe two rival Popes, one in Rome and one in Avignon. Chaucer, Langland and Wycliffe all attack these abuses.
- **The rise of the middle class.** Merchants, lawyers, doctors and craftsmen were growing rich and important in the towns. Chaucer's pilgrims include many of them.
- **Contact with Italy.** Chaucer travelled to Italy and read **Dante, Petrarch and Boccaccio**, bringing the early light of the Italian Renaissance into English poetry.

### 3.4.2 The Alliterative Revival

In the middle of the 14th century, especially in the **West and North-West Midlands**, poets returned to the old **alliterative verse** of Old English poetry. In this metre each long line is divided into two halves, and three or four of its stressed words begin with the same sound — for example, *"In a somer seson, whan softe was the sonne"*. This movement is called the **Alliterative Revival**. Its chief works are Langland's ***Piers Plowman***, ***Sir Gawain and the Green Knight***, ***Pearl***, the alliterative ***Morte Arthure*** and ***Wynnere and Wastoure***.

Chaucer, a Londoner, did not use this metre, and he gently mocks it through his Parson, who says:

::: quotes
- *"But trusteth wel, I am a Southren man; / I kan nat geeste 'rum, ram, ruf,' by lettre."*
  [Believe me, I am a southern man; I cannot tell a story in "rum, ram, ruf" — that is, by alliteration.]{.mean} [— Chaucer, *The Parson's Prologue*]{.src}
:::
