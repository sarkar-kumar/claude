## 3.5 Geoffrey Chaucer (c. 1343 – 1400)

::: facts
**Born:** c. 1343 (some books say 1340), London  
**Died:** 25 October 1400, London; buried in Westminster Abbey  
**Father:** John Chaucer, a wealthy vintner (wine merchant)  
**Wife:** Philippa (de) Roet, a lady-in-waiting to the Queen  
**Patron:** John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster  
**Main titles:** Father of English Poetry; Father of English Literature; the Morning Star of Song  
**Masterpiece:** *The Canterbury Tales*  
**Verse forms he introduced:** the heroic couplet and rhyme royal
:::

Geoffrey Chaucer is the greatest English poet of the Middle Ages and one of the greatest poets in the whole of English literature. Before him, English poetry was mostly religious, anonymous or rough. Chaucer gave it grace, music, humour and, above all, **real human beings**. Because he was the first English poet of the first rank, and because almost all later English poetry learned from him, he is called **"the Father of English Poetry"**.

### 3.5.1 Life and Career

Chaucer was born in **London** around 1343, the son of **John Chaucer**, a prosperous wine merchant. As a boy he became a **page** in the household of **Elizabeth de Burgh, Countess of Ulster**, the wife of **Prince Lionel**, a son of King **Edward III**. This gave him an early education in court manners and French poetry.

In **1359–60** he fought in the Hundred Years' War in France, was **taken prisoner**, and was set free when a ransom was paid; King Edward III himself contributed £16. In about **1366** he married **Philippa Roet**. Philippa's sister **Katherine Swynford** later became the wife of the powerful **John of Gaunt, Duke of Lancaster**, Chaucer's chief patron. Through this connection Chaucer was linked to the royal family.

For the rest of his life Chaucer was a busy public servant, not a full-time poet. He served three kings — **Edward III, Richard II and Henry IV**. He travelled on diplomatic missions to **France, Flanders and Italy**. His journeys to **Italy in 1372–73** (Genoa and Florence) and **1378** (Milan) were especially important, because there he came to know the works of **Dante, Petrarch and Boccaccio**. From **1374 to 1386** he was **Controller of Customs** for wool, skins and hides in the **Port of London**, and lived in a house over **Aldgate**, one of the gates of the city. He became a **Justice of the Peace** and a **Member of Parliament for Kent** (1386). From **1389 to 1391** he was **Clerk of the King's Works**, in charge of building and repairs at royal palaces, including the Tower of London and Westminster. In his last years he leased a house in the garden of **Westminster Abbey**.

Chaucer died on **25 October 1400** and was buried in Westminster Abbey. He was buried there because he lived within its grounds, not specially as a poet; but in **1556** a grander tomb was built for him, and later poets were buried near him. That part of the Abbey is now called **Poets' Corner**, and **Chaucer was the first poet buried there**.

His knowledge of so many kinds of work — the court, the army, trade, the law, the customs house, building, foreign travel — explains why he could describe people of every class with such truth.

### 3.5.3 The Three Periods of Chaucer's Work

Scholars divide Chaucer's literary career into three periods, according to the main influence on his writing.

| Period | Years (approx.) | Main influence | Chief works |
|----|----|-----|-------|
| **1. French Period** | up to 1372 | French poets, especially the *Roman de la Rose* | *The Romaunt of the Rose* (translation), *The Book of the Duchess*, *An ABC* |
| **2. Italian Period** | 1372 – 1386 | Dante, Petrarch and Boccaccio | *The House of Fame*, *The Parliament of Fowls*, *Troilus and Criseyde*, *The Legend of Good Women*, *Boece*, *Anelida and Arcite* |
| **3. English Period** (Period of Maturity) | 1386 – 1400 | His own observation of English life | *The Canterbury Tales*, *A Treatise on the Astrolabe*, short poems |

### 3.5.4 Works of the French Period

#### *The Romaunt of the Rose*

This is Chaucer's translation (only partly his) of the famous French allegorical poem ***Le Roman de la Rose***, begun by **Guillaume de Lorris** and completed by **Jean de Meun** in the 13th century. In it a young dreamer enters the Garden of Love and seeks to win a **Rose**, which stands for his beloved lady. Figures such as Fair Welcome, Danger, Shame and Reason help or block him. The poem taught Chaucer the dream-vision form and the language of courtly love. The English phrase **"castles in Spain"** (day-dreams) appears here for the first time in English: *"Thou shalt make castels thanne in Spayne."*

#### *The Book of the Duchess* (c. 1369–1370)

This is **Chaucer's first important original poem** and an **elegy** (a poem of mourning). It was written on the death of **Blanche, Duchess of Lancaster**, the first wife of John of Gaunt.

*Summary:* The poet cannot sleep. He reads the sad story of **Ceyx and Alcyone** from Ovid and falls asleep. In his dream he joins a hunt and meets a sorrowful **Knight in Black** sitting under a tree. With seeming innocence the dreamer asks why he grieves. The Knight describes his lost lady, **"good faire White"** (a pun on the name *Blanche*, which means "white" in French), her beauty, her goodness and their love. At last he says plainly: *"She ys ded!"* The dreamer replies, *"Is that youre los? Be God, hyt ys routhe!"* (Is that your loss? By God, it is a pity!) A bell strikes, and the poet wakes with the book still in his hand. The poem is written in **octosyllabic couplets** (lines of eight syllables rhyming in pairs).

#### *An ABC*

A prayer to the Virgin Mary, translated from French, in which each stanza begins with the next letter of the alphabet.

### 3.5.5 Works of the Italian Period

#### *The House of Fame* (c. 1379–1380)

A comic dream-vision in three books, written in octosyllabic couplets, and left **unfinished**. The dreamer, named **"Geffrey"**, first sees the story of Dido and Aeneas painted on the walls of the Temple of Venus. A talkative **golden eagle** then carries him up into the sky — lecturing him on science all the way — to the **House of Fame**, where the goddess Fame gives good or bad reputation to people quite at random. Next he visits the whirling **House of Rumour**, full of gossip and news. The poem stops just as "a man of gret auctorite" (a man of great authority) is about to speak. It shows the influence of Dante's *Divine Comedy* and is full of Chaucer's gentle self-mockery.

#### *The Parliament of Fowls* (c. 1380–1382)

Also called ***The Parlement of Foules*** (*foules* means "birds"). This poem of 699 lines is written in **rhyme royal**, and it is **the first poem in which Chaucer used the rhyme royal stanza**.

*Summary:* The poet reads Cicero's *Dream of Scipio* and dreams that he is led to a beautiful garden and the Temple of Venus. Then he sees the goddess **Nature** presiding over a great gathering ("parliament") of birds, who have come on **St Valentine's Day** to choose their mates. Three noble **tercel (male) eagles** all ask for the hand of a lovely **formel (female) eagle**. A long and funny debate follows among the lower birds — the goose, the duck, the cuckoo and the turtle-dove — each giving opinions according to its class. Finally the formel eagle asks for **a year's delay** before choosing. The other birds pick their mates and fly off singing a **roundel** in praise of summer. The poem may celebrate the betrothal of **King Richard II** and **Anne of Bohemia**.

It is the **earliest known poem to connect St Valentine's Day with love**, so it is often linked with the origin of the Valentine's Day tradition.

#### *Troilus and Criseyde* (c. 1385)

This is **Chaucer's greatest complete poem**, a long narrative of more than 8,000 lines in **five books**, written in **rhyme royal**. Its source is ***Il Filostrato*** by the Italian poet **Giovanni Boccaccio**. Many critics call it **"the first psychological novel in verse"**, because it shows the inner thoughts and feelings of its characters so fully.

*Summary:* The story is set in **Troy** during the **Trojan War**. **Troilus**, a young prince and son of King **Priam**, laughs at lovers — until he sees the beautiful young widow **Criseyde** in a temple and falls deeply in love. Criseyde's father, the priest **Calchas**, has foreseen Troy's fall and gone over to the Greeks. Troilus's friend **Pandarus**, who is Criseyde's uncle, cleverly arranges meetings between the two; after much hesitation Criseyde returns Troilus's love, and they are happy together for a time. Then the Trojans agree to **exchange Criseyde** for the Trojan prisoner **Antenor**, and she must go to her father in the Greek camp. She promises faithfully to return within **ten days**. But in the Greek camp, lonely and afraid, she accepts the attention of the Greek warrior **Diomede** and gives him her heart. Troilus waits in vain, learns the truth when he finds his brooch on Diomede's captured cloak, and is finally **killed by Achilles** in battle. His soul rises to the eighth heavenly sphere, from where he looks down, laughs at the sorrow of those weeping for him, and understands how foolish it is to set our hearts on earthly love instead of on God.

*Points to note:*

- Chaucer calls it **"the double sorwe of Troilus"** — his sorrow first in winning love and then in losing it.
- **Pandarus** is a brilliant comic character; from his name comes the English word **"pander"**, meaning a go-between in love affairs.
- Chaucer treats Criseyde with great sympathy and does not simply condemn her.
- In Book I, Troilus's love song (**"Canticus Troili"**) is a translation of a sonnet by **Petrarch** — **the first translation of Petrarch into English**.
- At the end Chaucer dedicates the poem to **"moral Gower"** (the poet John Gower) and **"philosophical Strode"** (Ralph Strode, an Oxford scholar).
- The same story was told later by **Robert Henryson** in ***The Testament of Cresseid*** and by **Shakespeare** in ***Troilus and Cressida***.

#### *The Legend of Good Women* (c. 1386)

*Summary:* In the **Prologue**, the poet, who loves the **daisy** above all flowers, falls asleep and dreams that the **God of Love** angrily accuses him of insulting women and love by translating *The Romance of the Rose* and by writing about the unfaithful Criseyde. The God's queen, **Alceste** (who in Greek legend gave her life for her husband), defends the poet and orders a penance: he must write the "legends" (lives) of women who were **faithful in love** and suffered for it, as the saints suffered for their faith. Nine legends follow, about ten women: **Cleopatra, Thisbe, Dido, Hypsipyle and Medea, Lucrece, Ariadne, Philomela, Phyllis** and **Hypermnestra**. The work is **unfinished**.

It is important in the history of English verse because it is **the first poem in English written in the heroic couplet** (pairs of rhyming lines in iambic pentameter) — the metre Chaucer then used for most of *The Canterbury Tales*.

#### Other Works of this Period

- ***Boece*** — a **prose translation** of the Latin ***Consolation of Philosophy*** by the Roman philosopher **Boethius**, which teaches that true happiness cannot depend on Fortune. Its ideas run through *Troilus* and *The Knight's Tale*.
- ***Anelida and Arcite*** — an unfinished poem of a faithful queen deserted by a false lover.

### 3.5.6 *The Canterbury Tales* (c. 1387 – 1400)

***The Canterbury Tales*** is Chaucer's masterpiece and the most famous work of Middle English literature. It is a **collection of stories** told by a group of pilgrims, held together by a **frame story** (a story that contains the other stories). Chaucer worked on it during the last thirteen years of his life and **left it unfinished**. It contains about **17,000 lines**, mostly in **heroic couplets**; two tales are in **prose**, and some are in **rhyme royal**.

#### The Frame Story

The poem opens in **April**. The narrator — Chaucer himself, as a simple pilgrim — is staying at the **Tabard Inn** in **Southwark**, just south of London, ready to go on a pilgrimage to the shrine of **St Thomas Becket** at **Canterbury Cathedral**. (Thomas Becket, Archbishop of Canterbury, was murdered in his cathedral in **1170** and was soon made a saint; his shrine was the most popular place of pilgrimage in England.) That evening a company of **"nyne and twenty"** (twenty-nine) pilgrims arrives, and Chaucer joins them.

The cheerful innkeeper, **Harry Bailly**, called **the Host**, proposes a game. Each pilgrim will tell **two tales on the way** to Canterbury and **two on the way back**. The pilgrim who tells the tale *"of best sentence and moost solaas"* (with the best moral meaning and the most enjoyment) will win **a free supper** at the Tabard, paid for by all the others. The Host himself will ride with them as guide and judge. Everyone agrees. Lots are drawn, and the **Knight** tells the first tale.

According to this plan there should have been about **120 tales**. Chaucer completed only **24** (some of them unfinished), and the pilgrims never reach Canterbury in the poem, nor return.

::: note
**Exam point:** The General Prologue says the company was **29 pilgrims**. With **Chaucer** they make **30**, and with the **Host** **31**. (If the "preestes thre" — three priests — are counted, the list in the Prologue actually gives a slightly larger number; this is a well-known puzzle. For exams, say **29 pilgrims**.) On the way, a **Canon** and his **Yeoman** join the group; the Canon soon rides away, and the Yeoman tells a tale.
:::

The book is compared with ***The Decameron*** of **Boccaccio**, an Italian collection of 100 tales told by ten young people who have fled from the plague in Florence. But Chaucer's frame is far more lively: his storytellers come from **every class of society**, they argue and interrupt one another, and each tale suits the character of its teller.

#### The General Prologue

The **General Prologue** (858 lines) is the most famous part of the work. After the beautiful spring opening, Chaucer describes the pilgrims one by one — their clothes, faces, horses, habits and morals. Together they form **a picture gallery of 14th-century English society**: the **nobility** (Knight, Squire), the **Church** (Prioress, Monk, Friar, Parson, Summoner, Pardoner) and the **middle and working classes** (Merchant, Lawyer, Doctor, Franklin, Wife of Bath, Miller, Cook, Plowman and others). Each pilgrim is both a real individual and a **type** of a whole class. Chaucer's method is gentle **irony**: the narrator praises everyone, but the details he reports show us their faults.

#### Summaries of the Principal Tales

**The Knight's Tale.** The first tale, a noble **romance** based on Boccaccio's ***Teseida***. **Theseus, Duke of Athens**, captures two young Theban cousins, **Palamon** and **Arcite**, and keeps them in a tower. From their window both see and fall in love with **Emelye**, the sister of Theseus's wife, and their friendship turns into bitter rivalry. Arcite is released but returns in disguise to serve in Emelye's household; Palamon escapes; the two meet and fight in a wood. Theseus arranges a great **tournament** a year later to decide who will marry Emelye. Before the battle Arcite prays to **Mars** for victory, Palamon prays to **Venus** for Emelye, and Emelye prays to **Diana** to stay unmarried. Arcite wins the tournament, but his horse is frightened by a fury sent from the god **Saturn**; he is thrown and fatally injured. Before dying he generously commends Palamon to Emelye, and after a period of mourning Palamon marries her. Thus both prayers come true. The tale shows the power of Fortune and the need to accept God's order patiently.

**The Miller's Tale.** A comic **fabliau** (a short, coarse, funny story of trickery, usually about ordinary townspeople). An old Oxford carpenter, **John**, has a young wife, **Alisoun**. Their lodger, a clever student called **Nicholas**, persuades John that a second **Noah's Flood** is coming, so John spends the night in a tub hung from the roof, while Nicholas courts Alisoun. A vain parish clerk, **Absolon**, also courts her and is tricked; he takes revenge with a red-hot plough-blade. Nicholas's cry of "Water!" makes John think the flood has come; he cuts the rope and crashes to the floor, and the neighbours laugh at him as a madman. The tale mocks the Knight's Tale: here too two young men compete for one woman, but in a low, comic setting.

**The Reeve's Tale.** The Reeve, a carpenter himself, answers the Miller with another fabliau, in which a dishonest miller, **Symkyn of Trumpington**, cheats two Cambridge students, **John and Aleyn**, and is paid back by them. The two students speak a **Northern dialect** — one of the earliest uses of regional dialect for comic effect in English literature.

**The Wife of Bath's Prologue and Tale.** In her long **Prologue**, the **Wife of Bath** tells the story of her life and her five marriages, and argues boldly — against the Church writers of her time — that experience is a better guide than authority, and that wives should rule their husbands. Her **Tale** is an Arthurian story. A knight of King Arthur's court commits a crime against a maiden and is condemned to death, but **Queen Guinevere** gives him **a year and a day** to find out **"what thyng is it that wommen moost desiren"**. He cannot find the answer, until an ugly **old woman** (the "**loathly lady**") gives it to him on condition that he grant her next request. The answer — that women most desire **sovereignty** (mastery) over their husbands — saves his life, and the old woman then demands that he marry her. On their wedding night she lectures him on **"gentilesse"** — true nobility comes from good deeds, not from birth or wealth — and offers him a choice: to have her **ugly but faithful**, or **young and beautiful** but perhaps unfaithful. The knight leaves the choice to her. Having been given the mastery, she becomes **both beautiful and faithful**, and they live happily. The Wife of Bath is one of Chaucer's greatest comic characters and is often discussed as an early voice of women's independence.

**The Clerk's Tale.** A tale of **patience**, taken from **Petrarch's** Latin version of the last story in Boccaccio's *Decameron*. **Walter**, Marquis of Saluzzo, marries **Griselda**, a poor peasant girl, who promises to obey him in everything. To test her, he takes away her baby daughter and later her son, pretending to have them killed; then he pretends to divorce her and sends her home in her simple shift; finally he orders her to prepare the palace for his new young bride. Griselda bears everything without complaint. At last Walter reveals that the "bride" is their own daughter and that both children are alive, and husband and wife are reunited. The Clerk says he learned the story at Padua from **"Fraunceys Petrak, the lauriat poete"** (Francis Petrarch, the poet laureate). **"Patient Griselda"** has become a byword for endless, uncomplaining patience.

**The Merchant's Tale.** An old knight named **January** marries a young wife called **May**, who deceives him with his squire Damyan in a garden; the gods **Pluto and Proserpina** take opposite sides. The tale is a bitter satire on marriage between old and young.

**The Squire's Tale.** An unfinished romance of the East about **Cambyuskan**, King of Tartary, who receives magic gifts: a flying horse of brass, a mirror that shows the future, a ring that lets its wearer understand the language of birds, and a sword that wounds and heals. **John Milton** refers to this unfinished tale in "Il Penseroso": *"Or call up him that left half told / The story of Cambuscan bold."*

**The Franklin's Tale.** A **Breton lay** about **truth to one's word** and **generosity**. **Dorigen**, the loving wife of the knight **Arveragus**, is courted by a young squire, **Aurelius**, while her husband is away. To get rid of him, she jokingly promises to love him if he removes all the black rocks from the coast of Brittany — rocks she fears will wreck her husband's ship. Aurelius pays a magician-scholar of **Orléans** a thousand pounds to make the rocks seem to disappear. Dorigen is in despair, but Arveragus says that she must keep her promise, because *"Trouthe is the hyeste thyng that man may kepe"*. Aurelius, moved by this nobility, releases her from her promise; and the magician, moved in turn, cancels Aurelius's debt. The Franklin ends by asking: **"Which was the mooste fre?"** — which of them was the most generous? The tale also contains Chaucer's famous lines on love and freedom in marriage.

**The Physician's Tale.** A Roman story from Livy: to save his daughter **Virginia** from the lust of a corrupt judge, **Appius**, her father **Virginius** kills her himself.

**The Pardoner's Tale.** One of the most powerful stories in the book. In his **Prologue**, the Pardoner shamelessly admits that he cheats simple people with false relics, and that his sermon always has the same text: ***"Radix malorum est Cupiditas"*** — **"Greed (the love of money) is the root of all evil."** He preaches against the very sin he practises. His **Tale** is an *exemplum* (a moral story used in a sermon). Three young **rioters** (drunkards and gamblers) are drinking in a tavern in Flanders when they hear that one of their friends has been killed by a secret thief called **Death**, who has killed thousands during the plague. Drunk and angry, they swear to find and kill Death. A mysterious, sorrowful **Old Man**, who longs to die but cannot, tells them they will find Death under a certain **oak tree**. There they find a heap of **gold florins** instead, and forget their oath. They draw lots, and the **youngest** goes to town to fetch bread and wine while the other two guard the gold. The two **plot to kill him** when he returns, so as to share the gold between them; meanwhile the youngest **poisons the wine** so as to keep all the gold himself. When he returns, the two stab him, then sit down to drink the poisoned wine — and **all three die**. They "found Death" after all. The tale is a perfect illustration of its moral and is famous for its tight construction and dramatic irony. At the end the Pardoner tries to sell his relics to the pilgrims, and quarrels with the Host.

**The Prioress's Tale.** A **miracle of the Virgin Mary**, written in rhyme royal. A little Christian schoolboy in a city in Asia sings the hymn *"Alma redemptoris mater"* as he walks through the Jewish quarter; he is murdered and his body thrown into a pit, but by a miracle he continues to sing, and the body is found. The tale reflects the religious hostility to Jews common in medieval Europe, and modern readers find it disturbing.

**Sir Thopas and Melibee (Chaucer's own tales).** When the Host asks Chaucer for a tale, Chaucer begins ***Sir Thopas***, a deliberately silly parody of the popular tail-rhyme romances of his day, with a ridiculous hero. The Host stops him rudely, calling it worthless "drasty rymyng". Chaucer then tells the long, serious ***Tale of Melibee*** in **prose**, a moral allegory in which **Prudence** persuades her husband **Melibeus** to forgive his enemies instead of taking revenge. The joke is that the poet gives himself the worst tale in the book.

**The Monk's Tale.** A series of short **"tragedies"** describing the falls of great people — Lucifer, Adam, Samson, Hercules, Nebuchadnezzar, Zenobia, Nero, Alexander, Julius Caesar, Croesus, Count Ugolino of Pisa (taken from Dante) and others. It gives the **medieval definition of tragedy**: the story of someone who falls from great prosperity into misery and ends wretchedly. The Knight finally stops the Monk because the tales are too depressing.

**The Nun's Priest's Tale.** A delightful **beast fable** and **mock-heroic** poem. In the yard of a poor widow lives a splendid rooster, **Chauntecleer**, the best crower in the land, with his favourite hen, **Pertelote**. Chauntecleer dreams that a beast like a dog is about to seize him. Pertelote laughs at his fear, says dreams mean nothing, and prescribes a laxative. Chauntecleer replies with a learned speech full of examples proving that dreams can come true — and then forgets his own warning. A sly fox, **Russell** (Daun Russell), **flatters** him by praising his father's singing, and when Chauntecleer stretches his neck, closes his eyes and crows, the fox seizes him and runs off. The widow, her daughters, the dogs and all the farm animals give chase in a wild uproar. Chauntecleer, though in the fox's mouth, cleverly suggests that the fox turn and shout insults at the pursuers; when the fox opens his mouth to speak, Chauntecleer escapes into a tree. The **moral**: beware of flattery, and do not speak when you should keep silent. The tale mocks learned argument and grand epic style by applying them to farmyard birds.

**The Second Nun's Tale.** The life of **St Cecilia**, a Roman martyr, in rhyme royal.

**The Canon's Yeoman's Tale.** The Yeoman exposes the frauds of **alchemists**, who pretend they can turn base metals into gold.

**The Manciple's Tale.** The god **Phoebus** has a white **crow** that can speak. The crow tells him that his wife is unfaithful; Phoebus kills her in anger, then regrets it, and punishes the crow by turning it **black** and taking away its voice. The moral is to guard one's tongue.

**The Parson's Tale.** The **last tale**, in **prose**: a long sermon on **penitence** and the **Seven Deadly Sins** (Pride, Envy, Wrath, Sloth, Avarice, Gluttony, Lust) and their remedies. It turns the pilgrimage to Canterbury into a symbol of the soul's journey to the "Jerusalem celestial" (heavenly Jerusalem).

**Chaucer's Retraction.** At the very end, Chaucer asks his readers to pray for him, and **"revokes"** (withdraws) those of his works that are worldly or sinful, including "the tales of Caunterbury, thilke that sownen into synne" (those that tend towards sin).

#### Other Tales in Brief

- **The Cook's Tale** — the story of an idle London apprentice, **Perkyn Revelour**; it breaks off after about 58 lines.
- **The Man of Law's Tale** — **Constance**, a Roman emperor's daughter, is twice set adrift at sea and suffers many trials, reaching Northumbria, but her faith and patience bring her through.
- **The Friar's Tale** — a greedy summoner meets a devil disguised as a yeoman and is carried off to hell.
- **The Summoner's Tale** — the Summoner's angry reply, mocking a greedy friar who is tricked by a sick man.
- **The Shipman's Tale** — a fabliau about a merchant, his wife and a monk called Daun John, involving a loan of a hundred francs.

#### Manuscripts, Printing and Translations

More than eighty manuscripts of *The Canterbury Tales* survive. The most famous are the **Ellesmere Manuscript** (now in the Huntington Library, California), beautifully decorated with pictures of the pilgrims, and the **Hengwrt Manuscript** (National Library of Wales). The tales survive in ten groups called **"fragments"**, and their exact intended order is uncertain. **William Caxton** printed the *Tales* in about **1476–77**, one of the first books printed in England, and again with woodcut pictures in **1483**. Later poets made modern versions of some tales: **Dryden** in *Fables Ancient and Modern* (1700), **Alexander Pope** ("January and May") and **William Wordsworth** ("The Prioress's Tale"). The best-known modern English verse translation is by **Nevill Coghill** (1951).

### 3.5.7 Short Poems and Prose

- ***A Treatise on the Astrolabe*** (1391) — a **prose** handbook on how to use the astrolabe (an instrument for measuring the positions of the stars), written for his ten-year-old son **"lyte Lowys"** (little Lewis). It is one of the **earliest scientific or technical works in English**.
- **"Truth"** (also called the *Balade de Bon Conseyl*, "Ballad of Good Advice") — advises the reader to flee the crowd and live with truth: *"Flee fro the prees, and dwelle with sothfastnesse."*
- **"The Complaint of Chaucer to His Purse"** — a witty poem, addressed to **King Henry IV**, in which the poet complains to his empty purse as if to a lady he loves: *"To yow, my purse, and to noon other wight / Complayne I, for ye be my lady dere."* The king responded by renewing his pension. It is probably Chaucer's **last poem**.
- **"Lak of Stedfastnesse"**, **"Gentilesse"** and **"Fortune"** — short moral ballades.

### 3.5.8 Chaucer's Art and Contribution

1. **The father of English realism and characterization.** Chaucer was the first English writer to show real, living men and women, each with a face, voice and personality. His pilgrims represent **every class of 14th-century society** except the very highest (the king and great lords) and the very lowest (serfs).
2. **Humour and irony.** His humour is broad and kindly. He laughs at human weakness but rarely condemns it. His irony is gentle: the "innocent" narrator praises the Monk or the Friar, and leaves us to see their faults for ourselves.
3. **Master storyteller.** He uses every kind of story: romance, fabliau, saint's life, beast fable, sermon, Breton lay, tragedy, allegory and parody — and fits each tale to its teller. In this sense *The Canterbury Tales* has been called a forerunner of the **novel** and of **drama**.
4. **Makes English a literary language.** At a time when the court still read French and the learned wrote Latin, Chaucer proved that English could be as graceful as any language. He wrote in the **London (East Midland) dialect**, which, partly because of his fame, became the basis of **Standard English**.
5. **New metres from France and Italy.** He brought the regular **iambic pentameter** (five-beat, ten-syllable line) into English. He introduced the **heroic couplet** (first in *The Legend of Good Women*), later used by Dryden and Pope, and the **rhyme royal** stanza (first in *The Parliament of Fowls*) — seven lines of iambic pentameter rhyming **ababbcc**. This stanza was later called "rhyme royal" because **King James I of Scotland** used it in *The Kingis Quair*; it is also called the **Chaucerian stanza**. Shakespeare used it in *The Rape of Lucrece*.
6. **Bridge between the Middle Ages and the Renaissance.** His interest in classical stories, his debt to Dante, Petrarch and Boccaccio, and his curiosity about this world rather than only the next, make him a herald of the Renaissance.

### 3.5.9 What the Critics Said

- **John Dryden** (Preface to *Fables Ancient and Modern*, 1700): *"As he is the father of English poetry, so I hold him in the same degree of veneration as the Grecians held Homer, or the Romans Virgil."* Of the pilgrims he wrote: ***"'Tis sufficient to say, according to the proverb, that here is God's plenty."*** He also called Chaucer *"a perpetual fountain of good sense."*
- **Edmund Spenser** (*The Faerie Queene*, Book IV): *"Dan Chaucer, well of English undefyled, / On Fames eternall beadroll worthie to be fyled."*
- **Lord Tennyson** ("A Dream of Fair Women"): *"Dan Chaucer, the first warbler, whose sweet breath / Preluded those melodious bursts that fill / The spacious times of great Elizabeth / With sounds that echo still."* In the same poem he calls Chaucer *"the morning star of song"*.
- **William Blake** (*Descriptive Catalogue*, 1809): *"As Newton numbered the stars, and as Linnaeus numbered the plants, so Chaucer numbered the classes of men."*
- **Matthew Arnold** (*The Study of Poetry*, 1880) admired Chaucer's *"truth of substance"*, his *"large, free, simple, clear yet kindly view of human life"* and his *"liquid diction"*, but said that his poetry lacks the **"high seriousness"** of the very greatest poets — so, in Arnold's view, Chaucer is not quite one of the supreme classics.
- **William Caxton** praised him as *"the worshipful father and first founder and embellisher of ornate eloquence in our English."*
