## 3.8 Sir Thomas Malory (c. 1415 – 1471)

::: facts
**Born:** c. 1415–1418, probably at Newbold Revel, Warwickshire  
**Died:** 14 March 1471, in or near Newgate Prison, London; buried at Greyfriars Church nearby  
**Description:** a "knight-prisoner" who wrote his great book in prison  
**Masterpiece:** *Le Morte d'Arthur* (*The Death of Arthur*), finished 1469–70  
**Printed by:** William Caxton, at Westminster, in 1485  
**Importance:** the greatest English prose work of the 15th century; the standard English version of the legend of King Arthur
:::

Sir Thomas Malory gathered the many French and English stories of **King Arthur and the Knights of the Round Table** into one great book in **English prose**. *Le Morte d'Arthur* is the most famous and influential telling of the Arthurian legend in English, and it marks the high point of English prose before the Renaissance.

### 3.8.1 Life

The author names himself at the end of his book as **"Sir Thomas Maleoré, knight"**, and asks his readers to pray that God will send him **"good deliverance"** — that is, release from prison. Most scholars identify him with **Sir Thomas Malory of Newbold Revel** in Warwickshire. This Malory was a soldier, was knighted in about 1441, and became a **Member of Parliament** for Warwickshire in 1445. But during the disorder of the **Wars of the Roses** he was accused of many crimes — robbery, cattle-stealing, extortion, violent attacks, raiding an abbey and an attempt to ambush the **Duke of Buckingham**. He was never brought to a full trial, but he spent most of his last twenty years in various **prisons**, including Newgate in London, escaping more than once. It was probably in prison, where he may have had access to a good library, that he wrote his book. He died in **March 1471**.

### 3.8.2 *Le Morte d'Arthur*

Although its title is French (Malory called it "the hoole book of kyng Arthur and of his noble knyghtes of the Rounde Table"; Caxton gave it the title *Le Morte Darthur*), the book is written in **English prose**. Malory finished it in the **ninth year of the reign of King Edward IV**, that is, between March **1469** and March **1470**.

**Sources:** Malory used the great French prose romances of the 13th century (the **Vulgate Cycle** and the **Prose Tristan**) and the English poems the alliterative ***Morte Arthure*** and the stanzaic ***Le Morte Arthur***. He shortened, rearranged and simplified them, cutting much of the religious explanation and keeping the action.

**Caxton's edition (1485):** William Caxton printed the book at Westminster on **31 July 1485** — only weeks before the Battle of Bosworth. He divided it into **21 books** and **507 chapters**, and wrote a famous **preface** recommending it to readers.

**The Winchester Manuscript (1934):** For more than 400 years Caxton's printed edition was the only known text. Then in **1934** **Walter Oakeshott** discovered a handwritten copy of Malory's work in the library of **Winchester College**. It showed that Malory had written the work as **eight separate tales** and that Caxton had edited it. The scholar **Eugène Vinaver** published this text in **1947** as ***The Works of Sir Thomas Malory***.

The eight tales of the Winchester Manuscript are:

1. The Tale of King Arthur
2. The Tale of King Arthur and the Emperor Lucius
3. The Tale of Sir Lancelot du Lake
4. The Tale of Sir Gareth of Orkney
5. The Book of Sir Tristram de Lyones
6. The Tale of the Sankgreal (the Holy Grail)
7. The Book of Sir Lancelot and Queen Guinevere
8. The Most Piteous Tale of the Morte Arthur (the Death of Arthur)

### 3.8.3 Summary of *Le Morte d'Arthur*

**The birth and rise of Arthur.** King **Uther Pendragon** loves **Igraine**, the wife of the Duke of Cornwall. With the help of the magician **Merlin**, who changes his appearance, Uther comes to her at **Tintagel** castle, and **Arthur** is born. Merlin takes the baby away and has him brought up secretly by Sir Ector. After Uther's death, England has no king. A **sword** appears in a **stone** in a London churchyard, with a golden inscription: whoever can pull it out is the true king. All the great lords fail; the boy Arthur pulls it out easily and is crowned. Later he receives the magic sword **Excalibur** from the **Lady of the Lake**. He marries **Guinevere**, daughter of King Leodegrance, who brings as her dowry the **Round Table**, at which a hundred and fifty knights can sit as equals. Arthur then defeats rebel kings and even the **Roman Emperor Lucius**, and is crowned emperor in Rome.

**The adventures of the knights.** The middle books tell the adventures of the knights of the Round Table: the greatest of them, **Sir Lancelot du Lake**; **Sir Gareth**, who serves humbly in Arthur's kitchen under the nickname **Beaumains** ("Fair Hands") before proving himself; and **Sir Tristram**, whose tragic love for **Isode** (Isolde), the wife of his uncle King Mark of Cornwall, forms the longest section of the book.

**The Quest of the Holy Grail.** At the feast of Pentecost the **Holy Grail** (the cup used by Christ at the Last Supper) appears in Arthur's hall, and the knights vow to seek it. Many fail because of their sins. Even Lancelot, the best of earthly knights, is allowed only a partial vision, because of his sinful love for Guinevere. The Grail is finally achieved by **Sir Galahad**, Lancelot's son, the perfect and pure knight who sits in the **Siege Perilous** (the dangerous seat at the Round Table, kept for the knight who would achieve the Grail), together with **Sir Percival** and **Sir Bors**. Galahad dies in a state of holiness, and the quest also costs the lives of many knights.

**The fall of the Round Table.** **Lancelot** and **Queen Guinevere** have long loved each other. **Sir Agravain** and **Sir Mordred** — Mordred is Arthur's son by his half-sister **Morgause** — expose the lovers to the King. Lancelot escapes, but Guinevere is sentenced to be burned. Lancelot rescues her and, in the fighting, unknowingly kills **Sir Gareth** and his brother **Gaheris**, the beloved brothers of **Sir Gawain**. Gawain, mad with grief, demands vengeance, and Arthur goes to war against Lancelot in France. While Arthur is away, **Mordred seizes the throne** and tries to marry Guinevere. Arthur returns. Gawain dies of his wounds after writing to Lancelot to forgive him.

**The death of Arthur.** On the eve of the last battle, a truce is arranged; but an adder bites a knight, he draws his sword to kill it, both armies see the flash of steel, and the terrible **battle near Salisbury** begins. Almost everyone is killed. Arthur kills Mordred with a spear, but Mordred, with his last strength, gives Arthur a mortal wound. The dying king orders **Sir Bedivere** to throw **Excalibur** into the lake. Twice Bedivere hides the sword; the third time he throws it, and a hand rises from the water, catches it, waves it three times and draws it under. A barge carrying three queens, including Arthur's half-sister **Morgan le Fay**, takes Arthur away to the **Isle of Avalon** to be healed. Malory says that some people believe Arthur is not dead but will come again. Guinevere becomes a nun at **Amesbury**; Lancelot becomes a priest and hermit. After Guinevere's death Lancelot wastes away and dies, and his brother **Sir Ector** speaks a famous lament over his body.

### 3.8.5 Malory's Style and Importance

1. **Simple, noble prose.** Malory writes in a plain, flowing, rhythmic prose, with short sentences joined by "and", and with lively dialogue. His style is direct but dignified, and its sadness in the final books is deeply moving.
2. **The last great work of medieval chivalry.** Written during the brutal Wars of the Roses, the book looks back with longing to an ideal age of knighthood, loyalty and courtesy — and shows how that ideal is destroyed by divided loyalties, jealousy and revenge.
3. **The first great prose romance in English** — some critics call it an epic in prose.
4. **A source for later writers.** It inspired **Edmund Spenser** (*The Faerie Queene*), **Lord Tennyson** ("Morte d'Arthur", 1842, and the ***Idylls of the King***, 1859–1885), **William Morris** (*The Defence of Guenevere*), **Algernon Swinburne** (*Tristram of Lyonesse*), **Mark Twain** (*A Connecticut Yankee in King Arthur's Court*, 1889), **T. H. White** (*The Once and Future King*, 1958) and **John Steinbeck**.
5. **Criticism.** The Renaissance scholar **Roger Ascham**, in *The Scholemaster* (1570), disapproved of the book, saying that its *"whole pleasure ... standeth in two special points, in open manslaughter and bold bawdry."*

## 3.9 Dante Alighieri (1265 – 1321)

::: facts
**Nationality:** Italian (from Florence)  
**Born:** c. May–June 1265, Florence  
**Died:** 14 September 1321, Ravenna, where he is buried  
**Beloved:** Beatrice Portinari, his ideal of love and goodness  
**Main titles:** the Supreme Poet (*il Sommo Poeta*); the Father of the Italian Language  
**Masterpiece:** *The Divine Comedy* (*La Divina Commedia*)  
**Verse form he invented:** terza rima
:::

Dante was **not an English writer**. He is included in English literature courses because he was the greatest poet of medieval Europe, because **Chaucer** read and borrowed from him and so brought his influence into English poetry, and because English poets from **Milton** to **Shelley**, **Tennyson** and **T. S. Eliot** have been deeply influenced by him. Together with **Petrarch** and **Boccaccio**, he is one of the **"Three Crowns"** (*Tre Corone*) of Italian literature.

### 3.9.1 Life

Dante was born in **Florence** into a family of minor nobility. When he was **nine years old** (1274), he saw **Beatrice Portinari**, a girl of about the same age, and fell in love with her for life. He met her again nine years later, when she greeted him in the street. Beatrice married another man and **died in 1290** at the age of about 24. Dante's love for her, which was pure and spiritual, became the inspiration of his poetry. He himself married **Gemma Donati**.

Dante was active in the politics of Florence and in **1300** became one of its six **priors** (chief magistrates). Florence was divided between two parties, the **White Guelphs** and the **Black Guelphs**. Dante belonged to the Whites. In **1302**, while he was away on a mission to Rome, the Blacks seized power, and Dante was **exiled** and sentenced to death by burning if he ever returned. He **never saw Florence again**. He spent the rest of his life wandering from city to city in northern Italy, living on the generosity of patrons such as **Can Grande della Scala** of Verona, to whom he dedicated the *Paradiso*. He died in **Ravenna** in **1321**, soon after finishing *The Divine Comedy*.

### 3.9.2 *The Divine Comedy* (c. 1308 – 1321)

***The Divine Comedy*** is a long narrative poem describing an imaginary **journey through the three realms of the dead** — **Hell, Purgatory and Paradise** — over the Easter of the year **1300**. It is regarded as the **greatest work of Italian literature** and one of the greatest poems in the world.

**About the title.** Dante called it simply ***Commedia*** (Comedy). It is a "comedy" in the **medieval sense**: a story that begins in sorrow (Hell) and ends in happiness (Paradise), written in a simple everyday language rather than in a lofty style. The word **"Divine"** was added in praise by **Boccaccio**, and the title ***La Divina Commedia*** first appeared on an edition printed in Venice in **1555**.

**Language.** Instead of Latin, the language of learned books, Dante wrote in the **Tuscan Italian** spoken by ordinary people of Florence. This made Tuscan the basis of **standard Italian**, and so Dante is called **"the Father of the Italian Language"**.

**Structure.** The poem is built on the numbers **three** (for the Holy Trinity) and **ten** (for perfection):

- It has **three parts** (*cantiche*): ***Inferno*** (Hell), ***Purgatorio*** (Purgatory) and ***Paradiso*** (Paradise).
- It has **100 cantos** (sections): one introductory canto plus **33** for each part (**34 + 33 + 33**).
- It is written in **terza rima**, a verse form Dante invented: three-line stanzas rhyming **aba bcb cdc ded** and so on, in which the middle line of each stanza gives the rhyme for the next.
- Each of the three parts ends with the word ***stelle*** ("stars").

**The guides.** Dante is the traveller. His guide through Hell and Purgatory is the Roman poet **Virgil** (author of the *Aeneid*), who stands for **human reason** and wisdom. Virgil, as a pagan, cannot enter Paradise, so there Dante is guided by **Beatrice**, who stands for **divine love, grace and faith**. At the very end, **St Bernard of Clairvaux** leads him to the vision of God.

### 3.9.3 Summary of *The Divine Comedy*

**Inferno (Hell).** The poem opens on the night before Good Friday in 1300, when Dante is **thirty-five** — "midway upon the journey of our life". He finds himself lost in a **dark wood** (a life of sin and error). Three beasts — a **leopard**, a **lion** and a **she-wolf** (often explained as lust, pride and greed) — block his way up a sunlit hill. **Virgil** appears and tells him that he has been sent by Beatrice to guide him by another road: through Hell itself.

Over the **gate of Hell** is written: *"Abandon all hope, ye who enter here."* Just inside, in a kind of vestibule, are the **neutrals** or opportunists, who never took sides between good and evil; they chase a banner for ever and are stung by wasps and hornets. Across the river **Acheron**, Hell is a huge **funnel** sinking to the centre of the earth, with **nine circles**, each lower one holding worse sinners. In each circle the punishment fits the sin (this principle is called ***contrapasso***):

| Circle | Sinners | Punishment / famous figures |
|---|----|--------|
| 1. **Limbo** | Virtuous pagans and unbaptized babies | No torment, only longing; the great poets Homer, Horace, Ovid and Lucan |
| 2. | The **lustful** | Blown about for ever by a whirlwind; **Paolo and Francesca**, Cleopatra, Dido, Helen |
| 3. | The **gluttonous** | Lying in filthy, icy rain, guarded by the three-headed dog **Cerberus** |
| 4. | The **greedy** and the **wasteful** | Pushing heavy weights against each other |
| 5. | The **wrathful** and the sullen | Fighting in, or sunk beneath, the muddy river **Styx** |
| 6. | **Heretics** | Lying in burning tombs inside the City of Dis |
| 7. | The **violent** | Against others: in a river of boiling blood; against themselves (suicides): turned into trees; against God and nature: on burning sand under a rain of fire |
| 8. **Malebolge** | The **fraudulent** (in ten ditches) | Flatterers, sellers of Church offices, false prophets, corrupt officials, hypocrites (in heavy gilded lead cloaks), thieves (tormented by serpents), evil counsellors (wrapped in flames — **Ulysses**), sowers of discord, forgers |
| 9. **Cocytus** | **Traitors** | Frozen in a lake of ice; at the centre, **Satan** (Lucifer), with three faces, chews the three greatest traitors: **Judas Iscariot** (who betrayed Christ), and **Brutus** and **Cassius** (who betrayed Julius Caesar) |

Two stories from the *Inferno* are especially famous. In **Canto V**, **Francesca da Rimini** tells how she and her husband's brother **Paolo** fell in love while reading the story of Lancelot and Guinevere together, and were killed by her husband; Dante faints with pity. In **Canto XXVI**, **Ulysses** tells how, in old age, he urged his companions to sail beyond the known world in search of knowledge, and was drowned. In **Canto XXXIII**, **Count Ugolino** tells how he and his sons were starved to death in a tower — a story Chaucer retold in *The Monk's Tale*. Climbing down Satan's body, Dante and Virgil pass through the centre of the earth and come out on the other side to see **the stars** again.

**Purgatorio (Purgatory).** Purgatory is a great **mountain** rising from the sea on the other side of the earth. Here souls who repented before death are cleansed of sin before entering heaven, and they suffer **gladly and with hope**. At the gate an angel marks seven **P**'s (for *peccatum*, "sin") on Dante's forehead. The mountain has **seven terraces**, one for each of the **Seven Deadly Sins** — **Pride, Envy, Wrath, Sloth, Avarice, Gluttony and Lust** — and as Dante climbs each terrace, one P is wiped away. At the top lies the **Earthly Paradise** (the Garden of Eden). There Virgil silently disappears, and **Beatrice** appears in a glorious procession. Dante drinks from the rivers **Lethe** (which washes away the memory of sin) and **Eunoe** (which restores the memory of good deeds), and is made ready to rise to the stars.

**Paradiso (Paradise).** With Beatrice, Dante rises through the **nine heavenly spheres** — the Moon, Mercury, Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, the Fixed Stars and the **Primum Mobile** (the outermost moving sphere). In each he meets blessed souls: the wise (such as **St Thomas Aquinas**) in the Sun, warriors for the faith in Mars, just rulers in Jupiter, and contemplatives in Saturn. In the sphere of Mars his ancestor **Cacciaguida** foretells Dante's exile. In the sphere of the Fixed Stars, **St Peter, St James and St John** examine him on **faith, hope and love**. Finally he enters the **Empyrean**, the heaven beyond time and space, where the saints are seated in the form of a vast white **Rose**. Beatrice returns to her place among them, and **St Bernard** prays to the Virgin Mary to grant Dante a vision of God. In a flash of light, Dante sees the mystery of the **Holy Trinity** and the union of God and man in Christ. His will and desire are now moved, like a wheel, by **"the love that moves the sun and the other stars"** — the last line of the poem.

### 3.9.4 Other Works of Dante

- ***La Vita Nuova*** (*The New Life*, c. 1293–1295) — a book of **31 poems** (mostly sonnets and *canzoni*) linked by **prose** commentary, telling the story of his love for **Beatrice** from their first meeting to her death. It ends with Dante's promise to write of her *"what has never been written of any woman"* — a promise fulfilled in *The Divine Comedy*. It was translated into English by the Victorian poet-painter **Dante Gabriel Rossetti**.
- ***Il Convivio*** (*The Banquet*, c. 1304–1307) — an unfinished work in Italian prose explaining philosophy to readers who did not know Latin.
- ***De Vulgari Eloquentia*** (*On Eloquence in the Vernacular*, c. 1303–1305) — written in **Latin**, it defends the use of the common spoken language (the **vernacular**) for serious poetry.
- ***De Monarchia*** (*On Monarchy*, c. 1312–1313) — a political treatise in **Latin** arguing that the **Emperor** and the **Pope** both receive their authority directly from God, so the Pope should not control the state. It was publicly burned in 1329 and later placed on the Church's list of forbidden books.
- ***Rime*** (lyric poems) and ***Eclogues*** (Latin pastoral poems).

### 3.9.5 Dante's Influence on English Literature

- **Geoffrey Chaucer** was the first English poet to know Dante's work. *The House of Fame* is partly modelled on the *Divine Comedy*; the story of **Ugolino** in *The Monk's Tale* comes from the *Inferno*; and in *The Wife of Bath's Tale* and *The Monk's Tale* Chaucer names him as *"the wise poete of Florence, / That highte Dant"* and *"the grete poete of Ytaille / That highte Dant"*.
- **John Milton's** *Paradise Lost* shares Dante's grand picture of Heaven, Hell and the fallen angels.
- **P. B. Shelley** wrote "Ode to the West Wind" in stanzas built on **terza rima**, and his last poem, *The Triumph of Life*, in terza rima. **Byron** wrote *The Prophecy of Dante*.
- **Lord Tennyson's** poem **"Ulysses"** (*"To strive, to seek, to find, and not to yield"*) develops the voyage of Ulysses in *Inferno* XXVI.
- The story of **Paolo and Francesca** inspired **Leigh Hunt** (*The Story of Rimini*), **Keats** (sonnet "On a Dream") and the sculptor **Rodin** ("The Kiss").
- **T. S. Eliot** used an epigraph from *Inferno* XXVII for "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock", echoed Dante in *The Waste Land* and *Four Quartets*, and wrote: ***"Dante and Shakespeare divide the modern world between them; there is no third."***
- **Thomas Carlyle**, in *On Heroes and Hero-Worship* (1841), described Dante as **the voice of "ten silent centuries"**.
- Famous English translations include those of **Henry Francis Cary** (1814), the American poet **H. W. Longfellow** (1867) and **Dorothy L. Sayers** (1949–1962, in terza rima).

### 3.9.6 The Italian Background: Petrarch and Boccaccio

Since Chaucer drew so much from the other two "Crowns" of Italian literature, they must be known along with Dante.

**Francesco Petrarca (Petrarch, 1304–1374)** is called **"the Father of Humanism"**. His ***Canzoniere*** (*Song Book*, also called *Rime Sparse*), a collection of **366 poems**, mostly sonnets, expresses his love for **Laura**, whom he first saw in a church at **Avignon** on 6 April 1327. He perfected the **Petrarchan (Italian) sonnet**: fourteen lines divided into an **octave** (eight lines, rhyming *abbaabba*) and a **sestet** (six lines, *cdecde* or *cdcdcd*). **Sir Thomas Wyatt** and the **Earl of Surrey** introduced this sonnet into English in the 16th century. Petrarch was crowned **poet laureate** in Rome in 1341 — Chaucer's Clerk calls him *"the lauriat poete"*. Chaucer translated one of his sonnets in *Troilus* and took the story of the Clerk's Tale from his Latin version.

**Giovanni Boccaccio (1313–1375)** wrote ***The Decameron*** (c. 1348–1353), a collection of **100 tales** told by **ten young people** (seven women and three men) who escape the **Black Death** in Florence to a country villa and tell one story each for ten days. Its frame story is often compared with *The Canterbury Tales*. Chaucer used Boccaccio's ***Il Filostrato*** for *Troilus and Criseyde*, his ***Teseida*** for *The Knight's Tale*, and his ***De Casibus Virorum Illustrium*** (On the Falls of Famous Men) for the idea of *The Monk's Tale*. Boccaccio also wrote a *Life of Dante*, gave public lectures on the *Commedia*, and first called it **"divine"**.

**A famous misquotation:** *"The hottest places in hell are reserved for those who, in times of great moral crisis, maintain their neutrality"* is often said to be from Dante, and was made popular by U.S. President John F. Kennedy. These words are **not in Dante**. What Dante actually does (in *Inferno* III) is place the **neutrals** — those who never took a side — just inside the gate of Hell, rejected by both Heaven and Hell.
