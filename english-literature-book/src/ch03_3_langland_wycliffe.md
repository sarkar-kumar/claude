## 3.6 William Langland (c. 1332 – c. 1386)

::: facts
**Born:** c. 1332, probably in the West Midlands (near the Malvern Hills, Worcestershire; some say Cleobury Mortimer, Shropshire)  
**Died:** c. 1386 (perhaps a little later)  
**Lived:** much of his life in London (Cornhill), with his wife Kitte and daughter Calote  
**Main title:** the poet of the poor; the first great poet of the English common man  
**Masterpiece:** *Piers Plowman* (*The Vision of William concerning Piers the Plowman*)  
**Verse form:** unrhymed alliterative long lines
:::

William Langland is the author of only one work — but that one work, ***Piers Plowman***, is the most important English poem of the 14th century after *The Canterbury Tales*. Where Chaucer looked at society with the amused eye of a courtier, Langland looked at it with the angry and sorrowful eye of a poor, deeply religious man. He is the voice of the **common people**, of the **honest labourer**, and of those who wanted to **reform** a corrupt Church and society.

### 3.6.1 Life

Very little is known for certain about Langland's life; most of it comes from the poem itself. His name is known from a note in one manuscript, and he hints at it inside the poem: *"I have lyved in londe," quod I, "my name is Longe Wille"* (I have lived in the land, said I; my name is Long Will) — "Long Will" may mean he was tall, and the words *londe* and *Longe Wille* hide the name **Will Langland**. He was probably educated at the priory of **Great Malvern**. He took **minor orders** in the Church but, being married, could not become a priest. In London he earned a poor living by singing psalms and prayers for the souls of the dead. He describes himself as a tall, thin, shabby man who would not bow to the rich.

### 3.6.2 *Piers Plowman*

***Piers Plowman*** (full title: ***The Vision of William concerning Piers the Plowman***; in Latin, *Visio Willelmi de Petro Ploughman*) is a long **allegorical dream vision** written in **alliterative verse**. Langland spent most of his life writing and rewriting it, and it survives in **three main versions**:

| Version | Date (approx.) | Length |
|---|---|---|
| **A-text** | c. 1367 – 1370 | about 2,500 lines (unfinished) |
| **B-text** | c. 1377 – 1379 | about 7,300 lines (the most read) |
| **C-text** | c. 1385 – 1388 | a revision of B |

The poem is divided into sections called **passus** (Latin for "step"). It has two main parts: the ***Visio*** (the Vision of the field of folk, Lady Meed and Piers the Plowman) and the ***Vita*** (the Life of **Do-well, Do-better and Do-best**).

### 3.6.3 Summary of *Piers Plowman*

**The Prologue — the Field Full of Folk.** On a warm May morning the dreamer, **Will**, dressed like a wandering hermit, falls asleep beside a stream on the **Malvern Hills**. He dreams of a **"fair feeld ful of folk"** — a field full of people of every kind. On a hill to the east stands a **Tower**, the home of **Truth** (God); in a deep valley below lies a **Dungeon**, the home of **Wrong** (the Devil). Between them the whole of human society is busy: ploughmen working hard, wasters eating up what others grow, merchants, minstrels, jesters, beggars, hermits, **friars** preaching for profit, a **pardoner** selling false pardons, lawyers who will not open their mouths without being paid, and priests who leave their parishes to earn money in London. The Prologue also contains the famous fable of the **rats and mice** who decide to hang a **bell on the cat's neck** but cannot find anyone brave enough to do it.

**Passus I — Holy Church.** A lovely lady, **Holy Church**, comes down from the Tower and explains the vision. Truth is God, the giver of all good things, and the most precious thing in the world is truth; the man in the Dungeon is the "father of falsehood". Love, she says, is the medicine of heaven and the plant of peace.

**Passus II–IV — Lady Meed.** The dreamer then sees **Lady Meed** (*meed* means "reward", but also "bribery"), richly dressed, who is about to be married to **False**. The marriage is challenged, and everyone goes to the **King's court at Westminster**. Lady Meed bribes judges, friars and officials, and everyone loves her. The King wants her to marry the knight **Conscience**, but Conscience refuses, saying that Meed corrupts everything. **Reason** supports Conscience. The King finally takes Reason and Conscience as his advisers.

**Passus V — The Seven Deadly Sins.** Reason preaches to the whole field of folk, and the **Seven Deadly Sins** come forward, one by one, to **confess**: Pride, Lechery, Envy, Wrath, **Avarice** (Covetousness), **Gluttony** and **Sloth**. These are some of the most vivid and comic scenes in medieval literature: Gluttony, on his way to church, is tempted into the tavern and drinks until he collapses; **Sloth**, a lazy priest, admits that he does not know his Lord's Prayer properly but knows the **rhymes of Robin Hood**. This is **one of the earliest references to Robin Hood in English literature**. Then a thousand people set out to seek **Truth**, but nobody knows the way.

**End of Passus V and Passus VI — Piers the Plowman.** Suddenly a simple farmer, **Piers the Plowman**, puts out his head and says he knows Truth well, for he has served him faithfully for forty years. He offers to show them the way, but first he must plough and sow his **half-acre**. He sets everyone to work. Some work hard; others, the **wasters**, refuse. Piers calls on **Hunger**, and when Hunger attacks them, even the wasters work eagerly — until the harvest comes, and they grow lazy again.

**Passus VII — The Pardon.** **Truth** sends Piers a **pardon** for himself and all who help him. When a priest reads it, it contains just two lines: *"Those who have done good shall go into eternal life; those who have done evil, into eternal fire."* The priest says this is no pardon at all. In anger and sorrow Piers **tears the pardon** in two and resolves to give up worldly work for prayer and penance. The quarrel wakes the dreamer.

**The *Vita* — Do-well, Do-better and Do-best.** In the second part, the dreamer wanders through the world asking what it means to **Do-well, Do-better and Do-best** — that is, how to live a good Christian life. He questions a series of allegorical figures — **Thought, Wit, Study, Clergy, Scripture, Imaginative, Patience** — and meets **Haukyn the Active Man**, whose coat is stained with sins. Gradually **Piers** comes to stand for something greater: the ideal Christian, and finally **Christ** himself in human nature. In one great vision, Christ rides into Jerusalem like a knight coming to a joust, wearing the "armour" of Piers — human nature. He is crucified and then **harrows Hell** (breaks into Hell and frees the souls of the righteous), while the **Four Daughters of God** — **Mercy, Truth, Righteousness and Peace** — argue and are reconciled. In the last part (*Do-best*), Piers builds the barn of **Unity** (the Church), but **Antichrist** attacks it, and the friars corrupt it from within. At the very end, the knight **Conscience** sets out as a pilgrim to find **Piers the Plowman** again, crying for grace — and the dreamer wakes. The poem ends without a final victory: the search for a truly Christian society goes on.

### 3.6.4 Themes and Importance

1. **Social satire.** The poem attacks the corruption of every class — especially greedy friars, pardoners, bribe-taking officials, lazy priests and idle beggars.
2. **The dignity of honest labour.** Langland honours the hard-working ploughman, who feeds everyone, as the true model of a good life.
3. **Religious search.** At its heart the poem is a soul's search for **Truth**, **Love** and **salvation**.
4. **Allegory.** Ideas become persons: Lady Meed, Conscience, Reason, Hunger, the Seven Sins. Its method has been compared with John Bunyan's ***The Pilgrim's Progress*** (1678).
5. **The voice of social protest.** During the **Peasants' Revolt of 1381**, the rebel priest **John Ball** used the name of "**Piers Plowman**" in his letters to the rebels, showing how famous the poem had become among ordinary people.
6. **The Alliterative Revival.** It is the greatest poem of the movement that brought back the native English alliterative metre.

### 3.6.5 Chaucer and Langland Compared

| Point | Geoffrey Chaucer | William Langland |
|---|-----|-----|
| Social position | Courtier, official, diplomat | Poor cleric in minor orders |
| Point of view | Amused, tolerant observer | Earnest moral reformer |
| Main mood | Humour and irony | Anger, sorrow and religious longing |
| Method | Realism: living individuals | Allegory and dream vision |
| Verse | Rhymed, syllabic (heroic couplet, rhyme royal) | Unrhymed alliterative long line |
| Influences | French and Italian poets | The native English tradition and the Bible |
| Readers | Court and educated middle class | The common people and clergy |
| Great work | *The Canterbury Tales* | *Piers Plowman* |

## 3.7 John Wycliffe (c. 1330 – 1384)

::: facts
**Name also spelt:** Wyclif, Wycliff, Wickliffe  
**Born:** c. 1330, Hipswell, Yorkshire  
**Died:** 31 December 1384, Lutterworth, Leicestershire  
**Profession:** Oxford theologian and philosopher; Master of Balliol College; Rector of Lutterworth  
**Main titles:** The Morning Star of the Reformation; the Father of English Prose (in some books)  
**Great achievement:** the first complete translation of the Bible into English  
**Followers:** the Lollards
:::

John Wycliffe was a brilliant **Oxford scholar**, a fearless **religious reformer** and a pioneer of **English prose**. About 150 years before **Martin Luther** began the Protestant Reformation in Germany (1517), Wycliffe attacked the wealth and corruption of the Church and declared that the **Bible**, not the Pope, is the highest authority for Christians. For this reason he is called **"the Morning Star of the Reformation"**.

### 3.7.1 Life

Wycliffe was born in **Yorkshire** and studied and taught at **Oxford**, where he became **Master of Balliol College** (c. 1360) and a Doctor of Theology (1372). He was regarded as the leading philosopher at Oxford. In **1374** he was sent by the king to **Bruges** to negotiate with the Pope's representatives, and in the same year he became **Rector of Lutterworth**. The powerful **John of Gaunt** (Chaucer's patron) protected him for many years, because Wycliffe's argument that the Church should not hold great wealth and political power suited the nobles, who resented paying money to the Pope.

In **1377** **Pope Gregory XI** condemned eighteen of his teachings. The **Great Schism** of **1378**, when two rival Popes cursed each other, made Wycliffe attack the papacy even more boldly; he called the Pope **Antichrist**. In **1379–80** he denied the doctrine of **transubstantiation** (the belief that the bread and wine of the Mass become the actual body and blood of Christ), and this cost him the support of John of Gaunt and of the University. After the **Peasants' Revolt of 1381**, many blamed his ideas for the unrest, although he did not support the rebels. In **1382** the **Blackfriars Council** in London — called the **"Earthquake Synod"** because an earthquake shook London during its meeting — condemned his teachings, and his followers were driven out of Oxford. Wycliffe retired to **Lutterworth**, where he continued writing. He suffered a stroke while hearing Mass and died on **31 December 1384**.

Long after his death, the **Council of Constance** (**1415**) declared him a **heretic**. In **1428**, on the Pope's order, his bones were **dug up and burned**, and the ashes thrown into the **River Swift** near Lutterworth. The 17th-century historian **Thomas Fuller** wrote a famous comment on this: *"Thus the brook hath conveyed his ashes into Avon, Avon into Severn, Severn into the narrow seas, they into the main ocean. And thus the ashes of Wickliffe are the emblem of his doctrine, which now is dispersed the world over."*

### 3.7.2 His Teachings

1. **The Bible is the highest authority** — above the Pope and the Church — and every Christian should be able to read it in his or her own language.
2. **Dominion by grace:** in his Latin works *De Dominio Divino* (On Divine Lordship) and *De Civili Dominio* (On Civil Lordship), he argued that all lordship and property come from God, and that a priest who lives in sin loses the right to his property. So the state could take away the wealth of a corrupt Church.
3. He attacked the **Pope's power**, the wealth of monks and bishops, **indulgences** (the sale of pardons), **pilgrimages**, the worship of images and relics, and compulsory confession to priests.
4. He denied **transubstantiation**.
5. He sent out **"Poor Priests"** — simple preachers in plain russet robes who travelled through the country preaching in English.

### 3.7.3 The Wycliffite Bible

Wycliffe's most important achievement was the **first complete translation of the whole Bible into English** (c. **1382–1395**). He inspired and led the work, though much of it was done by his followers. It was translated from the **Latin Vulgate** (the official Latin Bible of St Jerome), **not** from the original Hebrew and Greek. It exists in two versions:

- **The Early Version** (c. 1382) — very literal, following the Latin word order closely; much of the Old Testament was translated by **Nicholas of Hereford**.
- **The Later Version** (c. 1388–1395) — smoother and more natural English, revised mainly by Wycliffe's secretary **John Purvey**.

Since printing had not yet come to England, the Bible was **copied by hand**; about 250 manuscripts survive, which shows how widely it was read. In **1409** the **Constitutions of Oxford**, issued by **Archbishop Thomas Arundel**, forbade anyone to translate the Bible into English or to read such a translation without the approval of a bishop.

::: note
**The English Bible — a chain of "firsts":**

- **John Wycliffe** (c. 1382–95): the **first complete English Bible** — handwritten, translated from Latin.
- **William Tyndale** (1526): the **first printed English New Testament**, translated from the Greek.
- **Miles Coverdale** (1535): the **first complete printed English Bible**.
- **The King James Version** or **Authorized Version** (1611): the most famous and influential English Bible.
:::

### 3.7.4 Other Works

Wycliffe wrote most of his scholarly works in **Latin**: *De Dominio Divino* (1375), *De Civili Dominio* (1376), *De Veritate Sacrae Scripturae* (On the Truth of Holy Scripture, 1378), *De Ecclesia* (On the Church, 1378), *De Officio Regis* (On the Office of the King, 1379), *De Potestate Papae* (On the Power of the Pope, 1379), *De Eucharistia* (1379–80) and *Trialogus* (1382). He and his followers also wrote many **English sermons** and short **tracts** (pamphlets) for ordinary people. It is not always certain which of these Wycliffe wrote himself.

### 3.7.5 Wycliffe and English Prose

Wycliffe chose to write for ordinary people in **plain, direct and forceful English**. By putting the Bible and serious religious argument into English, he showed that English prose could carry the most important ideas of the age. His Bible spread a common English to readers all over the country and introduced many Latin-derived words into the language. For these reasons some books call him **"the Father of English Prose"**.

::: note
**"Father of English Prose" — three answers you may meet:**
**King Alfred the Great** is the Father of English Prose for the **Old English** period. In the **Middle English** period the title is usually given to **John Wycliffe**. Older books sometimes gave it to **Sir John Mandeville**, the supposed author of *The Travels* (see 3.10.3). For exams, the most common answer is **King Alfred**; for the Middle English period, **Wycliffe**.
:::

### 3.7.6 The Lollards and Wycliffe's Influence

Wycliffe's followers were called **Lollards** — probably from a Middle Dutch word *lollaert*, meaning a "mumbler" (of prayers). After his death they were persecuted; under the law ***De heretico comburendo*** (**1401**, "On the burning of heretics") many were burned. The Lollard leader **Sir John Oldcastle** (Lord Cobham) was executed in **1417**. Oldcastle is remembered in literature because Shakespeare's comic knight **Sir John Falstaff** (in *Henry IV*) was originally named **Oldcastle**; the name was changed after protests from Oldcastle's descendants.

Wycliffe's ideas spread to **Bohemia** (in today's Czech Republic) through students who had studied at Oxford. There they inspired the reformer **Jan Hus** (John Huss), who was burned at the Council of Constance in **1415**. Hus, in turn, influenced **Martin Luther**. In this way Wycliffe's "morning star" led to the full daylight of the Reformation.
