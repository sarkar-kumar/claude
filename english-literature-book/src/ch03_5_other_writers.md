## 3.10 Other Writers of the Age of Chaucer

### 3.10.1 John Gower (c. 1330 – 1408)

**John Gower**, a wealthy and learned gentleman from Kent, was Chaucer's friend; Chaucer called him **"moral Gower"** when he dedicated *Troilus and Criseyde* to him. Gower is remarkable for writing a major long poem in each of the **three languages** of medieval England:

| Work | Language | Content |
|-------|---|-------|
| ***Mirour de l'Omme*** (*The Mirror of Man*; Latin title *Speculum Meditantis*), c. 1376–79 | **French** | An allegory of the struggle of the Vices and Virtues for the human soul, and a survey of the sins of every class |
| ***Vox Clamantis*** (*The Voice of One Crying*), c. 1377–81 | **Latin** | A dream-vision of the **Peasants' Revolt of 1381**, in which the rebels appear as wild beasts, and a criticism of every class of society |
| ***Confessio Amantis*** (*The Lover's Confession*), 1390 | **English** | His best work (see below) |

***Confessio Amantis*** is a poem of about **33,000 lines** in **octosyllabic couplets**. Gower says that **King Richard II**, meeting him in a boat on the Thames, asked him to write "some new thing"; later Gower rededicated it to **Henry of Lancaster** (the future Henry IV). In the poem a Lover, **Amans**, confesses his sins against love to **Genius**, the priest of Venus. To teach him, Genius tells him **141 stories**, arranged under the **Seven Deadly Sins**, taken from Ovid, the Bible, history and legend. At the end Venus shows Amans that he is now too old for love, and he gives it up. The poem is clear, smooth and gentle, but less lively than Chaucer.

**Points to remember:** Gower's tale of **Apollonius of Tyre** is the source of Shakespeare's play ***Pericles***, in which **Gower himself appears as the Chorus**. His tale of **Florent** tells the same "loathly lady" story as Chaucer's *Wife of Bath's Tale*. He is buried in **Southwark Cathedral**, where his tomb shows his head resting on his three great books.

### 3.10.2 The Pearl Poet (or Gawain Poet)

The unknown poet called the **Pearl Poet** or **Gawain Poet** lived in the late 14th century and wrote in the dialect of the **North-West Midlands** (around Cheshire and Staffordshire). Four poems in a single manuscript, known as **Cotton Nero A.x** (now in the British Library), are believed to be his: ***Pearl***, ***Cleanness*** (or *Purity*), ***Patience*** and ***Sir Gawain and the Green Knight***. He is the greatest poet of the **Alliterative Revival**.

#### *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*

This poem of **2,530 lines**, divided into **four parts** (called "fitts"), is the **finest Arthurian romance in English verse**. Its stanzas consist of a group of long **alliterative** lines followed by five short **rhyming** lines called the **"bob and wheel"**.

*Summary:* At **Camelot**, King Arthur's court is celebrating **New Year**. Suddenly a huge knight rides into the hall — he and his horse are entirely **green**. He carries a holly branch and a great axe, and proposes a **"Christmas game"**: any knight may strike him one blow with the axe, if he agrees to receive a return blow **a year and a day** later. **Sir Gawain**, Arthur's nephew, accepts and **cuts off the Green Knight's head**. But the Green Knight calmly picks up his head, which speaks and reminds Gawain to meet him at the **Green Chapel** next New Year, and rides away.

Next autumn Gawain sets out. On his **shield** is a golden **pentangle** (a five-pointed star), the symbol of his perfect virtue. On Christmas Eve he reaches the castle of a lord, **Sir Bertilak de Hautdesert**, who says the Green Chapel is nearby and invites him to stay. Bertilak proposes another game: for three days he will go **hunting**, and Gawain will rest in the castle; at the end of each day they will **exchange whatever they have won**. Each day Bertilak's beautiful **wife** visits Gawain's bedroom and tries to **tempt** him. Gawain remains courteous but faithful, and accepts only kisses — one, then two, then three — which he honestly gives to Bertilak in exchange for the deer, the boar and the fox. But on the third day the lady also offers him a **green girdle** (sash) which, she says, will protect its wearer from death. Fearing for his life, Gawain accepts it — and **hides it** from Bertilak.

At the Green Chapel, the Green Knight swings his axe twice but stops short; the third time he gives Gawain only a **small nick on the neck**. Then he reveals that he is **Bertilak** himself. The two harmless blows were for the two days on which Gawain was honest; the nick was for the third day, when he kept the girdle. The whole adventure was arranged by the enchantress **Morgan le Fay**, to test the pride of Arthur's court and to frighten Queen Guinevere. Gawain is deeply ashamed of his failure and vows to wear the green girdle always as a sign of his fault. When he returns to Camelot, the knights laugh kindly and decide to wear green sashes too, in his honour.

*Themes:* the testing of **truth and honour**, the conflict between **courtesy** and **chastity**, the human love of life, and the imperfection of even the best of men. At the end of the manuscript is written ***"Hony soyt qui mal pence"*** (Shame on him who thinks evil of it), the motto of the **Order of the Garter**, founded by Edward III. The poem was edited by **J. R. R. Tolkien** (with E. V. Gordon, 1925) and has been translated by Tolkien and, more recently, by **Simon Armitage** (2007).

#### *Pearl*

***Pearl*** is an **elegy** and **dream vision** of 1,212 lines in **101 twelve-line stanzas**, using both rhyme and alliteration. A father mourns the loss of his precious **"pearl"** — his little daughter, who died before she was two years old. Falling asleep on her grave in a garden, he dreams that he sees her, now a radiant young queen in heaven, standing across a stream. She explains to him the mysteries of God's grace and shows him the **Heavenly Jerusalem**. In his eagerness to reach her he tries to cross the stream, and wakes up on her grave, comforted and resigned to God's will. It is one of the most beautiful and complex poems in Middle English.

*Patience* retells the story of the prophet **Jonah**; *Cleanness* uses Bible stories (the Flood, Sodom, Belshazzar's feast) to teach purity.

### 3.10.3 Sir John Mandeville — *The Travels*

***The Travels of Sir John Mandeville*** (written c. **1357–1371**) claims to be the account of an English knight of **St Albans** who left England in 1322 and travelled for **34 years** through the Holy Land, Egypt, Ethiopia, India, Cathay (**China**) and the land of the legendary Christian king **Prester John**. It describes real places side by side with wonders: men with no heads and faces in their chests, one-eyed giants, dog-headed people, a fountain of youth, and trees that grow lambs. It also states that the **earth is round** and could be sailed around.

The book was enormously popular for centuries and was read by explorers such as **Christopher Columbus**. Scholars now believe "Mandeville" never existed: the book was **compiled from other travel books** and encyclopaedias, and was first written in **French**, then translated into English (c. 1400). In older histories of literature Mandeville was called **"the Father of English Prose"**, but that title is now usually given to Wycliffe (for Middle English) or to King Alfred (for Old English).

### 3.10.4 John Barbour (c. 1320 – 1395)

**John Barbour**, Archdeacon of Aberdeen, wrote ***The Bruce*** (c. **1375**), a long patriotic poem of about 13,000 lines in **octosyllabic couplets**, in the **Scots** language. It tells the life of **Robert the Bruce**, King of Scotland, and the Scottish War of Independence against England, ending in Bruce's great victory at the **Battle of Bannockburn** (**1314**). Barbour is called **"the Father of Scottish Poetry"**. The most famous lines of the poem are a praise of liberty:

- *"A! fredome is a noble thing! / Fredome mayss man to haiff liking."*
  [Ah! Freedom is a noble thing! Freedom makes a man able to live as he pleases (and be content).]{.mean} [— John Barbour, *The Bruce*, Book I]{.src}

### 3.10.5 The Mystics

The 14th century produced a group of English religious writers called **mystics**, who described the soul's direct, personal experience of God.

- **Richard Rolle** (c. 1300–1349), the **Hermit of Hampole** in Yorkshire, wrote religious lyrics and prose works such as ***The Form of Living*** and ***Ego Dormio***.
- ***The Cloud of Unknowing*** (late 14th century), by an unknown author, teaches that God cannot be known by thought, but only reached through love, across a "cloud of unknowing".
- **Walter Hilton** (d. 1396) wrote ***The Scale (Ladder) of Perfection***.
- **Julian of Norwich** (1342 – c. 1416), an **anchoress** (a woman living alone, enclosed in a cell attached to a church) at Norwich, wrote ***Revelations of Divine Love*** (also called *Showings*), based on visions she received during a serious illness in **1373**. It is believed to be **the earliest surviving book in English written by a woman**. Her most famous words were later quoted by **T. S. Eliot** in "Little Gidding" (*Four Quartets*):

::: quotes
- *"All shall be well, and all shall be well, and all manner of thing shall be well."*
  [— Julian of Norwich, *Revelations of Divine Love*]{.src}
:::

- **Margery Kempe** (c. 1373 – after 1438), a merchant's wife from **King's Lynn**, Norfolk, went on pilgrimages to Jerusalem, Rome and Spain, and **dictated** her life story to scribes in the 1430s. ***The Book of Margery Kempe*** is regarded as **the first autobiography in the English language**. In it she describes visiting Julian of Norwich. Its only manuscript was rediscovered in **1934**.

### 3.10.6 Other Prose Writers

- **John of Trevisa** (c. 1342–1402) translated **Ranulf Higden's** Latin world history, the ***Polychronicon***, into English (**1387**), adding a famous note that English had replaced French in schools.
- **Thomas Usk** (d. 1388) wrote ***The Testament of Love***, an allegorical prose work in imitation of Boethius, written while he was in prison (he was later executed, in 1388).

## 3.11 The Fifteenth Century (1400 – 1485/1500)

The fifteenth century is often called **the Barren Age**, **the Dark Age** or **the Age of Transition** of English literature, because after the death of Chaucer (1400) no poet of real greatness appeared in England for nearly two hundred years — until Edmund Spenser. The reasons usually given are:

1. **The Wars of the Roses** (1455–1485), the civil war between the **House of Lancaster** (the red rose) and the **House of York** (the white rose), and the long last stage of the **Hundred Years' War**, filled the country with disorder.
2. **The language was changing.** The final *-e* of many words, which was pronounced in Chaucer's time, stopped being pronounced. Later poets therefore could not hear the music of Chaucer's lines, and their own verse became clumsy.
3. **Imitation instead of originality.** Most poets simply copied Chaucer's forms and allegories.

But the age was not entirely barren. **Prose** grew strong (Malory, Caxton, the Paston Letters), the great **mystery and morality plays** flourished, the **popular ballads** were sung, **printing** arrived, and in **Scotland** a brilliant group of poets appeared.

### 3.11.1 The English Chaucerians

The English poets who followed and imitated Chaucer are called the **English Chaucerians**.

- **Thomas Hoccleve** (or **Occleve**, c. 1368–1426), a clerk in the government's Privy Seal office, knew Chaucer personally and loved him as a master. His chief work, ***The Regement of Princes*** (1411–12), is a book of advice for **Prince Henry** (the future Henry V). It contains a famous **portrait of Chaucer** painted in the margin of the manuscript, and calls Chaucer *"the firste fyndere of our fair langage"*. His ***La Male Regle*** is an amusing confession of his own wild youth in London.
- **John Lydgate** (c. 1370–1451), a monk of **Bury St Edmunds**, was the most prolific poet of the age, writing about 145,000 lines. His chief works are ***The Troy Book*** (1412–20, written for Prince Henry), ***The Siege of Thebes*** (in which Lydgate imagines himself joining Chaucer's pilgrims and telling a new Canterbury tale), ***The Fall of Princes*** (stories of the falls of great men, based on Boccaccio) and ***The Temple of Glas***.
- **Stephen Hawes** (c. 1474 – c. 1523) wrote ***The Pastime of Pleasure*** (1509), a long allegory of a knight's education.

### 3.11.2 The Scottish Chaucerians (the Makars)

In Scotland, the 15th and early 16th centuries were a golden age of poetry. The Scottish poets, who called themselves **makars** ("makers", that is, poets), admired Chaucer but wrote with much more freshness and power than the English Chaucerians.

- **King James I of Scotland** (1394–1437) was captured by the English as a boy and held prisoner in England for **eighteen years**. In captivity he saw from his prison window a lovely lady walking in the garden — **Lady Joan Beaufort**, whom he later married. He described this in ***The Kingis Quair*** (*The King's Book*, c. 1424). Because this royal poet used Chaucer's seven-line stanza, it came to be called **"rhyme royal"**.
- **Robert Henryson** (c. 1425 – c. 1500), a schoolmaster at Dunfermline, wrote ***The Testament of Cresseid***, a powerful **sequel to Chaucer's *Troilus and Criseyde***. Deserted by Diomede, Cresseid curses Venus and Cupid, and the gods punish her with **leprosy**. As she begs by the roadside with other lepers, **Troilus** rides by; he does not recognise her, but something in her face reminds him of his lost love, and he throws her a purse of gold. When she learns who he was, she repents, sends him a ring and dies. Henryson also wrote ***The Morall Fabillis of Esope the Phrygian*** (thirteen animal fables), ***Orpheus and Eurydice*** and ***Robene and Makyne***, an early **pastoral** dialogue.
- **William Dunbar** (c. 1460 – c. 1520), court poet of **King James IV**, was the most brilliant of the makars. His ***The Thrissil and the Rois*** (*The Thistle and the Rose*, 1503) celebrates the marriage of James IV of Scotland (the thistle) to **Margaret Tudor**, daughter of Henry VII of England (the rose) — the marriage that a century later united the crowns of Scotland and England under James VI and I (1603). His ***Lament for the Makaris*** mourns the dead poets, including Chaucer, Gower, Lydgate and Henryson, with the Latin refrain ***"Timor mortis conturbat me"*** (The fear of death troubles me). He also wrote ***The Golden Targe***, the comic ***Tretis of the Tua Mariit Wemen and the Wedo***, and ***The Flyting of Dunbar and Kennedie*** (a "flyting" is a contest of poetic insults).
- **Gavin Douglas** (c. 1474–1522), Bishop of Dunkeld, completed his ***Eneados*** in **1513** — a translation of **Virgil's *Aeneid*** into Scots, the **first complete translation of a major classical poem into any form of English**. His original prologues to each book include fine descriptions of the Scottish seasons. He also wrote ***The Palice of Honour***.

(**Sir David Lindsay**, the last of the makars, is described in Chapter 04, under the Preparation for the Renaissance.)

### 3.11.3 Fifteenth-Century Prose

- **Sir Thomas Malory**, ***Le Morte d'Arthur*** (1469–70) — the greatest prose work of the century (see 3.8).
- **The Paston Letters** (1422–1509) — about a thousand letters written by and to members of the **Paston family**, gentry of **Norfolk**. They give a vivid, real picture of family life, business, marriage, lawsuits and violence during the Wars of the Roses.
- **Sir John Fortescue** (c. 1394 – c. 1479), a judge, wrote ***The Governance of England***, one of the first political treatises in English, comparing the limited monarchy of England with the absolute monarchy of France.
- **Reginald Pecock** (c. 1395 – c. 1461), Bishop of Chichester, wrote ***The Repressor of Over Much Blaming of the Clergy*** (c. 1455) in English against the Lollards. Ironically, he was himself condemned for heresy because he said that reason could judge matters of faith.

### 3.11.4 William Caxton and the Coming of Printing

**William Caxton** (c. 1422–1491) was a successful English cloth merchant living in **Bruges** (in Flanders, modern Belgium). He became interested in translating French books, and learned the new art of **printing** — invented in Germany by **Johannes Gutenberg** in about 1440–1450 — at **Cologne**. He then set up a press at Bruges.

- ***The Recuyell of the Historyes of Troye*** (c. **1473–74**, printed at **Bruges**) — Caxton's own translation from the French of Raoul Lefèvre; it is **the first book ever printed in the English language**.
- ***The Game and Playe of the Chesse*** (1474, Bruges) — the second book printed in English.
- In **1476** Caxton returned to England and set up the **first printing press in England** near **Westminster Abbey**, at the sign of the Red Pale.
- ***The Dictes or Sayengis of the Philosophres*** (**1477**) — **the first dated book printed in England**.
- He printed about **100 books**, including Chaucer's ***Canterbury Tales*** (c. 1476–77 and 1483), Gower's ***Confessio Amantis*** (1483), Malory's ***Le Morte d'Arthur*** (1485) and ***Aesop's Fables*** (1484). About twenty of his books were his own translations.

Caxton is called **"the first English printer"** or **"the Father of English Printing"**. Because he printed in the **London English** of the court and government, printing helped to **fix this dialect as Standard English** and to make spelling more uniform. Books became cheaper and more plentiful, which prepared the way for the Renaissance and the Reformation. After his death his assistant **Wynkyn de Worde** carried on the business and moved it to **Fleet Street**, which became the centre of the English printing and newspaper trade.

In the prologue to his ***Eneydos*** (1490), Caxton tells a famous story to show how much English varied from place to place. Some merchants sailing down the Thames landed in Kent and asked a woman for **"egges"**. She replied that she could not speak French. Another of them asked for **"eyren"**, and then she understood — both words meant "eggs". *"Lo,"* says Caxton, *"what should a man in these days now write, egges or eyren?"*

## 3.12 Medieval Drama

English drama, like the drama of ancient Greece, was born from **religion**. In the Middle Ages it grew out of the services of the Church, moved outside into the streets, and passed through three main stages: the **mystery and miracle plays**, the **morality plays** and the **interludes**.

### 3.12.1 Origins: Liturgical Drama

In the 10th century, small dramatic scenes in **Latin** were added to the church services at Easter and Christmas. The earliest is the Easter **"Quem quaeritis?"** ("Whom do you seek?") — the question of the angels to the women who came to Christ's empty tomb, sung as a little dialogue by priests. Gradually more scenes were added, the plays grew longer and more popular, and they moved from the altar to the church porch and then into the **market-place**. They began to be performed in **English**, by ordinary townspeople instead of priests.

### 3.12.2 Mystery and Miracle Plays

- **Mystery plays** dramatized stories from the **Bible**, from the **Creation** and the **Fall of Lucifer** to the **Last Judgement**. The name comes from the Latin *ministerium* ("craft" or "trade"), because each play was produced by a **trade guild**, the "mystery" or craft-association of the town. The guilds often chose suitable stories: in York, the **shipwrights** performed the *Building of the Ark* and the **bakers** the *Last Supper*.
- **Miracle plays** dramatized the lives and miracles of the **saints**, especially the Virgin Mary and St Nicholas. In England the two words are often used for the same kind of play.

The plays were performed in **cycles** (series) on movable stages called **pageant wagons**, which rolled from one street-corner to the next, especially during the festival of **Corpus Christi** in early summer. Four great English cycles survive:

| Cycle | Number of plays |
|---|---|
| **York** | 48 |
| **Chester** | 24 |
| **Wakefield** (also called **Towneley**) | 32 |
| **N-Town** (once wrongly called the *Ludus Coventriae* or Coventry cycle) | 42 |

The plays mixed holy subjects with **comedy and realism**. **Noah's wife** is a shrewish scold who refuses to enter the Ark until she is dragged in. **King Herod** is a ranting, raging tyrant — from which comes Shakespeare's phrase in *Hamlet*, **"it out-herods Herod"**, meaning to overact wildly.

The most famous single play is the ***Second Shepherds' Play*** (*Secunda Pastorum*) of the Wakefield cycle, by an unknown dramatist of genius called the **Wakefield Master**. Three poor shepherds complain about the cold and their masters. A rogue called **Mak** steals one of their sheep and hides it in a **cradle**, pretending with his wife **Gill** that it is their newborn baby. The shepherds discover the trick and toss Mak in a blanket as punishment. Then an angel announces the birth of Christ, and the shepherds go to Bethlehem to worship the real holy baby lying in a manger. The comic stolen "lamb" in the cradle cleverly prepares for the true **Lamb of God**. It is admired as the finest comic play of the medieval English stage.

### 3.12.3 Morality Plays

In the 15th century a new kind of play appeared: the **morality play**. Its characters are **personified abstractions** — Mankind, Good Deeds, Knowledge, Death, the Seven Deadly Sins, Mercy, Mischief — and its purpose is to teach a **moral lesson** about how to live and how to save one's soul.

- ***The Castle of Perseverance*** (c. 1405–1425) — the **earliest complete morality play** in English. The soul of Mankind is besieged in a castle by the Vices.
- ***Mankind*** (c. 1470) — a lively, comic play in which the farmer Mankind is led astray by the rascals **Mischief**, **New-Guise**, **Nowadays** and **Nought**.
- ***Wisdom*** (c. 1460–70) — also called *Mind, Will and Understanding*.
- ***Everyman*** (c. 1495–1510) — **the most famous morality play**, probably translated from the Dutch play *Elckerlijc*.

*Summary of* ***Everyman****:* **God** sees that people have forgotten Him and sends **Death** to summon **Everyman** to give an account of his life. Terrified, Everyman asks his friends to go with him on this journey. **Fellowship** (friendship), **Kindred** and **Cousin** (family) all promise help but refuse to go. **Goods** (wealth) also refuses and mocks him: riches cannot go beyond the grave. **Good Deeds** would go, but she is too weak to stand, because Everyman has done so few good deeds. Her sister **Knowledge** leads him to **Confession**, and after his penance Good Deeds becomes strong. **Beauty, Strength, Discretion** and **Five Wits** set out with him, but they too desert him at the edge of the grave. Only **Good Deeds** goes down into the grave with Everyman, and an angel receives his soul. The moral: at death, only our good deeds can go with us.

::: quotes
- *"Everyman, I will go with thee, and be thy guide, / In thy most need to go by thy side."*
  [— Knowledge, *Everyman* (later the motto of the famous Everyman's Library series of books)]{.src}
- *"Lo, yonder I see Everyman walking. / Full little he thinketh on my coming."*
  [— Death, *Everyman*]{.src}
:::

The comic tempter in the morality plays, called **the Vice**, is the ancestor of the clown and the stage villain of Elizabethan drama. The **Good and Evil Angels** in Marlowe's ***Doctor Faustus*** come directly from the morality tradition.

### 3.12.4 Interludes

At the end of the 15th and the beginning of the 16th century appeared the **interlude** — a short play, often comic, performed between the courses of a banquet or between other entertainments, in the halls of the rich. Interludes deal with ordinary people more than with allegory, and form a bridge from medieval drama to the regular comedy of the Renaissance. Henry Medwall's ***Fulgens and Lucrece*** (c. 1497) is the **earliest surviving secular (non-religious) play in English**. The most famous writer of interludes, **John Heywood**, is studied in Chapter 04.

## 3.13 The Popular Ballads

A **ballad** is a short **story in song**, composed by unknown people and passed on by word of mouth. The great age of the English and Scottish popular ballads was the 14th and 15th centuries, though most were written down much later.

**Features:** a simple, dramatic story, often tragic, told without the author's comments; much **dialogue**; **refrains** and **repetition**; and usually the **ballad stanza** — four lines with four and three stresses in turn, rhyming **abcb**.

**Main kinds and examples:**

- **The Robin Hood ballads** — about the generous outlaw of Sherwood Forest who robs the rich to help the poor. ***A Gest of Robyn Hode*** (printed c. 1500) is the longest. The first literary mention of Robin Hood is in *Piers Plowman* (see 3.6).
- **The Border ballads** — about fighting on the border between England and Scotland: ***Chevy Chase*** and ***The Battle of Otterburn*** (about the fight of the **Percy** and the **Douglas** in **1388**). Sir Philip Sidney later wrote in *The Defence of Poesy*: *"I never heard the old song of Percy and Douglas that I found not my heart moved more than with a trumpet."*
- **Tragic and supernatural ballads** — ***Sir Patrick Spens*** (a Scottish captain drowned at sea on the king's orders), ***The Twa Corbies*** (two ravens plan to feed on a dead knight), ***Lord Randal*** (a young man poisoned by his sweetheart), ***Barbara Allan*** and ***The Wife of Usher's Well***.

These old ballads were collected and published later by Bishop **Thomas Percy** in ***Reliques of Ancient English Poetry*** (**1765**), by **Sir Walter Scott** in ***Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*** (1802–03) and by the American scholar **F. J. Child** in ***The English and Scottish Popular Ballads*** (305 ballads, 1882–98). Percy's *Reliques* greatly influenced the **Romantic poets**, and Wordsworth and Coleridge called their famous 1798 collection ***Lyrical Ballads***.
